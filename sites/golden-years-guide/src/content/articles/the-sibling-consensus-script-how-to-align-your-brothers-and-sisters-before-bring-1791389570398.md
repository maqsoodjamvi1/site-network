Bringing up assisted living with your aging parents is stressful enough. But for most families, the hardest part isn't actually talking to Mom or Dad—it’s getting your brothers and sisters on the same page first. 

If your siblings are fractured by old sibling rivalries, living in different time zones, or simply in deep denial about how fast your parents are declining, a family meeting can quickly devolve into a shouting match. When that happens, your parents end up caught in the middle, and nothing gets solved.

Over the years, I’ve learned that the secret to a smooth family meeting is treating it like a professional project briefing. You need a script, a strategy, and a unified front before anyone says the words "senior living" to your parents.

## Step 1: Divide and Conquer with Individual Phone Calls

Never bring up assisted living for the first time in a full group text or a crowded conference call. That sets off defensive triggers. Instead, call each sibling individually at least a week before you want to meet as a group.

Your goal on these individual calls is to listen, not to pitch your solution. Ask open-ended questions to figure out where their head is at: *"Have you noticed Dad struggling with his medication lately?"* or *"How did you feel about how Mom looked at Thanksgiving?"*

When you talk to them one-on-one, you also discover their specific fears. Your sister in Denver might be terrified of the financial cost because she's paying for her own kids' college. Your brother down the street might feel guilty because he thinks moving Mom means he failed as a caregiver. Once you know their specific resistance point, you can address it privately before the group gets together.

## Step 2: Use the "Facts, Not Feelings" Framework

When you finally get everyone on a video call or sitting around the kitchen table, emotions will run high. To keep things from getting personal, frame the conversation around objective reality rather than emotional impressions. 

Brothers and sisters love to argue about opinions ("Mom is fine!" vs. "Mom is a wreck!"). You can sidestep this by bringing concrete examples. 

Write down a specific list of red flags from the past thirty days. Instead of saying, "Mom's house is unsafe," say: *"Mom had two unsteadiness falls in the hallway this month, the pharmacist confirmed she missed four days of blood pressure pills last week, and her pantry only had expired canned goods when I checked it yesterday."* 

It is much harder for a sibling in denial to argue with a stack of specific, documented incidents than it is for them to dismiss a general complaint about aging.

## Step 3: Run the Script

When you pivot the conversation toward solutions, use exact phrasing that removes blame and focuses strictly on safety and relief. You want to make sure no one feels blindsided or accused of not doing enough.

Here is the exact script framework I recommend using with your siblings to lock in a consensus:

* *"We all agree that keeping Mom in her home as long as safely possible was our shared goal. But based on the falls this month and the medication mix-ups, the current setup is putting her safety at serious risk."*
* *"None of us has the time or medical training to be there 24/7 to prevent another fall. Continuing to stretch ourselves thin isn't working for us, and it's not fair to her."*
* *"Instead of waiting for a midnight trip to the emergency room after a bad fall, let's look at our options proactively. I want us to agree today that we will tour two local assisted living communities together over the next two weeks. We don't have to sign anything, and we don't have to make a final decision today—we just need to gather the facts as a team."*

## Step 4: Divide the Homework Fairly

A sibling consensus falls apart immediately if one person feels stuck doing all the heavy lifting. Before you end your alignment meeting, assign specific tasks so everyone has skin in the game. 

* **The Financial Researcher:** The sibling with the head for numbers gathers Mom's pension, Social Security, and insurance details to figure out the exact monthly budget.
* **The Tour Booker:** The sibling with the most flexible schedule calls local facilities and schedules two or three private tours for the family.
* **The Medical Recorder:** The sibling closest to Mom requests her most recent doctor's notes and medication lists so you know what level of care she actually requires.
* **The Document Hunter:** The sibling who keeps track of paperwork locates the durable power of attorney and healthcare proxy forms.

By giving everyone a concrete job, you shift the dynamic from a venting session into an action plan. Once your siblings feel heard, understand the hard data, and have a clear role to play, getting everyone to lock arms and support a move becomes entirely possible. 

Taking these preliminary steps now lays the groundwork for the bigger conversation ahead, helping you transition smoothly from family debate to practical execution.
