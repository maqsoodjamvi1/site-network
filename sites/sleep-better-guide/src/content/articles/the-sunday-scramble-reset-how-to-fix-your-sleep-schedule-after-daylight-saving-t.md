Twice a year, I brace myself for the exact same disruption. We lose an hour of sleep in the spring, or we gain a confusing chunk of dark in the afternoon in the fall, and suddenly my internal clock is entirely out of sync. For days afterward, I find myself staring at the ceiling at midnight, dragging myself through morning meetings, and reaching for a third cup of coffee just to function. 

If you are reading this because you are dreading the upcoming time change—or you are currently typing "why am I so tired" into a search bar days after it already happened—you are not imagining things. Shifting the clock by a single hour creates a mini-jetlag that ripples through your digestion, mood, and focus. 

Waiting for your body to naturally adjust on its own usually takes longer than you think. Instead of suffering through a sluggish week, I have found that a deliberate, structured reset over the weekend makes all the difference. Here is the exact game plan I use to get my sleep schedule back on track before Monday morning rolls around.

## Start Shifting the Target on Friday Night

The biggest mistake I used to make was trying to absorb the time change all at once on Sunday night. By waiting until the weekend is nearly over to adjust my bedtime, I practically guaranteed myself a restless night and a miserable Monday. 

Instead, I treat the weekend as a mini runway. If we are losing an hour, I start moving my bedtime fifteen minutes earlier on Friday night, and another fifteen minutes earlier on Saturday night. If we are gaining an hour, I do the opposite, staying up just fifteen minutes later each night. 

This micro-shifting approach tricks your circadian rhythm. Dropping fifteen or thirty minutes at a time is small enough that your brain does not trigger the usual resistance or racing thoughts that happen when you try to force yourself to sleep an hour early.

## Control Your Morning Light Exposure on Sunday

When your internal clock is scrambled, light is your most powerful remote control. Your brain relies on early morning sunlight to shut down melatonin production and set a timer for the rest of the day. 

On the Sunday right after the time change, I force myself outside within thirty minutes of waking up, regardless of how tired I feel or what the weather looks like. Even on an overcast morning, natural outdoor light is exponentially brighter than indoor lighting, and it sends an immediate signal to your brain that the day has begun. 

I make it as frictionless as possible: I put my walking shoes right by the front door on Saturday night, and I drink my first glass of water on the porch or take a brisk ten-minute walk around the block. That early hit of photons makes it much easier to naturally wind down when bedtime rolls around Sunday night.

## Reset Your Evening Wind-Down Buffer

When my schedule is off, my brain uses the quiet of the night to process all the daytime frustration of feeling exhausted. To prevent that late-night loop, I aggressively guard my evening buffer zone during transition weekends.

My routine is straightforward, but it requires discipline:
* **The hard digital cutoff:** All screens go away forty-five minutes before my targeted sleep time. No scrolling, no checking work email, and no television.
* **Temperature drop:** I turn down the thermostat a few degrees. A cooler room mimics the body's natural temperature drop that happens right before deep sleep.
* **Low-stakes distraction:** I keep a physical book or a crossword puzzle on my nightstand. If I cannot fall asleep within twenty minutes, I get out of bed and read under a dim light until my eyelids feel heavy, rather than lying there tossing and turning.

## Adjust Your Fuel and Caffeine Cutoffs

When I am operating on less sleep, my first instinct is to lean heavily on caffeine to survive the afternoon slump. Unfortunately, doing this during a time-change transition is a trap. 

During the weekend reset, I draw a hard line: no caffeine past noon. Because my sleep architecture is already fragile, a mid-afternoon iced coffee will quietly sabotage my ability to reach deep sleep that night, keeping me stuck in the exhaustion cycle for days longer than necessary. 

I also watch out for heavy meals and late-night snacks. Digestion raises your core body temperature and heart rate, both of which work against the physical relaxation needed to anchor a new sleep schedule. 

Getting your sleep schedule back on track after a time change does not require a complete lifestyle overhaul, but it does require a bit of intention over the weekend. By taking small, proactive steps with your lighting, bedtime targets, and daily habits, you can reclaim your rest before the workweek even begins.
