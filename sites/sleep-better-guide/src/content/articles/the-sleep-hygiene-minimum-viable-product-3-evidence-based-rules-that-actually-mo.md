Like a lot of people who struggle to fall asleep, I used to treat bedtime like an extreme sport. My evening routine looked like a wellness influencer’s fever dream: blue-light-blocking glasses at 7:00 PM, a complicated 10-step skincare ritual, a customized herbal tea blend, magnesium supplements, and a white noise machine blasting the sound of a Costa Rican rainstorm. 

If I missed even one step, anxiety would spike. I’d lie awake calculating how many hours of sleep I had left, which naturally guaranteed I wouldn't get any. 

Then I had a conversation with a behavioral sleep therapist who gave me some tough love. She told me that if my sleep required a two-hour production to succeed, my sleep system was fundamentally broken. Healthy sleep shouldn't be that fragile. 

That conversation led me to adopt what I call the Sleep Hygiene Minimum Viable Product (MVP). In software development, an MVP is the simplest version of a product that still actually solves the user's core problem. I applied that same ruthless minimalism to my nights. Instead of twenty different wellness hacks, I boiled my routine down to the three evidence-based rules that actually move the needle. 

If you are tired of spending money on supplements and apps that don't work, here is the bare minimum you actually need to do to fix your sleep.

## Rule 1: The Non-Negotiable Wake-Up Time

Most people think a good night's sleep starts when they get into bed. That is backward. Good sleep actually starts the moment you wake up.

Your body runs on a roughly 24-hour internal clock called the circadian rhythm, and it relies on external cues to stay synchronized. The single most powerful cue you can give it is consistent morning light exposure paired with a fixed wake-up time. 

When you wake up at the exact same time every single day—yes, even on Saturdays—you anchor your circadian rhythm. Within a few days, your brain automatically begins releasing alertness hormones in the morning and sleep-inducing melatonin roughly 16 hours later.

Here is what this looks like in practice:
* **Pick a wake time** that you can realistically maintain seven days a week, even after a bad night of sleep. 
* **Get bright light within 30 minutes** of opening your eyes. Step outside onto your porch for five minutes, or stand right next to a bright window while you drink your coffee. 
* **Resist the snooze button.** Fragmented morning grogginess (known as sleep inertia) only gets worse if you drift back into a broken, unrefreshing 9-minute sleep cycle.

If you sleep in until 10:00 AM on Sunday, you shouldn't be surprised when you can't fall asleep until midnight on Sunday night. You essentially gave yourself a mini-jetlag. 

## Rule 2: The 20-Minute Reset (Stop Trying So Hard)

If Rule 1 is about your mornings, Rule 2 is about what to do when your brain refuses to cooperate at night. 

We’ve all been there: It is 2:00 AM, you are staring at the ceiling, and your brain is replaying an awkward thing you said at a staff meeting in 2018. Every minute you lie there awake, your brain builds a subconscious association between your bed and frustration. 

The clinical term for what you should do instead is stimulus control, but I call it the 20-Minute Reset. 

If you have been lying awake in bed for what feels like 20 minutes (don't check the clock; just estimate) and you feel wired or annoyed, get out of bed. Go to a dimly lit room, sit on a comfortable chair, and do something low-stimulation. Read a physical book, fold some laundry, or listen to a boring podcast at a low volume. 

Do not check your phone, do not answer emails, and do not turn on bright overhead lights. Stay out of bed until you feel genuinely heavy-eyed, then head back to sleep. 

The first few times you do this, it feels deeply counterintuitive. You will worry that getting out of bed will cost you precious sleep time. But lying in bed wide awake costs you something worse: peace of mind. By getting up, you break the panic loop and teach your brain that the bed is exclusively for sleeping, not for stressing.

## Rule 3: The Curfew on Cognitive Heavy Lifting

We talk a lot about blue light from screens, but the physical light of your phone is rarely the real reason scrolling keeps you awake. The real culprit is cognitive activation. 

Looking at a spreadsheet, reading a stressful news article, or doom-scrolling through social media floods your brain with problem-solving energy. You cannot expect your nervous system to slam on the brakes and transition into deep rest if you were actively processing high-stakes information two minutes prior.

You need a hard stop for cognitive heavy lifting about an hour before your target bedtime. 

This doesn't mean you have to sit in total darkness staring at a wall. It just means you shift the *type* of content you consume. If you must use a screen, switch it to entertainment that doesn't demand problem-solving—watch a sitcom you've seen a dozen times, read fiction, or listen to music. Give your prefrontal cortex permission to clock out for the day. 

When you strip away the expensive gadgets, the complicated tracking rings, and the exhaustive nighttime checklists, sleep is actually a biological default state. Your body *wants* to sleep; it just needs you to stop getting in its own way. 

As you start experimenting with keeping a fixed wake time, managing nighttime wakefulness, and giving your brain a proper wind-down buffer, pay attention to how your body responds over the course of a week.
