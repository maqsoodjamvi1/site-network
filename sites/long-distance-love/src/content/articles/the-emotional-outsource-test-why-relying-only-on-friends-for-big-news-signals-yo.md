When my long-distance partner didn't call me the night I closed on my first apartment, I didn't cry. Instead, I opened group chat and typed: "Keys in hand!" to three college friends. 

I spent the next two hours voice-noting my friends about the squeaky front door and the tiny kitchen, while my phone sat face down on the counter. My partner—the person who supposedly shared my life from three time zones away—found out about the biggest milestone of my year four hours later via a text message that read: "Went well, tired now."

That was the exact moment I realized my long-distance relationship was dead. We just hadn't signed the paperwork yet.

## The Shift From Partner to Project

In a healthy local relationship, your partner is usually the first person you want to talk to when something good, bad, or weird happens. In a dying long-distance relationship, that impulse doesn't just fade—it redirects. You start saving your best stories, your deepest frustrations, and your funniest observations for your group chats, your coworkers, or your local friends. 

This is what I call the Emotional Outsource Test. 

Ask yourself this: Who did you tell first when you got the promotion? Who did you call when your car broke down on the highway? Who do you vent to when your boss drives you crazy? If your immediate instinct is to look outside your relationship for empathy, validation, or excitement, you are no longer treating your partner as a partner. You are treating them as a chore you have to check in with later.

## Why We Hoard Our Good News

When you are thousands of miles apart, sharing your life requires logistical effort. You have to schedule a FaceTime call, make sure your Wi-Fi is cooperating, and sometimes manufacture enthusiasm around a seven-second video delay. 

If your relationship is already on life support, calling your partner feels like unpaid labor. Why tell them about the apartment keys when they are just going to ask logistical questions about when you're visiting next, or respond with a flat "That's great, babe" because they're half-watching YouTube? 

Instead of dealing with that disappointment, you outsource the emotional reward to people who can give you an immediate, high-energy reaction. You take the easy hit of dopamine from friends who are physically close to you, while starving your romantic relationship of the very things that bond people together: shared joy and immediate vulnerability.

## The Danger Zone: Complaining to Outsiders

It goes both ways. Not only do we stop sharing our wins with our long-distance partners, but we also stop sharing our struggles with them—and start sharing them with everyone else.

If you find yourself venting about your partner's lack of effort to your roommate, your siblings, or your coworkers, but you put on a brave, low-conflict face when you actually talk to your partner, you have crossed a major line. 

* You are rewriting the narrative of your relationship for an audience outside of it.
* You are building an emotional safety net with other people so the breakup won't hurt as much when it happens.
* You are avoiding direct, difficult conversations with your partner because, deep down, you've already checked out and don't care enough to fix it.

When you stop fighting with your partner and start complaining about them to others, you aren't keeping the peace. You are quietly packing your bags emotionally.

## How to Run the Test Today

If you suspect your long-distance relationship is running on fumes, stop guessing and look at your actual behavior over the last fourteen days. Open your phone right now and scroll through your recent text threads. 

* Check who you texted first when you had a strong emotion this week—positive or negative.
* Count how many times you summarized your day to your partner in a brief, bullet-point text just to get it over with, versus how many deep conversations you had with friends.
* Notice if you feel a sense of relief when your partner's flight gets delayed or when they have a busy work night that cancels your scheduled call.

If your phone records show that your friends are getting the real version of you while your partner is getting the administrative updates, you have to ask yourself why you are holding on. Distance is tough, but emotional detachment is a choice. 

Recognizing that you have already mentally broken up with someone is painful, but it is also the first step toward reclaiming your time and energy.
