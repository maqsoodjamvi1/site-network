When my partner and I were six months into a grueling cross-country long-distance stretch, we decided we needed a shared intellectual hobby. Reading the same book at the same time felt sophisticated, intentional, and romantic. We envisioned cozy Sunday mornings on FaceTime, sipping coffee and passionately debating character arcs like a two-person lit class.

Instead, we fell straight into what I call the Book Club Trap. 

One of us was a fast reader who plowed through three chapters on a Tuesday lunch break, while the other was stuck in a draining work week and barely managed two pages before falling asleep. By the time our scheduled Sunday chat rolled around, the power dynamic was ruined. One person was eager to dissect the ending, and the other was frantically skimming Wikipedia summaries just to avoid feeling guilty. 

If you and your long-distance partner are considering a shared reading routine to feel closer, it is worth looking at why this specific habit frequently backfires—and what you should do instead.

## The Built-In Pressure of Pace

The fundamental flaw of the couple's book club is that reading speed is rarely matched. In a standard book club, members have a month to read a text, and if someone falls behind, they simply skip that month's meeting. 

In a long-distance relationship, however, every shared activity carries emotional weight. If your partner finishes the book in three days and you take three weeks, it stops being a fun bonding exercise and starts feeling like a homework assignment with a disappointing grade. 

Worse, you either end up rushing through a novel you aren't enjoying just to keep up, or you spend your limited FaceTime hours discussing why you both haven't read enough. That guilt creeps into the rest of the relationship, replacing the comfort of the call with low-level performance anxiety.

## Spoilers and Conversation Fatigue

Another trap is the conversational bottleneck. When you spend all week apart, your communication naturally revolves around daily updates, logistics, and trying to recreate micro-moments of domestic life. 

If you force a complex, 400-page narrative into the mix, you risk turning your intimate check-ins into book reports. You might find yourselves arguing over a fictional character's toxic behavior while ignoring your own emotional disconnect. Furthermore, if one person accidentally slips up and drops a major plot spoiler over text on a Wednesday afternoon, the anticipated Sunday discussion instantly loses its excitement. 

## Ditch the Novel and Try Asynchronous Media Instead

If you want a shared cultural experience that actually works across a distance, you need a format that accommodates mismatched schedules and keeps the pressure low. 

Instead of forcing yourselves through the same heavy paperback, try these practical alternatives:

* **The Audio-First Experiment:** Pick a memoir read by the author or a full-cast audiobook. Listen to it independently while doing chores or commuting, but agree to only listen *while* walking the dog or folding laundry. It creates a parallel experience without demanding synchronized reading speeds.
* **The Non-Fiction Chapter Swap:** Instead of a novel, pick a book of essays, short stories, or accessible pop-science. You don't have to read the whole thing sequentially. Pick one essay that catches your eye, read it independently, and spend 15 minutes debating just that specific idea on your next call.
* **The "Micro-Article" Habit:** Ditch books entirely for a week. Send each other one long-form journalism piece, a strange Wikipedia rabbit hole, or an opinion essay every morning. Discuss it over text throughout the day. It provides fresh conversational fodder without the multi-week commitment of a novel.

The goal of a long-distance hobby isn't to prove you can finish a reading list together. It is to generate fresh energy and genuine curiosity between two people who miss sharing physical space. 

If the heavy novel is currently sitting on your nightstand collecting dust while you both feel guilty looking at it, give yourselves permission to close the cover. Figuring out how to stay connected from afar requires dropping the things that feel like chores so you can make room for what actually brings you closer. Consider what kind of low-pressure shared rhythm fits your actual lifestyles right now as you plan your next steps.
