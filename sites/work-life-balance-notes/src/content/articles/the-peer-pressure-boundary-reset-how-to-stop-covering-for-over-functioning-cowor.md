Have you ever looked across the office or your Slack channels and realized you are constantly bailing out the same person? Let’s call my version of this coworker "Dave." Dave is chronically disorganized, stays online until 8:00 PM every night because he spent the afternoon scrolling news sites, and treats deadlines as loose suggestions. Because work still needs to get done, I used to step in. I formatted his messy spreadsheets, took over client emails at 5:00 PM on a Friday, and smoothed things over with our manager so "the team" didn't look bad.

I thought I was being a good team player. What I was actually doing was enabling a broken dynamic and sacrificing my own evenings. 

The hardest part of fixing this isn't dealing with the over-functioning coworker—it is managing the peer pressure from the rest of the office. When you suddenly stop cleaning up someone else’s messes, you risk looking like the bad guy. Here is how I learned to reset my boundaries without torpedoing my reputation or burning out.

## Diagnose the Difference Between True Emergencies and Manufactured Crises

The first trap I fell into was assuming every panicked Slack message from Dave constituted a five-alarm fire. Over-functioning coworkers are masters of manufactured urgency. Because they manage their time poorly, everything feels like an immediate crisis to them, which they then attempt to make an immediate crisis for you.

Before you respond to a late-day request for help, ask yourself three concrete questions:
* Is this impacting a hard company deadline, or is it just Dave’s self-imposed pressure?
* If I do nothing, will the client actually notice, or will Dave just have to explain himself to our boss tomorrow morning?
* Is this task part of my actual job description, or am I doing it because I type faster than he does?

If the answer points to a manufactured crisis, pause. Giving an immediate "Sure, I'll take a look!" trains your coworkers that your time is infinitely elastic. 

## Use the "Capacity Check" Script Instead of a Flat "No"

When you have spent months acting like a human rescue boat, you cannot suddenly slam the door in a coworker's face without looking hostile. You need a transition phase. My favorite tool for this is the "Capacity Check" script. It shifts the burden of proof from your willingness to help to the reality of your current workload.

When Dave drops a last-minute favor in your lap, resist the urge to apologize. Instead, try one of these responses:
* "I am currently heads-down on the Q3 report for Sarah and won't have daylight for anything else today. Have you looped in [Manager's Name] to see if we should push this deadline?"
* "My plate is completely full until Thursday afternoon. If this needs to go out tonight, you'll have to handle it solo."

Notice what this script does. It refuses the work without being defensive, and crucially, it subtly loops back to the manager or standard accountability channels rather than letting the problem disappear into your evening.

## Redefine Teamwork to Your Manager Before It Becomes a Problem

The biggest fear when stepping back from covering for someone is that your boss will think you have stopped caring. To prevent this, you have to frame the boundary reset around quality, not laziness. 

About a month into my own boundary cleanup, I asked for a quick ten-minute check-in with my manager. I didn't complain about Dave; instead, I brought data about my own output. 

I said: "I want to make sure I am focusing my hours on the high-impact projects we agreed on for this quarter. Lately, I've been spending a chunk of my afternoons untangling handoffs from other workflows. I want to make sure I protect my time so my primary deliverables don't slip." 

Most managers are thrilled to hear you care about your primary deliverables. By getting ahead of the narrative, you ensure that when Dave inevitably drops a ball because you didn't catch it, your manager already knows you are focused on your own lane.

## Accept the Temporary Discomfort of Looking "Unhelpful"

Here is the bitter pill: when you stop over-functioning for others, people who benefited from your lack of boundaries will be annoyed. Dave might act cold, sigh loudly when you log off at 5:00 PM, or imply that you aren't a team player. 

Let them be annoyed. 

Peer pressure only works if you care more about immediate, superficial harmony than your long-term mental health. The discomfort of someone being slightly miffed at you for two weeks is vastly preferable to the slow creep of resentment that ruins your evenings and weekends. Usually, after a brief adjustment period, the over-functioning coworker figures out how to manage their own workload—or your manager finally notices where the real bottleneck lies.

Deciding to protect your personal time isn't a lack of commitment to your job; it is a commitment to sustainability.
