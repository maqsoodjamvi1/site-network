Coming back from vacation used to fill me with a very specific kind of dread. I would spend my final Sunday afternoon staring at a suitcase, already mentally spiraling over the mountain of notifications waiting in my inbox. Monday morning arrived, and instead of feeling rested, I’d walk straight into a brutal wall of back-to-back meetings. By Tuesday afternoon, I felt like I hadn't taken time off at all. 

The turning point came a few years ago when I realized that vacation burnout isn't caused by the time away. It’s caused by the chaotic reentry. 

If you want to protect your post-vacation peace, you need a system to block meeting creep before you even log back in. I call this the PTO return-firewall. Here is the exact playbook I use to protect my first three days back at work, including the copy-and-paste scripts that actually work.

## Build the Shield Before You Leave

A solid return-firewall starts two weeks before your trip, not on your first day back. Most people make the mistake of leaving their calendars wide open right up until departure. When colleagues see white space on your calendar for the day after you return, they will drop meetings into it. 

Before you lock your computer for vacation, block your entire first three days back with tentative, all-day placeholders labeled "Focus Time: Post-Trip Catch-Up." Set these to show you as "Busy." 

This doesn't mean you won't attend meetings ever again. It simply buys you time to sort through your inbox and decide which requests actually require your presence, and which ones can be handled via a quick Slack message or skipped entirely.

## Day One: The Triage Script

Your first day back should be entirely zero-meeting. Your only job is to process email, clear urgent blockers, and get your bearings. When people inevitably try to slide a meeting onto your Day One schedule, you need to push back immediately with a polite boundary.

I use this exact script when someone tries to book time on my first day back:

> "Hi [Name], I’m stepping back into a massive backlog of emails today and tomorrow morning, so I’m keeping my calendar clear of calls to focus strictly on triage. Could we push this to Thursday afternoon or Friday instead? If there's a critical decision you need from me right now, drop the details here and I'll review them by end of day."

This script works because it offers an alternative (Thursday or Friday) while setting a firm boundary about your capacity. It also forces the sender to write down their request, which often reveals that the meeting wasn't actually necessary in the first place.

## Day Two: The Delegation and Re-routing Script

By your second day back, the initial wave of urgent fires has usually been put out, but people will start requesting "catch-up chats" to brief you on everything that happened while you were away. If you let every project lead book a 30-minute sync, your entire week will vanish.

Instead of sitting through endless status updates, redirect people to asynchronous updates or delegate to peers who held down the fort. 

Use this script to handle unnecessary catch-up requests:

> "Hi [Name], thanks for reaching out. I'm catching up on project threads today and trying to keep meetings to a minimum. Could you drop a quick bulleted summary of any key decisions or blockers in this thread? If we still need a quick sync after that, I have 10 minutes open on Friday."

Limiting these syncs to a strict 10-minute cap—or eliminating them entirely in favor of a written summary—saves hours of lost productivity. Most colleagues are more than happy to type out a quick update once they realize you aren't going to sit through a lengthy presentation.

## Day Three: The Strategic Reset

By day three, you are finally ready to re-enter the meeting ecosystem, but you should still control the agenda. Do not accept blank meeting invites with titles like "Sync" or "Catch-up." 

If a meeting invite lands on your third day back without an agenda, send this response:

> "Hi [Name], I'd love to chat, but I'm still working through my post-vacation backlog. Could you add a brief agenda or a list of specific discussion points to the calendar invite so I can prep properly before we jump on the call?"

This simple habit filters out about half of all low-value meetings. People hate writing agendas, and if they don't have a good reason for a meeting, they will often cancel it themselves when asked to provide one.

Protecting your reentry period is one of the highest-leverage habits you can build for long-term work-life balance. When you stop treating your calendar like a public park and start treating it like a secured building, your time off actually starts to stick.
