When you bring home a working dog puppy—whether it is an Australian Shepherd, Border Collie, German Shepherd, or Heeler—you are signing up for a very specific kind of energy. These dogs were bred for centuries to control livestock, which means their default setting involves moving fast, making independent decisions, and using their mouths to direct movement. 

If you do not want your ankles treated like stray sheep, you need to teach the right foundational skills early. Working dog puppies do not just need to sit and stay; they need to learn impulse control and how to channel their genetic drive into appropriate outlets. Here are the first skills you should teach to prevent herding and nipping before those behaviors turn into permanent habits.

## The "Leave It" Cue as a Herding Antidote

Herding breeds are visual predators. They are hardwired to notice and chase fast-moving objects, which includes running children, bicycles, flapping pant legs, and bare feet. When a puppy tries to control the movement of these objects by nipping at heels, they are simply following their instincts. 

The "Leave It" cue is your primary tool to short-circuit this sequence. Unlike "Drop It," which teaches a puppy to release something already in their mouth, "Leave It" teaches them not to engage with something moving toward them.

To practice this in a real-world scenario, hold a piece of kibble in a closed fist. Present your fist to your puppy. They will sniff, lick, and paw at it. Say nothing, and remain completely still. The exact second your puppy pulls their nose away from your hand—even out of brief confusion—say "Yes!" and feed them a different, better treat from your other hand. Never let them eat the food directly from the fist they were pawing at. Once they consistently back away from a closed fist, progress to dropping kibble on the floor under your shoe while keeping them on a short leash, rewarding them when they look up at you instead of diving for the food.

## Redirecting Oral Fixation with a Flirt Pole

Working puppies have a high bite-to-exhaustion ratio. They use their teeth to explore, communicate, and manage their environment. Punishing a puppy for nipping usually backfires because they interpret a sharp Yelp or a hand swat as the thrilling movement of erratic prey. 

Instead of fighting the bite, manage it by building a strict boundary around *what* is allowed to be chased and caught. A flirt pole—essentially a giant cat toy with a fleece lure on a rope attached to a long horse-lunge whip—is the single best investment you can make for a herding breed puppy.

Set up a game in your backyard with specific rules:
- The flirt pole only moves when you say so.
- The puppy must sit and make eye contact before you drag the toy across the grass.
- If teeth accidentally touch your skin during play, the game instantly freezes, and you turn your back for ten seconds.

This teaches the puppy that self-control unlocks the chase, and biting human skin makes the fun stop completely. It burns physical energy while teaching the exact impulse control they need when a soccer ball or a running toddler zips past them.

## Teaching a Structured "Place" Command for Over-Arousal

Herding puppies are notoriously bad at self-settling. Because they are bred to work all day on minimal rest, an overtired working puppy often transforms into a manic shark, biting ankles and barking at shadows when they actually just need a nap. 

Instead of waiting for your puppy to crash on the living room rug, teach them a strict "Place" command. Choose an elevated dog cot or a specific heavy mat. 

Lure your puppy onto the mat with a treat, and the second all four paws are on the surface, mark it with "Yes!" and feed them right there on the mat. Do not ask them to sit or down yet; just reward the physical boundary of the mat. Gradually increase the duration they must stay on the mat before getting a reward. 

Use this command when you are cooking dinner, folding laundry, or when guests come over. It gives the puppy a clear, designated job—guarding their spot—which satisfies their working brain while forcing their nervous system to calm down.

## Building Focus Around Moving Triggers

The ultimate test for a working dog puppy is remaining calm while motion happens around them. Once your puppy understands basic obedience in a quiet living room, take them to a tennis court perimeter or a park edge where they can watch movement from a safe distance.

Practice the "Look At Me" game. Every time your puppy notices a runner, a skateboard, or another dog, wait one second to see if they look at it. The moment their ears perk up toward the trigger, say your marker word and shove a high-value treat, like a piece of boiled chicken, right to their nose. 

You are teaching them a conditioned reflex: *When I see something moving fast, I should immediately look back at my human for a payout.* This replaces the genetic urge to chase and grip with a learned habit of checking in with you.

Preventing herding and nipping behaviors is not about suppressing your puppy's natural working instincts; it is about giving those instincts rules and structure from day one. By prioritizing impulse control, teaching clear boundaries for play, and managing over-arousal, you set your puppy up to be a balanced, manageable companion rather than an uncontrolled livestock dog in a suburban home.
