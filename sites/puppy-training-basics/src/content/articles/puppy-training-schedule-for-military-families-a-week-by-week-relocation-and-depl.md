Bringing a new puppy home is a massive undertaking for any household, but when your life revolves around Permanent Change of Station (PCS) moves, field exercises, and sudden deployments, the standard puppy manual just doesn't cut it. As a military spouse who has navigated both a cross-country move and a sudden two-month TDY while raising a high-drive Labrador retriever, I learned that predictability is your best friend. 

Military life throws a lot of chaos your way, but a structured training schedule can keep your puppy grounded even when the rest of the household is packed into cardboard boxes. Here is a practical, week-by-week roadmap tailored for military families who need a resilient dog.

## Weeks 8 to 10: Building Crate Confidence and Isolation Tolerance

Your absolute first priority should be teaching your puppy that being alone is safe. Between long shifts at the unit, morning physical training (PT) formations, and appointments at the base pass-and-ID office, you will not always be able to have eyes on your dog. 

Start by making the crate a high-value zone. Feed every meal inside the crate and leave the door open during the day so they can wander in and out. Practice closing the door for just a few seconds while you walk to the other side of the room, gradually building up to an hour. 

You also need to introduce a rotating cast of characters early. Military life means relying on neighbors, friends in the unit, or boarding kennels. Have different people hand-feed your puppy, touch their paws, and put on heavy boots or uniforms around them so they do not become reactive to standard military gear.

## Weeks 11 to 14: Socialization on the Installation and Basic Impulse Control

Socialization goes far beyond basic puppy classes. You need to expose your dog to the specific sights and sounds of military life before their fear window closes around 16 weeks old. 

Take your puppy to the edge of the base housing area or a nearby park where they can safely watch and hear unusual stimuli. Let them observe people in uniform, children riding bikes, golf carts, and the rumble of large trucks. If your base plays reveille and retreat over the loudspeakers, pair those bugle calls with high-value treats so your puppy learns to ignore the sudden noise rather than panic.

During this phase, lock down core obedience cues: sit, down, stay, and a reliable recall. Keep training sessions short—about five minutes at a time—while waiting for dinner to cook or standing in line at the commissary.

## Weeks 15 to 20: Preparing for the PCS Transition

If orders drop, your training shifts from basic manners to relocation survival. Your puppy needs to be completely comfortable in a travel crate for hours at a time. 

Introduce the hard-shell aviation crate that meets airline or travel regulations. Feed meals inside it and place it in the living room with the door removed. Next, practice putting the puppy in the crate and moving it around. Carry the crate to your vehicle, put them inside, and drive around the block a few times without stopping, gradually increasing the length of the car rides. 

This is also the time to secure all veterinary records in a dedicated binder. Keep a physical copy of your puppy’s rabies certificate and vaccination history handy, as base housing offices and off-base landlords will demand them immediately upon arrival at your new duty station.

## Weeks 21 to 24: Managing Sudden Deployments and Routine Disruptions

When the service member deploys or leaves for a month-long field training exercise, the household routine collapses overnight. Puppies thrive on predictability, so a sudden absence can trigger regression in house-training or leash manners.

Maintain the exact same feeding and walking schedule, even if you are now flying solo parent-style. If you have to hire a neighborhood teenager or a local pet sitter to handle midday potty breaks while you manage solo errands, introduce them to the puppy well before the deployment begins. Show them your exact training hand signals and reward systems so the rules remain consistent. 

Consistency is what keeps a military dog stable when everything else around them is shifting. By front-loading this structured training during their first few months, you create a confident companion who can roll with the punches of military life just as well as you do.
