Adding a second or third dog to your home brings double the joy, but it completely changes the dynamic when it comes to obedience training. If you have ever tried to teach a basic "sit" or "stay" while two pairs of hopeful eyes stare you down, you know the chaos. The biggest hurdle isn't getting them to understand the cue—it is managing the high-value treats without triggering resource guarding or outright dog fights. 

When I brought my second rescue home, my older dog instantly assumed every piece of cheese or boiled chicken coming out of my pouch belonged by default to his seniority. Fixing that required a complete shift in how I structured training sessions. Here is the exact blueprint I used to train each dog individually while keeping peace in the multi-dog living room.

## Separate Before You Meditate on Obedience

You cannot effectively teach focus to a dog whose brain is hijacked by food anxiety. Before any training happens, physical management is your best friend. 

If your dogs currently scramble over each other the second you touch the treat bag, you need to separate them behind baby gates or in separate rooms for short, two-minute sessions. I started by putting my resident dog in his crate with a long-lasting chew toy loaded with peanut butter, giving me a solid five minutes of uninterrupted, zero-pressure time to work with the newcomer in the hallway. 

Separation is not a punishment; it lowers the arousal levels in the room. When dogs aren't worried that someone is going to steal their meal, their cognitive processing improves dramatically. Once your dogs can successfully execute a basic command in separate spaces without whining at the barrier, you are ready to bring them back into the same room using a structured protocol.

## Master the "Go to Mat" Command as a Neutral Zone

When training one dog in the presence of another, you need a way to keep the non-training dog safely occupied and out of the personal space of the working dog. Enter the boundary command, often called "place" or "mat."

Teach this one dog at a time when things are calm. Toss a treat onto a designated dog bed or mat, and the second all four paws step onto it, mark the behavior and deliver another high-value reward *on the mat*. Eventually, add a release word like "break" so they know when they can leave.

Once both dogs reliably understand their respective mats, you can use this setup for active sessions. Put Dog A on Mat A with a steady stream of low-value, boring kibble every few seconds just for staying put. Meanwhile, you stand five feet away and work with Dog B on "sit," "down," or loose-leash walking indoors using high-value treats like freeze-dried liver. Switch roles so both dogs get turns being the active learner and the relaxed watcher on the mat.

## Manage Treat Delivery to Prevent Anticipation Anxiety

Resource guarding often starts because the non-working dog feels cheated or anxious about the discrepancy in rewards. To prevent this, you have to be deliberate about your hands and your timing.

*   **Never hand-feed the working dog right in front of the watching dog's nose.** If Dog B is sitting two inches away while you reward Dog A, you are actively tempting fate and building tension.
*   **Deliver rewards behind the working dog's shoulder line.** When teaching a "stay," step slightly behind or to the side of the dog so your hand doesn't drift toward the waiting audience.
*   **Keep a decoy pouch of boring kibble for the spectator.** Toss a piece of kibble onto the watching dog's mat *just* as you deliver a piece of chicken to the working dog. This teaches both dogs that your hand moving toward the treat pouch predicts good things for everyone, rather than signaling a competitive threat.

Keep your mechanical movements smooth and predictable. Jerky, panicked movements when you reach for a treat will make both dogs tense up.

## Keep Sessions Short and Read the Room

Multi-dog households add a layer of mental fatigue to training that single-dog homes don't experience. The watching dog is expending mental energy trying to stay on their mat and suppress the urge to rush the treat zone. The working dog is trying to concentrate while sensory distractions pace around them.

Limit your targeted sessions to five minutes per dog, once or twice a day. If you notice whale eyes, stiffened bodies, lip-licking, or displacement behaviors like excessive scratching in either dog, call it quits immediately. Training should build confidence and household harmony, not raise the ambient cortisol levels in your living room.

As you build consistency over a couple of weeks, you will notice the frantic scramble for treats replaced by calm, patient turns. Taking the time to establish these boundaries now prevents dangerous conflicts later and ensures every dog in your home feels secure. From here, you can start exploring how to safely integrate them into simultaneous obedience games without losing control of the room.
