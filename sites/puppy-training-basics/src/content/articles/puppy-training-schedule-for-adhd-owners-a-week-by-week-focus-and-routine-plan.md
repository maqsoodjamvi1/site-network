Bringing home a puppy when your brain runs on a different operating system comes with unique hurdles. Traditional dog training guides love to tell you to stick to a rigid, minute-by-minute daily schedule for months on end. If you have ADHD, reading that advice usually triggers an immediate wave of overwhelm and shame. Sticking to a pristine calendar is hard enough for me when it comes to my own meals and laundry; managing a sharp-toothed infant animal on top of that felt entirely impossible at first.

When I raised my current dog, I had to throw out the neurotypical rulebook. I realized that a puppy doesn't need perfection; they need consistency in the *outcomes*, even if the *timing* is a bit chaotic. By breaking training down into small, hyper-focused weekly chunks and leaning into my natural bursts of high energy, we survived the puppy phase without losing our minds. Here is the realistic, ADHD-friendly week-by-week blueprint I wish I had from day one.

## Week 1: Survival, Sleep, and One-Word Commands

Forget teaching fancy tricks during your puppy's first week home. Your only job right now is building trust, establishing a safe physical space, and setting up a house-training rhythm. Because ADHD brains struggle with working memory, keep your vocabulary absurdly small. Pick three words to use for the next seven days: "Potty," "Bed," and your puppy's name. 

Set a loud, repeating interval timer on your phone for every 45 minutes during the day. When it buzzes, take the puppy directly to their spot outside. No distractions, no phone scrolling. When they go, throw a mini-party with high-value treats (tiny bits of boiled chicken work wonders). If you forget to set the timer and an accident happens, take a deep breath, clean it up with an enzymatic spray, and move on. Do not beat yourself up.

## Week 2: Channeling Hyperfocus into Crate Training

During Week 2, novelty starts wearing off and puppy blues often peak. This is a great time to lean into your ADHD superpower: hyperfocus. When you feel a burst of energy, channel it directly into crate games. 

Instead of forcing your puppy into a closed crate and walking away, make the crate the most rewarding place on earth. Toss a handful of kibble inside, let them walk in to eat it, and walk right back out. Repeat this ten times in a row while you listen to a podcast. Then, try closing the door for five seconds, opening it before they cry, and rewarding them. By gamifying the process, you keep your own brain engaged while teaching the puppy that settling in a crate is a safe, low-stress choice.

## Week 3: Leash Walking and Managing Impulse Control

Walking a bouncy puppy while easily distracted is a recipe for tangled legs and frustration. For Week 3, ditch the long, meandering walks. Instead, practice "focus walking" inside your hallway or living room for just three minutes at a time. 

Hold a wooden spoon slathered with plain Greek yogurt or peanut butter down by your left side. As you take three steps, let the puppy lick the spoon. This naturally positions them right where you want them, reinforces eye contact, and burns mental energy without requiring you to monitor a dozen variables at once. Keep a stash of pre-cut treats in every single room of the house so you can reward good choices (like sitting quietly instead of jumping on guests) in the exact moment they happen, bypassing the need to remember where you left the treat pouch.

## Week 4: Setting Up Environmental Shortcuts

By the end of your first month together, the initial chaos should be settling into a predictable hum. Now is the time to build structural safety nets for your ADHD brain. If you are prone to leaving doors open or forgetting to pick up shoes, buy heavy-duty baby gates and designate specific drop-zones for your belongings. 

Training a dog successfully with ADHD isn't about becoming a hyper-organized robot. It is about environmental design. Use heavy rubber mats to cover areas where the puppy likes to dig or chew, keep a leash hanging on a hook right by the front door, and write your daily training goals on a sticky note slapped onto your bathroom mirror where you are guaranteed to look at it every morning.

When you are ready to take your next steps toward mastering a calm, confident routine with your new companion, consider exploring additional structured resources and toolkits designed to support different learning styles.
