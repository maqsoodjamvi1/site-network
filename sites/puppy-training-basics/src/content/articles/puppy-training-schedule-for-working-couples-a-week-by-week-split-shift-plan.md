Raising a puppy when both you and your partner work full-time outside the home is entirely possible, but it requires treating your schedules like a military operation. When my partner and I brought home an eight-week-old Labrador retriever mix, we both worked standard 9-to-5 jobs in different parts of the city. We quickly realized that traditional puppy advice—which often assumes one person is home all day—was useless to us. 

Instead of waiting around for a mythical free weekend to train our dog, we built a split-shift system. By dividing the day into distinct blocks based on our commute times, we kept our sanity, survived the house-training phase, and raised a well-adjusted dog without quitting our jobs or hiring expensive full-time day care. Here is the exact week-by-week blueprint we used to make it work.

## Weeks 8 Through 10: The Shock and Awe Phase

The first two weeks are about survival, bond-building, and establishing the skeleton of your routine. At eight weeks old, a puppy can physically hold their bladder for about two hours during the day, meaning you cannot leave them alone for a standard morning block. 

To bridge the gap, map out your exact working hours the weekend before the puppy arrives. If Partner A leaves the house at 7:00 AM and Partner B works from home or leaves later at 9:00 AM, Partner B takes the morning shift. If both of you must leave early, one of you needs to wake up at 5:30 AM to start the day early so the puppy's first meal and potty break happen well before you walk out the door.

During these first three weeks, your daily split-shift should look like this:
* **Morning Shift (6:00 AM - 8:30 AM):** Partner A feeds, takes the puppy outside for 15 minutes of dedicated potty time, and does a five-minute crate-settling session.
* **Midday Check-In (11:30 AM - 1:00 PM):** This is the pivot point. Either a dog walker comes by, a neighbor lets the puppy out, or one of you drives home on a lunch break for a 30-minute feeding and play session.
* **Evening Shift (5:00 PM - 10:00 PM):** Shared duty, but assigned strictly. One person cooks dinner while the other watches the puppy like a hawk to prevent indoor accidents. 

## Weeks 11 Through 13: Introducing Independence and Enforced Naps

By week eleven, your puppy is starting to figure out that outside is the bathroom, but their energy levels will skyrocket. This is also the danger zone for overtired puppies, which leads directly to biting furniture and relentless whining. 

This is the exact time to introduce "enforced naps." Working couples often make the mistake of letting a puppy roam free until the puppy crashes. Instead, adopt a strict schedule of one hour of out-of-crate time—featuring training, a meal, and play—followed by two hours locked safely in a crate or an enclosed puppy-proof pen. 

Divide the midday logistics firmly. Do not leave it up to chance by texting each other at noon asking, "Are you coming home today?" Put it on a shared digital calendar. Partner A is responsible for the Monday and Wednesday midday check-ins; Partner B takes Tuesday and Thursday. Friday can be a flex day where whoever has the lighter afternoon schedule handles it. This eliminates mental fatigue and resentment.

## Weeks 14 Through 16: Building Basic Obedience Cues

With the house-training baseline established and your split-shift running smoothly, weeks fourteen through sixteen are for adding actual obedience. Because your time is limited by your work schedules, you cannot afford hour-long training sessions. 

Instead, practice micro-training. Keep small jars of kibble on the kitchen counter and by the front door. Every time you walk past the puppy, ask for a "sit" or a "down" before opening a door or putting down a food bowl. 

We used our morning coffee-brewing time for impulse-control drills. While the coffee brewed, I would ask the puppy to stay on a mat while I moved around the kitchen. It took two minutes, but doing this twice a day compounded into real impulse control by the end of the month. If your puppy starts regressing in crate training or house-breaking during this phase, do not panic; regression is normal as they hit their first minor adolescent boundary-testing stage. Just tighten up your supervision window and lean back on your calendar splits.

## Weeks 17 Plus: Solidifying the Routine and Looking Ahead

By the time your puppy reaches four months old, they can typically hold their bladder for roughly four hours, which drastically cuts down the midday logistical gymnastics required from working couples. You can safely drop down to one solid midday visit instead of two, and your puppy can start participating in longer morning walks because their growth plates can handle more impact.

Juggling a puppy and a career requires letting go of the idea that training has to look like a pristine obedience class. It looks like practicing loose-leash walking on the sidewalk outside your apartment in work clothes at 6:30 in the morning. It looks like coordinating your lunch breaks over text like air traffic controllers.
