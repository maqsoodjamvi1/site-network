Adding a new puppy to a home that already has resident dogs is an exciting milestone, but it also completely shifts the household dynamic. When I brought my second dog home, I quickly learned that relying on standard puppy training advice doesn't quite cut it. You aren't just teaching the puppy how to sit; you are managing the existing dogs' boundaries, preventing resource guarding, and making sure nobody feels left out. 

To keep the peace and set your new puppy up for long-term success, you need a structured game plan. Here is how I break down the first four weeks of integrating a new puppy into a multi-dog household.

## Week One: Neutral Territory and Strict Management

The absolute biggest mistake I see owners make in week one is allowing free roaming right out of the car. When your new puppy arrives, your resident dogs are naturally going to feel a bit defensive of their territory. 

Start by having a neutral party walk the resident dogs and the new puppy together in a neutral space, like a quiet park or a block away from your house. Let them sniff and interact outside before crossing your front threshold together. Once inside, do not let the puppy have free access to the house. Set up baby gates or playpens immediately. 

Your resident dogs need to know their safe zones—places like their crates or specific dog beds—are off-limits to the puppy. At the same time, keep your puppy's training sessions short and separate. Teach your puppy their name and basic house rules behind a baby gate so the older dogs can observe without feeling pressured.

## Week Two: Controlled Interactions and Feeding Protocols

By week two, the novelty of the puppy has worn off slightly, and your resident dogs might start showing signs of annoyance. This is normal, but you need to manage it carefully. 

Feeding time is the number one trigger for multi-dog household conflicts. During week two, feed your resident dogs and your puppy in completely separate rooms or inside closed crates. Do not leave toy baskets or chew sticks lying around the living room floor, as puppies do not yet understand doggy etiquette and will try to steal a senior dog's favorite chew.

* Feed all dogs separately to prevent resource guarding before it starts.
* Pick up high-value toys and chews when you cannot actively supervise the room.
* Reward your resident dogs with high-value treats whenever the puppy is nearby and they remain calm. This builds a positive mental association with the new arrival.

## Week Three: Parallel Training and Impulse Control

Now that basic household routines are settling in, week three is all about teaching your puppy how to exist calmly around older dogs. Puppies naturally want to climb all over resident dogs, bite their ears, and demand play 24/7. 

Older dogs often have zero patience for this. Instead of letting the puppy constantly pester them, practice parallel training. Put your puppy on a leash ten feet away from your resident dog, and practice basic commands like "sit" and "stay" while both dogs are in the same room. 

This teaches the puppy that good things happen when they focus on you, rather than harassing the resident dogs. If your resident dog gives a polite warning growl because the puppy is too close, do not punish the resident dog. They are communicating boundaries. Instead, calmly redirect the puppy away and reward the older dog for using communication instead of a snap.

## Week Four: Expanding Freedom and Individual Routine

By week four, you should start seeing glimmers of a cohesive pack. The puppy is beginning to understand household rules, and your resident dogs are learning that the puppy is a permanent fixture rather than a temporary annoyance.

This is the week to re-introduce individual one-on-one time. Your resident dogs remember what life was like before the puppy took up all your attention. Take your older dogs out for a walk by themselves while a family member watches the puppy, or vice versa. Maintaining that individual bond prevents resentment from building up. 

You can also start allowing supervised group hangouts in the living room, provided everyone can relax. If the puppy gets overly hyper, use a leash tether or a pen to enforce a mandatory nap. Overtired puppies are rude puppies, and keeping everyone rested prevents reactive outbursts.

Managing a multi-dog household takes patience, observation, and a willingness to step in before conflicts happen.
