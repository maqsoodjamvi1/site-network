If you have ever tried to teach a simple "sit" to one dog in a multi-dog home, you already know the immediate circus that ensues. The second you reach for a treat, your second dog comes barreling across the room, pushes the first dog out of the way, and sits so hard on the floor you would think they won a prize. 

When I first brought a second dog into my home, my training sessions completely derailed. I thought I just needed to be louder or faster. Instead, I learned that training one dog in a multi-dog household requires a completely different strategy than training an only dog. You aren't just teaching a dog a cue; you are managing dog psychology, resource guarding, and sheer canine FOMO.

## Set Up Physical Barriers Before You Say a Single Word

The biggest mistake I made early on was trying to train in an open living room with both dogs loose. Dogs are opportunists. If food or high-value praise is on the line, the un-targeted dog will naturally try to participate. 

Before you even pick up the treat pouch, separate your dogs using physical boundaries. You can use baby gates, a closed door, or even put the resting dog in a sturdy wire crate across the room. The goal is to remove the physical option of interference. 

If you have a very clingy second dog who barks or scratches at the baby gate the second you start working with dog number one, you need to prep them first. Give the observer dog a long-lasting chew, like a frozen Kong stuffed with peanut butter, inside their separated space a few minutes before you begin. This keeps their brain busy while you focus entirely on your training partner.

## Master the Art of the Scatter Treat

Even with a baby gate up, the non-training dog is likely staring at you with intense, laser-like focus. How do you reward Dog A without driving Dog B crazy behind the barrier? 

Use a technique I call the perimeter scatter. Before you ask Dog A to do anything, take a handful of kibble and toss it on the floor away from the training zone for Dog B—preferably in the opposite corner of the room or out the door into a hallway. 

While Dog B is frantically sniffing the ground to vacuum up every last piece of kibble, you have a crucial thirty-second window of quiet time to focus exclusively on Dog A. It buys you breathing room without needing an assistant to hold the second dog back.

## Keep Training Sessions Scarily Short

When you live with multiple dogs, patience wears thin quickly—both yours and theirs. If you try to run a ten-minute obedience session with Dog A while Dog B is whining behind a gate, frustration builds. 

Cut your training sessions down to two or three minutes max. I prefer doing multiple micro-sessions throughout the day rather than one long block. Do a quick "down-stay" practice while waiting for the kettle to boil. Work on "look at me" while letting the dogs back in from the backyard. 

Short sessions keep the non-training dog from melting down in isolation, and they keep the training dog from getting bored or overwhelmed. High frequency paired with short duration yields much faster results in a busy household.

## Rotate Who Gets the Spotlight

Dogs have an uncanny sense of fairness, and jealousy is a real hurdle in multi-dog homes. If Dog A always gets the special treats and the undivided attention, resentment builds, and you might accidentally trigger resource-guarding behaviors around you.

Make sure you strictly alternate who goes first. Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, start your morning session with Dog B. Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday, start with Dog A. 

* Keep a simple tally on your phone or fridge if you tend to forget who went first yesterday.
* Use visibly different value rewards if necessary, but keep the baseline treats equal so neither dog feels shortchanged.
* Always release both dogs with a simultaneous "all done" marker so they understand the session is mutually finished.

## Address the Bystander Anxiety

Sometimes the dog left behind isn't being pushy—they are genuinely anxious or confused about why they are being excluded. If your second dog paces, whines, or looks stressed while you work with the first dog, you need to back up. 

Start by simply rewarding the observer dog for staying calm behind the barrier while you walk toward Dog A. You are teaching the bystander dog that calm, quiet observation brings rewards, too. Over time, the second dog learns that being separated doesn't mean punishment; it just means a break before their own turn comes up.

Training in a multi-dog home takes patience, extra logistics, and a lot of kibble scattering. But by managing the environment first, you remove the chaos and let your dogs actually focus on learning.
