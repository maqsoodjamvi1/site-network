If you share your home with a Belgian Malinois, a German Shepherd, a Border Collie, or a high-drive terrier mix, you already know the reality of living with a dog bred for an intense job. These animals do not just have energy; they have an occupation-seeking missile inside their heads. When I brought home my first working-line German Shepherd, I made the classic mistake of trying to exhaust him physically before training. By month six, I had a dog who could run ten miles a day and still chew through a drywall corner because his brain was completely under-stimulated. 

Traditional obedience advice often tells you to start with "Sit" and "Stay" in a quiet living room. For a high-drive dog, that approach usually fails because it ignores the animal's core biological motivation: scanning the environment, hunting, and executing tasks. To prevent frustration for both of you, you need to teach commands that harness that intensity rather than fighting it. Here are the specific foundational skills I wish I had prioritized from day one.

## Teach "Place" Before "Stay"

Standard stays ask a high-drive dog to freeze while their environment keeps moving, which creates immense internal pressure. Often, this results in a cracked-out "down-stay" where the dog vibrates with tension, staring at you like a loaded spring ready to snap. 

Instead, teach a boundary-based "Place" command using an elevated cot or a specific mat. "Place" is an active job, not a passive punishment. 

1. Lure your dog onto the platform with a high-value treat.
2. The exact second all four paws are on the surface, mark the behavior (with a clicker or a verbal marker like "Yes!") and pay them *on* the platform.
3. Release them immediately with a clear release word like "Break!"
4. Gradually increase the duration they must remain on the platform before the release. 

This works because it gives them a clear geographical boundary and a specific target. High-drive dogs love parameters; once they understand the game is "stay on the cot to earn your reward," they channel their mental focus into holding that position.

## Master the Release Word as an Accelerator

Many owners treat the release word as an afterthought, but for a working breed, the release is the paycheck. If your dog does not fully understand that a release word means "you are completely free to move," they will constantly anticipate your next move and break position early.

Treat your release word like a starting gun. When you practice recalls or stationary positions, do not just say "Okay" in a casual voice. Build value into the release by making it explosive. 

* Put your dog in a short sit-stay.
* Take two steps away.
* Give your release word with high energy and toss a toy or a piece of kibble away from you so they have to chase it.

By teaching your dog that stillness earns the right to explode into movement, you use their natural predatory drive as the ultimate reinforcer. They learn that holding back is just the setup for the fun part.

## Channel the Grab with a Structured "Leave It"

High-drive dogs experience the world primarily through their mouths. Whether it is a squirrel darting across the yard, a dropped kitchen knife, or the hem of your pants, their instinct is to grab and hold. A soft "leave it" taught with boring kibble will fail the moment a real-world trigger appears.

You need to teach a functional "Leave It" that uses movement as the currency. 

* Hold a piece of food in a closed fist. Your dog will sniff, lick, and paw at it. Say nothing.
* The exact millisecond your dog pulls their nose away from your fist, mark that choice with "Yes!" and feed them a *different*, better piece of food from your other hand. Never let them eat the food they are actively trying to pry from your fist.
* Once they master that, progress to dragging a toy across the floor in front of them while telling them to leave it, rewarding them by eventually letting them catch the toy as the ultimate prize.

This teaches an invaluable psychological lesson: turning away from a moving stimulus is actually what makes the moving stimulus accessible. 

## Build a Flawless Emergency Recall Using Prey Drive

Recalls fail in high-drive dogs because calling them back usually means the fun stops. If you call your dog away from the fence line just to put them inside the crate, you are teaching them that coming to you ruins their day.

To fix this, turn your recall into a high-stakes game of chase where *you* are the prey. 

* Run backward away from your dog while making high-pitched, exciting noises.
* When they catch up to you, do not just hand them a dry kibble; physically engage with them by playing a brief game of tug with a toy you pulled out of your pocket.
* Practice this on a long line in open fields so they learn that coming when called results in an explosion of social and predatory play with you.

Getting these foundational skills in place early will save you months of behavioral headaches down the road. If you are currently mapping out a training curriculum for a new puppy or a rescue working breed, exploring structured gear, long lines, and high-value reward delivery methods can make a massive difference in your daily routine.
