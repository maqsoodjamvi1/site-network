Bringing home a shelter dog is an emotional milestone, but the first two weeks can also feel overwhelming. When I adopted my hound mix, Buster, he spent his first three days hiding behind the living room couch and refusing treats. I quickly learned that traditional dog training advice—which often assumes a dog walks into your home ready to learn—fails miserably with shelter rescues. 

Dogs from shelters are dealing with a flood of new smells, strange sounds, and shifting routines. Trying to teach "sit" or "stay" on day one usually results in frustration for both of you. Instead, you need a structured timeline that prioritizes decompression before heavy obedience. Here is the exact 14-day progression I used with Buster to build trust and teach core commands without breaking his confidence.

## Days 1 to 3: The Decompression Phase and the "Name Game"

For the first 72 hours, forget about traditional obedience. Your only goal is to let your dog realize that your home is a safe place. Shelter environments are loud and unpredictable, so your dog’s nervous system is likely still in overdrive. 

During this window, I only focus on one verbal cue: their name. Whenever Buster looked at me, I dropped a high-value treat—like a tiny piece of boiled chicken—right on the floor between his paws. I did not ask him to come to me, and I did not force eye contact. I just said his name in a bright, friendly tone, and when his ears flicked toward me or he glanced my way, *boom*, chicken reward. 

This teaches your new dog that paying attention to you equals good things happening, without putting any pressure on them to perform. Keep sessions under two minutes, and let your dog retreat to their crate or bed whenever they want.

## Days 4 to 7: Introducing "Look at Me" and "Leave It"

Once your dog is eating normally, exploring the house without trembling, and responding to their name, you can start adding actual structure. 

The first functional command to introduce is "Look at Me." This is your emergency brake for future walks and distractions. Hold a treat near your own eyes, say "Look," and the second your dog makes eye contact, mark the behavior with a verbal "Yes!" and deliver the treat. Practice this in a quiet hallway or living room with zero distractions. 

Next, introduce "Leave It." Grab a lower-value treat in a closed fist. Say "Leave it" as your dog sniffs your hand. Ignore their attempts to lick or paw at your knuckles. The exact second they pull their head back or stop trying, say "Yes!" and feed them a *different*, much better piece of food from your other hand. This establishes the rule that giving up something they want leads to something even better.

## Days 8 to 11: Mastering "Sit" and "Wait" at Thresholds

By week two, your dog is starting to understand how your household operates. Now is the time to add "Sit." 

Shelter dogs often jump up out of excitement or anxiety. To teach "Sit" without grabbing their collar or pushing their rear down, hold a treat right in front of their nose and slowly move it backward over their head toward their ears. As their nose goes up to follow the treat, their rear naturally hits the floor. The moment their bottom touches the ground, say "Yes!" and hand over the treat. 

Once they grasp "Sit," apply it to real-world scenarios. Make your dog sit before you clip their leash on, before you open the back door to let them into the yard, or before you put their food bowl down. This builds impulse control, which is the foundation of every good house-mannered dog.

## Days 12 to 14: Pulling It Together with Loose-Leash Walking and "Come"

The final phase of the two-week mark involves taking these skills on the road. A common mistake is trying to teach a shelter dog to heel while walking past barking neighborhood dogs on day two. By day twelve, your bond is stronger, and you can tackle outdoor focus.

When practicing the recall command ("Come"), never use it to end fun or do something your dog dislikes, like giving a bath. Instead, use a long leash in a quiet park or your backyard:
* Call your dog's name followed by "Come!" in an upbeat, running-away-from-them motion.
* Reward them with jackpot treats—three or four pieces of chicken in a row—when they reach you.
* Release them immediately back to sniffing with a cue like "Go play."

If your dog pulls on the leash during walks, use the "be a tree" method. The second tension hits the leash, stop walking entirely. Do not yank them back. Just stand completely still. The moment they take a step backward to put slack in the leash, praise them and resume walking. 

Teaching a newly adopted shelter dog requires patience and precise timing. By pacing your expectations over these first two weeks, you prevent behavioral regression and set clear expectations that last a lifetime. As you continue building this daily routine, your next step is figuring out how to handle minor setbacks like jumping or counter-surfing without losing the trust you've worked so hard to build.
