If you brought home a Shiba Inu, a Beagle, a Dachshund, or a Northern sled breed expecting a furry shadow that hangs on your every word, you might be in for a rude awakening. When I raised my first independent breed—a fiercely stubborn Hound mix named Otis—my standard tricks fell flat. He didn’t care about pleasing me. He cared about the dead squirrel behind the trash can. 

Independent and hound-type breeds were originally bred to work *away* from humans, making decisions on their own. Traditional obedience tactics based purely on wanting to make the owner happy simply do not work here. You need a different framework. This week-by-week schedule is designed to work with an independent mind rather than fighting against it.

## Week 1: Build the Currency of Attention

With a biddable breed like a Golden Retriever, you are the center of the universe. With an independent breed, you are competing with a hundred more interesting smells. Week one is not about sit and stay; it is about proving that looking at you is the most lucrative thing your puppy can do.

Stop using a food bowl. Measure out your puppy's daily kibble and use it entirely as training currency. Every time your puppy accidentally looks at your face, mark it with a clear word like "Yes!" and hand over a piece of food. 

Practice the "Name Game" in low-distraction environments like the hallway. Say your puppy’s name in a normal tone. The exact millisecond their head snaps toward you to see what you want, mark and reward. Repeat this ten times a session, twice a day. You are teaching them that paying attention to you triggers good things, laying the groundwork for recall later.

## Week 2: Establish Boundaries Without Breaking Trust

Independent pups are often pragmatic. If a rule doesn't make sense to them, they will push against it. Instead of harsh corrections—which often cause these breeds to shut down or become defensive—rely heavily on management and environmental control.

If your puppy insists on chewing the rug corners, do not spend hours yelling "leave it." Block access to the rug using baby gates or exercise pens. 

For house training, take the initiative entirely out of their paws. Take your puppy outside every 45 minutes during waking hours. When they eliminate outside, throw a jackpot party with high-value treats like tiny bits of roasted chicken. If you wait for an independent puppy to tell you they need to go out, you will be waiting until they decide to use your favorite rug as a bathroom.

## Week 3: Master Short-Duration Cues

Traditional training advice often suggests pushing for longer stays and holds as the puppy improves. With independent breeds, long-duration holds usually result in the puppy getting bored and walking away to find a better hobby. 

Keep your training sessions under three minutes. Seriously. End the session *before* your puppy loses interest. 

When teaching a basic cue like "Sit," do not hold the treat over their nose forever, or you will create a dog that only works when they see food in your hand. Instead, lure them into position once, reward, and then immediately reset them by tossing a treat a few feet away so they have to trot back to you. This builds engagement and keeps the energy high, preventing the stubborn freeze-up.

## Week 4: Turn Training Into a Game of Negotiation

By week four, your puppy knows the rules, and they are testing which ones are optional. This is where stubborn breeds shine. If Otis decided he didn’t want to come inside from the yard, he would literally drop to the ground and turn into a furry boulder. Dragging him caused a tug-of-war mentality.

Instead, change the game. If your puppy refuses to come when called, run *away* from them in the opposite direction while making high-pitched, exciting noises. Their predatory chase instinct will kick in, and they will usually sprint after you. Once they catch you, reward heavily. 

Introduce puzzle toys and snuffle mats for feeding. Independent breeds have sharp minds that need problem-solving outlets. A dog that has burned mental energy figuring out how to slide plastic compartments for kibble is far less likely to invent their own entertainment by chewing your baseboards.

Raising an independent dog requires patience, a lack of ego, and a heavy supply of high-value treats. You aren't going to get a robot who executes commands with military precision, but with consistency, you *will* build a cooperative partnership.
