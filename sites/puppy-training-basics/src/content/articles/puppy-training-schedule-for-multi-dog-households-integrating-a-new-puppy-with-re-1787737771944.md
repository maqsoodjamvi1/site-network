Bringing an 8-week-old puppy into a home with existing dogs is a completely different challenge than raising a single puppy. When I brought my second dog home, I quickly realized my older dog didn't share my enthusiasm for the squeaky, needle-toothed intruder. Adult dogs have established routines, personal space boundaries, and zero patience for puppy antics. 

To keep the peace and set your new puppy up for success, you need a structured daily routine that balances socialization, basic obedience, and fierce protection of your older dog's sanity. Here is how I structure the critical window from 8 to 16 weeks in a multi-dog home.

## Weeks 8 to 10: Containment, Neutral Ground, and Controlled Exposure

During these first two weeks, your primary goal is not making your dogs best friends. Your goal is preventing your older dog from becoming resentful and keeping your puppy safe from overcorrection. Puppies at this age lack social etiquette and will bite tails, pounce on sleeping seniors, and steal food bowls.

Set up a system of baby gates and exercise pens immediately. Your puppy should spend a significant portion of the day in a designated "puppy zone" where they can see the household action without having physical access to your resident dogs. 

*   **Feed separately:** Feed your resident dogs and your puppy in entirely different rooms or behind closed crates. Resource guarding starts early, and a puppy walking up to an adult dog's bowl is a recipe for a fight.
*   **Use neutral territory for intros:** If possible, do the very first meeting outside in the yard or on a neutral sidewalk rather than right inside the front door. 
*   **Keep interactions short:** Limit face-to-face greetings to five minutes at a time, followed by a mandatory nap in the crate for the puppy. Overtired puppies are obnoxious, and adult dogs have a low tolerance for obnoxious behavior.

## Weeks 11 to 13: Parallel Training and Structured Socialization

By week 11, your puppy is starting to understand your voice, and your resident dogs are getting used to the new rhythm of the house. This is the time to introduce parallel training. 

Instead of training your puppy alone in the kitchen while your older dog watches from the couch, bring them together. Put your older dog in a reliable "down-stay" a few feet away while you practice basic puppy commands like "sit" or "look at me." This teaches the puppy that other dogs are present when good things (like treats) happen, and it teaches your older dog that the puppy isn't stealing all your attention.

Manage household traffic carefully. When you are sitting on the floor playing with the puppy, use your body to block the puppy from rushing your older dog if the senior dog is resting. Your resident dogs need to know you are acting as a buffer. If your older dog gives a low warning growl because the puppy is biting their ear, do not scold the older dog. That growl is healthy communication. Instead, calmly redirect the puppy away and reward your older dog for communicating politely instead of snapping.

## Weeks 14 to 16: Expanding Freedoms and Establishing New House Rules

At this stage, your puppy has more physical stamina and coordination. They will likely start trying to initiate actual play with your resident dogs. Pay close attention to the body language of your adult dogs during these play sessions. 

Healthy puppy-adult play involves taking turns. One dog chases, then they switch roles. There should be loose bodies, bouncing, and pauses. If your older dog freezes, curls their lip, tucks their tail, or constantly tries to get away from the puppy by jumping onto the couch, step in immediately. Your older dog is telling you they are overwhelmed. 

Integrate your puppy into short, calm household routines with the pack. If you go for a walk, practice walking your resident dog on your dominant side while a partner handles the puppy on a separate leash a few paces back. Avoid letting them tangle their leashes together, which can cause panic and accidental aggression.

Managing a multi-dog household with a new puppy requires constant supervision and strict management, but the effort pays off tenfold. By week 16, you are laying the foundation for a harmonious pack where boundaries are respected and everyone knows their place in the daily routine.
