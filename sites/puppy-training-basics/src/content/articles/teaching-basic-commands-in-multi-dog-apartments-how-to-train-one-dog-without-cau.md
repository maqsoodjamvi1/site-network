Living in an apartment with multiple dogs means square footage is tight, and dog antics feel a lot bigger when they happen two feet away from your couch. When I brought my second rescue home to join my older terrier mix in a two-bedroom apartment, I quickly realized that traditional training advice falls flat in small spaces. 

If you try to teach a basic command like "sit" or "stay" in a small living room with two eager dogs, you usually end up with a pile of tangled leashes, frustrated pets, and one dog aggressively elbowing the other out of the way for a piece of cheese. Teaching basic commands in a multi-dog home isn't just about teaching manners; it is about managing canine psychology so training builds confidence rather than resource guarding and rivalry.

## The Crate and Barrier Setup

Before you even say the word "sit," you need to manage the physical environment. In a house with a big yard, you can put one dog in another room. In an apartment, you are likely working in an open-plan kitchen and living area. 

My biggest breakthrough came when I stopped trying to train both dogs in the same room without boundaries. I started using a sturdy wire exercise pen and well-placed crates. When it is time for a training session with Dog A, Dog B goes into their crate or behind a baby gate with a long-lasting chew, like a frozen Kong stuffed with plain Greek yogurt and kibble. 

This does two things. First, it prevents the un-trained dog from rushing in and stealing treats, which instantly spikes frustration. Second, it teaches the observing dog impulse control. They learn that good things happen when they watch quietly from behind a barrier. Rotate who gets the training session first so nobody feels chronically left out.

## Establishing the "Release Word" and Individual Cues

When you finally transition to having both dogs in the same room for practice, you cannot use general commands. If you shout "sit!" into the air, both dogs will scramble to perform it, and the faster, pushier dog will usually try to bully the slower one away from your hands.

You need to use individual name cues. Instead of "sit," your new vocabulary is "[Dog's Name], sit." More importantly, you need a strict release word that applies only to the dog currently working. 

I use the word "free." When I am working with my younger dog, Buster, I will tell him to "Buster, down." My older dog, Luna, knows that unless I use her name or give her specific release word, she must maintain her stay on her dog bed across the room. If Luna tries to creep over to steal Buster's treat, I calmly block her with my body and reset her to her bed. Start with very short durations—just two or three seconds of focus—before releasing Buster and rewarding Luna for holding her position.

## Managing High-Value Treats Without Fights

Food is the primary currency of basic obedience, but in a small apartment, high-value treats can easily trigger barrier frustration or redirected aggression. If you drop a piece of steak on the laminate floor between two excited dogs, you are asking for a scuffle.

When training in close quarters, ban all floor-dropping entirely. Every single piece of food must be delivered directly from your hand to the dog's mouth. Furthermore, practice hand-targeting (touching their nose to your palm) to guide their bodies away from each other. 

If one dog tends to guard space, practice your treats behind a physical divider even when both dogs are technically "out." Stand on opposite sides of a coffee table or keep one dog on a short house leash so you can physically manage their proximity. Keep training sessions short—five minutes maximum—long before fatigue or food-envy leads to a growl.

## Building Routine and Fairness

Dogs in multi-dog households watch your routines like hawks. If they suspect favoritism, rivalry spikes. To keep the peace, rotate the order of who gets trained first every single day. Monday, Buster goes first. Tuesday, Luna goes first. 

Keep your body language neutral and avoid scolding the dog who is waiting. If Luna whines while Buster is doing his down-stay, do not yell at Luna, as that adds chaotic energy to the room. Simply ignore the whining and reward the silence. Over time, they realize that patience is the only reliable way to earn the good treats.

Taking things slow in a small space pays off massively in the long run. By keeping sessions structured and preventing competitive free-for-alls, you turn obedience practice into a calm, predictable part of apartment life.
