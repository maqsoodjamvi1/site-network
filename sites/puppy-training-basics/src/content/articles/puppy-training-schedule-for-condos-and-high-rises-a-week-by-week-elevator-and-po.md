Raising a puppy on the 14th floor of a high-rise building is a completely different experience than training a dog with a sprawling suburban backyard. When your front door opens directly into a shared hallway instead of a patch of grass, house-training requires a logistical strategy. You cannot simply open the back door while sipping your morning coffee. Every potty break involves shoes, a leash, an elevator ride, and navigating a lobby. 

Living in a condo means you need a hybrid system. You have to balance indoor relief options for those middle-of-the-night emergencies with the ultimate goal of outdoor street training. Here is a practical, week-by-week blueprint designed specifically for apartment and high-rise living to help you survive the puppy phase without losing your sanity or your security deposit.

## Weeks 1 to 2: The Indoor Setup and Elevator Desensitization

Your first two weeks are all about damage control and laying the groundwork. If you bring a puppy home at eight weeks old, their bladder capacity is roughly one to two hours during the day. Waiting for an elevator from the top floor is a recipe for indoor accidents in the hallway. 

Set up a puppy pen (playpen) in your living area with a designated potty pad zone on one side and their crate, water, and bed on the other. Puppies naturally avoid soiling where they sleep, so this teaches them the concept of a designated elimination area. 

Simultaneously, start desensitizing your puppy to the building infrastructure. Do not wait until they are fully vaccinated to introduce them to the elevator. Carry your puppy in your arms or a sling into the elevator during non-peak hours. Feed them high-value treats like tiny bits of boiled chicken the second the doors close. This creates a positive association with the small, moving metal box, preventing future leash-reactivity or fear in shared spaces.

## Weeks 3 to 4: The 60-Second Rule and Hallway Protocol

By weeks three and four, your puppy’s bladder control improves slightly, and you can start phasing out indoor pads if you live on a lower floor, or refining how you use them if you are high up. 

When it is time for a trip outside, use the "60-second rule." Put your shoes on, clip the leash to the puppy, scoop them up, and aim to hit the front door within one minute of waking from a nap. The moment your feet hit the sidewalk, carry them directly to your designated patch of urban grass—whether that is a median strip, a small dog relief area, or a local park corner. 

Hallways are high-traffic zones filled with strange smells, echoing sounds, and neighbors. Practice strict "leave it" and "focus" cues in the hallway. Keep your puppy close to your leg using a short leash. If a neighbor walks by with another dog, reward your puppy with a treat just for looking at you instead of reacting. 

## Weeks 5 to 6: Mastering Leash Communication and Curb Etiquette

By this stage, your puppy is around twelve weeks old and likely has received their second round of vaccinations, making outdoor exploration much safer. Now is the time to transition away from indoor potty pads entirely, unless you live in a high-rise where outdoor access genuinely takes longer than three minutes.

City walking presents unique challenges that suburban puppies never face. Your puppy needs to learn how to ignore discarded food on the sidewalk, loud city buses, construction noise, and passing bicycles. 

*   **The Urban Sit:** Practice having your puppy sit at every single street corner before stepping onto the asphalt. This builds impulse control and teaches them that curbs mark the boundary between safe walking zones and dangerous streets.
*   **Trash Avoidance:** City sidewalks are full of chicken bones, takeout wrappers, and dropped food. Practice the "drop it" cue daily inside your condo using boring kibble, then graduate to high-value treats on your walks so you can safely extract dangerous items from their mouth.
*   **Elevator Etiquette:** Teach your puppy to sit politely facing the door inside the elevator, rather than jumping up to greet every neighbor who steps inside.

## Weeks 7 to 8 and Beyond: Consistency and Routine Maintenance

By week eight in your home, your puppy should understand the routine. They know that waking up means an immediate elevator ride down to the pavement, and they understand that eliminating outside brings massive praise and treats. 

Regression is normal around four to five months of age as your puppy enters adolescence. They might suddenly have an accident in the elevator lobby or refuse to walk past a noisy construction site. When this happens, do not panic or scold. Simply return to the basics: shorten their freedom inside the condo, increase the frequency of your elevator trips, and carry high-value treats on every single outing to reinforce good behavior.

Raising a high-rise dog takes immense patience, but the payoff is a wonderfully adaptable companion who is unfazed by urban chaos.
