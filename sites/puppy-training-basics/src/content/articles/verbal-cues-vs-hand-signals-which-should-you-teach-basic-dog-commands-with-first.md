When I first brought home a high-energy rescue puppy named Buster, I made a classic beginner mistake. I stood in the middle of the kitchen, flailing my arms like a semaphore flag while shouting "Sit! Sit! DOWN!" at the top of my lungs. Buster just tilted his head, completely overwhelmed. He had no idea what I wanted because I was firing two entirely different languages at his brain at the same time. 

If you are standing at this exact crossroads with a new dog, you might be wondering whether you should teach basic commands using verbal words, hand signals, or a mix of both. After years of training my own dogs and helping neighbors navigate those chaotic first months, I have found a clear, practical answer for which one to tackle first.

## Why Dogs Actually Understand Hands Faster Than Words

Human beings are verbal creatures. We rely on spoken language to communicate, so it feels natural to assume our dogs do the same. But evolutionarily and cognitively, dogs are visual animals. Long before they ever learned to parse human vocabulary, wild canines were reading body language, subtle posture shifts, and movement patterns from each other. 

When you test this in real life, the difference is striking. If you drop a piece of food on the floor and point at it, a domestic dog will immediately look at your finger and then at the food. Try spelling out "FOOD" in the air, and your dog will just stare at your hand. 

Because of this natural bias, teaching hand signals first often leads to much faster success during those frustrating initial training sessions. You do not need your dog to master the English language to get them to lower their hindquarters; you just need to guide their nose with your hand.

## How to Teach Hand Signals First (A Step-by-Step Guide)

Let us look at how this works in practice with the most important command you will ever teach: "Sit." You do not need any fancy equipment—just a handful of small, smelly treats like tiny pieces of cheddar cheese or boiled chicken.

Start by holding a treat right in front of your dog's nose, letting them sniff it without actually biting it. Slowly move that treat upward and slightly backward over their head toward their ears. As their nose follows the food upward, their rear end will naturally drop to the floor to keep their balance. The exact moment their bottom hits the floor, say "Yes!" and hand over the treat. 

In this scenario, your hand movement is the physical lure and eventual signal. You are using physics and geometry rather than vocal persuasion. Once you repeat this motion ten or fifteen times across a couple of short, five-minute sessions, your dog will start anticipating the movement. Eventually, you can fade the food out of your hand and just use an empty-handed upward motion. That empty-handed gesture is your official hand signal.

## Bringing Verbal Cues Into the Picture

Does this mean you should completely ignore words like "Sit," "Stay," and "Come"? Absolutely not. Verbal cues are vital, especially for situations where your dog is looking away from you, or when you are calling them from across a dark room, or when you simply have your hands full of groceries. 

The secret is to introduce the verbal cue *after* the hand signal, not before. 

Most people make the mistake of saying "Sit," pausing, and then waiting for the dog to figure it out. If the dog doesn't know what "Sit" means yet, you are just repeating a meaningless noise. Instead, pair them correctly:

*   Say the verbal command softly just a split second *before* you make your hand motion.
*   Make the hand signal and guide your dog into the position.
*   Reward them immediately.

By saying the word right before the physical motion, your dog's brain connects the sound of the word to the action they are already about to perform. Over time, the sound of the word starts to trigger the mental image of the action. Eventually, you can drop the hand signal entirely if you want a purely verbal response.

## Choosing Your Personal Training Strategy

While hand signals are generally easier for dogs to grasp initially, the best approach is to use both simultaneously once your dog understands the baseline mechanics of a command. Deaf dogs thrive entirely on hand signals, while working dogs often benefit from distance commands that combine sharp verbal markers with crisp physical gestures. 

If you have a stubborn breed or a very distracted puppy, start with hand signals to build quick confidence and early wins. Once your dog realizes that following your physical cues leads to high-value rewards, layering in words becomes remarkably easy. 

To help you decide on your exact daily routine, consider mapping out your next steps by evaluating your dog's primary distractions and choosing the specific reward treats that will keep their attention focused on your hands.
