Balancing a full-time job, kids, and a new puppy feels a bit like running a three-ring circus while juggling sideways. When my husband and I brought home our Labrador mix last year, both of us were working hybrid schedules, and our two kids were in elementary school. We quickly realized that rigid, internet-famous training schedules meant for stay-at-home dog trainers were going to crash and burn in our house. 

If you leave the house for work, manage school drop-offs, and still need to cook dinner, you need a routine built around real life. Here is the realistic, week-by-week schedule we used to survive the puppy phase without losing our jobs or our sanity.

## Week 1: Survival, Bonding, and Crate Basics

The first week is not about teaching fancy tricks. It is about establishing a predictable rhythm and managing your puppy's bladder. 

Because working parents cannot stare at a puppy eight hours a day, crate training is your best friend right from day one. Place the crate in a relatively quiet spot at night, but put a secondary playpen or gated area in the kitchen or family room for daytime monitoring. 

*   **The 6:00 AM Shift:** One parent takes the puppy outside immediately upon waking, while the other gets the kids' breakfast started. 
*   **The Midday Plan:** If you work outside the home, book a dog walker for a midday visit, or have a family member drop by to let the puppy out and provide a quick meal.
*   **Nighttime Potty Breaks:** Expect to wake up at least once or twice during the night to take the puppy out. Tag-team this with your partner so you do not both become sleep-deprived zombies.

Keep training sessions short—about two minutes, three times a day. Practice calling the puppy's name and rewarding them with a high-value treat like a tiny piece of boiled chicken whenever they look at you.

## Week 2: Implementing the Work-From-Home or Out-of-House Routine

By week two, the novelty of the puppy has worn off for the kids, and reality sets in. This is the week to cement boundaries so the puppy does not learn bad habits while you are busy on work calls.

If you work remotely, set up a puppy pen right next to your desk. Toss a frozen Kong stuffed with plain Greek yogurt or peanut butter into the pen when you have an important video meeting. This buys you 20 minutes of absolute quiet.

For parents who commute to an office, rely heavily on structured nap times. Puppies need 18 to 20 hours of sleep a day. If your puppy is biting ankles, racing around the living room, and ignoring commands, they are likely overtired. Put them in their crate for a enforced two-hour nap while you are away or busy with chores. 

Involve the kids by giving them age-appropriate jobs. Seven-year-olds can measure out the puppy's kibble for the day into a container, and nine-year-olds can practice "sit" and "stay" using dinner pellets as treats under your supervision.

## Weeks 3 and 4: Socialization and Impulse Control

Once your puppy is comfortable in the house, it is time to expand their world. Working parents often struggle to find time for socialization, but you can weave it into your existing family errands.

Take the puppy along when you pick the kids up from soccer practice or walk to the neighborhood bus stop. Let them watch skateboarders, hear sirens, and see people wearing hats from a safe distance while you feed them treats. This prevents fear-based reactivity later on.

Impulse control is also critical for homes with children. Kids tend to drop food, run fast, and wave toys around—all of which trigger a puppy's instinct to jump and nip. 

*   Teach the "Leave It" command using a closed fist containing a treat. Only open your hand when the puppy backs away and stops sniffing.
*   Practice the "Off" command by turning your back and ignoring the puppy the second their paws touch your kids' clothes.
*   Teach your children to freeze like trees if the puppy gets too mouthy, rather than squealing and running away, which turns the puppy into a chasing predator.

## Weeks 5 and 6: Consistency and Leash Manners

By the end of the first month, your puppy should know their name, sit on command, and understand that the crate is a safe place to rest. Now is the time to tackle loose-leash walking, which is notoriously difficult for busy families.

Instead of a long, structured morning walk that you do not have time for, combine mental exercise with physical movement. Feed half of your puppy's breakfast out of a snuffle mat or puzzle toy while you pack the kids' school lunches. Then, take a short, 10-minute walk around the block focused entirely on loose leash work. 

Reward your puppy every single time they walk beside your knee without pulling. If they pull, stop walking immediately and wait until the leash goes slack before moving forward again. 

Training a puppy while working and raising children requires patience, grace, and a willingness to lower your housekeeping standards for a couple of months. As you look toward the next steps of your puppy's development, establishing these foundational routines now will pay off with a well-behaved family dog.
