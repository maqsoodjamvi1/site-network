If you spend more than ten minutes scrolling through LinkedIn, Substack, or Instagram, it is almost guaranteed that someone you graduated with, started a podcast with, or once shared a draft with will announce a massive win. Maybe they hit the bestseller list, signed a lucrative brand deal, or launched a project that instantly gets ten thousand likes. 

Your stomach drops. That familiar, burning ache of creative envy hits you right in the chest. Instead of feeling inspired, you feel paralyzed, wondering why your own work is moving at a snail's pace. 

When this happens, mindless scrolling only makes it worse. What actually helps is stepping away from the screen, closing the apps, and resetting your brain with a good book. Over the years, I’ve found specific titles that cut through the noise of comparison and help me get back to my own desk. Here are seven personal growth books that helped me stop measuring my worth by other people's highlight reels and get back to making things.

## *Steal Like an Artist* by Austin Kleon

If your envy stems from feeling like everything has already been done, or that your peers are just inherently more original than you, this short, punchy book is the antidote. Austin Kleon argues that originality is basically a myth and that all creative work builds on what came before. 

Instead of telling you to lock yourself in a room until you have a lightning-bolt epiphany, Kleon encourages you to actively study the work of people you admire, remix their ideas, and be open about your influences. Reading this shifts your mindset from "Why them and not me?" to "What can I learn from how they built this?" It is a quick read you can finish in one sitting, making it ideal for an afternoon when your creative confidence is scraping the bottom.

## *The Creative Act: A Way of Being* by Rick Rubin

Legendary music producer Rick Rubin approaches creativity not as a competitive sport, but as a spiritual and practical practice. When you are drowning in envy, you are usually obsessing over the outcome—the book deal, the metrics, the applause. Rubin drags your focus entirely back to the process.

He treats art as a reflection of who you are right now, rather than a scorecard to prove your worth against your peers. Whenever I catch myself envying someone else's launch, opening Rubin’s book reminds me that nobody else has my specific vantage point or life experience. It quiets the external noise and makes the blank page feel inviting again instead of intimidating.

## *The War of Art* by Steven Pressfield

Envy is often just Resistance in disguise. Steven Pressfield famously defines Resistance as the internal force that stops us from doing our actual work, and it frequently manifests as scrolling through social media, obsessing over what other creators are achieving, and feeling like a fraud.

Pressfield writes with a blunt, no-nonsense toughness that acts like a cold glass of water to the face. He treats creation as a professional duty rather than a mood-dependent hobby. If you find yourself paralyzed by the success of peers, this book will help you identify that procrastination-via-envy for what it is: a defense mechanism to keep you from facing your own blank page.

## *Big Magic: Creative Living Beyond Fear* by Elizabeth Gilbert

Elizabeth Gilbert takes aim at the suffocating pressure we put on our creative pursuits to pay our bills, validate our egos, and make us famous. When a peer succeeds, we often feel like our own creative life has somehow failed or diminished. Gilbert dismantles this zero-sum mindset completely.

She argues that ideas are floating around waiting for someone to manifest them, and just because someone else grabbed one doesn’t mean the universe has run out of inspiration for you. Her warm, conversational tone feels like sitting down with a wiser friend who gently tells you to stop worrying about what everyone else is doing and just finish your draft.

## *Do the Work* by Steven Pressfield

Yes, Pressfield gets two spots on this list because *The War of Art* diagnoses the problem, while *Do the Work* is the tactical field manual. When you are knee-deep in creative envy, you don't need a theoretical framework; you need a step-by-step checklist to get out of your own head.

This book walks you through the actual stages of starting, sticking with, and finishing a project despite the self-doubt that creeps in when you see others winning. It teaches you to start before you feel ready, ignore the critics (and the successful peers), and ship your work into the world regardless of how polished it is.

## *Show Your Work!* by Austin Kleon

This is the practical sibling to *Steal Like an Artist*. If you feel like your peers are winning simply because they are better at marketing themselves, Kleon teaches you how to share your journey without feeling sleazy or performing for the algorithm.

Instead of telling you to pitch yourself as an overnight expert, it encourages the radical act of documenting your process and sharing what you are learning along the way. Reading this helps diffuse the bitterness of envy by transforming your relationship with your audience from a hostile competition into a community of people figuring things out together.

## *Refuse to Choose!* by Barbara Sher

Sometimes creative envy happens because your peers are hyper-focused specialists who are climbing one single mountain, while your brain wants to build a dozen different things at once. If you are a multipassionate creator who feels behind because you can't stick to just one niche, Barbara Sher is your champion.

She gives validation to "Scanners"—people who are endlessly curious about multiple subjects and projects. Seeing a peer win an award in a specific category can make you feel inadequate if your interests span five different industries. Sher’s book helps you embrace your wide-ranging curiosity as a superpower rather than a distraction, giving you permission to build a creative life on your own eclectic terms.

Choosing one of these books and reading just a single chapter the next time you feel that familiar sting of comparison can completely alter your trajectory for the day.
