Buying crystals online feels a bit like a treasure hunt, but without a strict eye, it is easy to end up with a piece of dyed glass instead of the genuine mineral you paid for. When I first started collecting, I learned my lessons the hard way—like the time a bright blue "turquoise" I ordered turned out to be stabilized howlite with marker streaks that rubbed off on my fingers. 

The online crystal market is flooded with mass-produced fakes, synthetic materials sold under spiritual-sounding trade names, and heat-treated stones disguised as rare finds. If you are about to spend your hard-earned money on a piece for your collection, you need practical ways to spot the imposters before you hit checkout. Here is what I look for when I am shopping for crystals on the internet.

## Watch Out for Too-Good-To-Be-True Prices and "Rare" Colors

Nature does not make neon pink amethyst, electric blue quartz, or perfectly spherical spheres of rainbow fluorite for five dollars. If a seller on Etsy, Instagram, or eBay is offering a massive, flawless cluster of a notoriously expensive stone for the price of a latte, run the other way. 

Real minerals have imperfections, inclusions, and natural color variations. If every single stone in a seller's inventory looks identical, or if the colors look spray-painted on, you are looking at man-made glass, resin, or plastic. 

## The Trap of Heat-Treated and Man-Made Stones

Not all fakes are outright plastic; many are real minerals that have been altered in a lab and sold under misleading names. While heat-treated stones still have energetic properties to many practitioners, you deserve to know what you are actually buying.

*   **Citrine vs. Baked Amethyst:** True natural citrine is a pale, smoky yellow or earthy mustard color. The vast majority of bright, carrot-orange "citrine" on the market is actually low-grade amethyst baked in a kiln at high temperatures. 
*   **Aura Quartz:** Those metallic, rainbow-coated quartz points did not come out of the earth that way. They are real quartz crystals bonded with vaporized gold, titanium, or other metals in a laboratory chamber. 
*   **Goldstone:** This sparkly brown or blue stone is entirely man-made from glass infused with copper flakes. It is pretty, but it is a manufactured novelty, not a crystal dug from the ground.

## Scrutinize the Listing Photos and Seller Policies

Shopping online means you cannot touch the stone, so you have to rely heavily on the visual evidence provided. Be wary of listings that use generic stock photos instead of pictures of the exact piece you will receive. If a shop uses professional glamour shots with heavy studio lighting, ask for a video or a natural daylight photo.

Check the return policy before you put anything in your cart. A reputable seller who stands by the authenticity of their minerals will usually accept returns if the stone arrives and is clearly synthetic glass or plastic. If a shop has a strict "no returns" policy coupled with blurry photos, consider it a major red flag.

## Read Between the Lines of the Description

Pay close attention to the vocabulary the seller uses in the product description. Scammers and low-effort dropshippers often use clever phrasing to avoid lying outright while still misleading buyers.

Look for words like "man-made," "synthetic," "simulated," "stabilized," or "glass-filled." If a listing title says "Natural Healing Opalite," that is a contradiction in terms—opalite is a man-made glass, not a natural mineral. Similarly, terms like "hydrothermal quartz" mean the stone was grown in a lab, which is fine if that is what you want, but you shouldn't pay wild natural-crystal prices for it.

## Simple Questions to Ask the Seller

If you are eyeing a piece from a small shop or a mineral dealer online, do not hesitate to send a quick message. Asking a few direct questions can instantly tell you whether the seller knows their inventory or is just flipping cheap wholesale items from a factory.

Ask them where the crystal was mined or sourced, and whether it has undergone any treatments like dyeing, heating, or stabilization. A knowledgeable seller will be happy to share those details. If they dodge the question or get defensive, take your business elsewhere.

Building a crystal collection online takes a bit of skepticism and patience, but knowing what to look for keeps your collection authentic and your wallet safe. As you start browsing for your next piece, take a moment to evaluate the seller's transparency and check the return policy to ensure you are investing in the real deal.
