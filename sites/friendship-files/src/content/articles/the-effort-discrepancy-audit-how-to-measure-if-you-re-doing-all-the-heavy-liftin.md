Have you ever looked at your text messages and realized you are always the one asking, "How was your weekend?" Or maybe you are the designated planner for every single coffee date, birthday dinner, and casual Friday hangout, while your friend just passively shows up—or worse, cancels at the last minute. 

When you feel like you are doing all the heavy lifting in a relationship, it is easy to brush it off as bad timing or a busy season of life. But after a while, that imbalance starts to feel heavy. To figure out if your friendship is genuinely one-sided or just going through a rough patch, I use a simple framework I call the Effort Discrepancy Audit. 

## Step 1: Track the Initiation Ratio for Two Weeks

Memory is notoriously unreliable when we feel resentful. When we are hurt, our brains tend to highlight all the times we texted first and forget the rare Tuesday afternoon when our friend actually reached out. 

To get past your gut feelings, grab a notebook or open the notes app on your phone for a quick, two-week tracking experiment. Look at your communication logs and count three specific metrics:
* Who initiated contact over the last 14 days (texts, DMs, phone calls)?
* Who proposed the last three plans or get-togethers?
* Who asked open-ended questions to keep the conversation going when it stalled?

If you find that you are initiating 90 percent of the interactions, you have your first concrete data point. This isn't about keeping score to be petty; it is about seeing the actual baseline of the dynamic without emotional distortion.

## Step 2: Test the "Drop and Wait" Boundary

Once you have a baseline, it is time to run a behavioral test. For the next seven to ten days, completely stop filling the conversational and logistical void. 

This means if you usually text every morning, let your friend text first. If you are the one who always suggests grabbing tacos on Thursday, let Thursday pass without a word from you about dinner. 

What you are looking for here isn't whether they eventually text you—because eventually, most people will reach out if enough time passes. Instead, look at the *latency*. Does it take them three days to notice you have gone quiet? Do they reach out only when they need a favor, or do they check in because they actually missed your presence? In my experience, a reciprocally invested friend will usually feel the silence within a few days and step up to fill the gap. A one-sided friendship will often result in a week of absolute radio silence until you break it.

## Step 3: Evaluate the Crisis and Celebration Test

Low-effort friends are often great at hanging out when life is fun, but relationships are ultimately tested by how people handle high-stakes moments. Think back to the last major event in your life—a promotion, a bad breakup, a sick parent, or even just a really stressful week at work.

Did this friend show up for you without you having to beg for support? Or did they offer a quick "that sucks" text and immediately pivot the conversation back to their own daily routine? 

Conversely, look at how you treat their milestones. If you are regularly moving mountains to remember their big presentation, send thoughtful gifts, and celebrate their wins, but they offer a generic emoji when you share good news, the effort discrepancy goes deeper than just being "bad at texting." It points to a fundamental mismatch in how much you value each other.

## Step 4: Have a Specific Conversation, Not a General Complaint

If your audit reveals a glaring imbalance, you have a choice to make: step back and let the friendship fade, or talk about it. If you choose to speak up, avoid vague statements like "you never try anymore," which usually makes people defensive.

Instead, point to a specific pattern using neutral language. You might say, "I've noticed over the last few months that I've been doing all the planning for our get-togethers. I love seeing you, but it's starting to feel like a one-way street, and I'd love for us to share that mental load." 

Pay close attention to how they respond. A defensive friend will make excuses or accuse you of being too demanding. A self-aware friend will apologize, acknowledge the drop in effort, and actually make a tangible change over the following weeks.

Deciding what to do next requires looking honestly at the data you just gathered and figuring out whether this relationship is worth fighting for or if it is simply time to redirect that energy toward people who match your level of care.
