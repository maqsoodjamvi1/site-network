Have you ever walked away from a phone call or coffee date with a friend feeling completely hollowed out? You spent an hour nodding, offering advice, and managing their crisis, but the moment you tried to mention your own stressful week at work or your kid’s ear infection, they seamlessly steered the conversation right back to themselves. 

I’ve been there. For a long time, I thought being a good friend meant being an infinite sponge for other people's problems. But eventually, I realized there is a massive difference between supporting someone through a rough patch and functioning as their unpaid therapist. 

If you are questioning the balance in one of your close connections, it is time to run a simple emotional drain check. Here is how to figure out if you are only being used as a listener, and what you can do about it.

## The One-Way Text Audit

Look at the last twenty text conversations you’ve had with this person. Scroll up and visually map out the pattern of how your chats actually start and end. 

In a balanced friendship, people take turns initiating. One day you check in on them because you know they have a big presentation; the next day they text you a funny meme or ask how your weekend went. In a one-sided dynamic, the text history tells a glaringly different story. 

Count how many threads start with a greeting or a check-in versus threads that open immediately with a crisis: "Are you awake?", "I need to vent," or a giant paragraph detailing a fight they had with their partner. Notice what happens after you solve their problem or validate their feelings. Does the conversation naturally die until the next time they need something, or do they ask how you are holding up? If every single interaction requires you to drop into emergency-response mode, you aren't a friend in that moment—you are an emotional relief valve.

## The Topic Shift Test

Conversation is a tennis match. Someone serves an update or a thought, and the other person hits it back with a related comment before sharing their own perspective. 

When you are cast exclusively in the listener role, your matches look less like tennis and more like target practice. You are just standing there catching whatever is thrown at you. Pay attention to how your friend handles topic shifts. 

The next time you are talking, try sharing a personal win or a struggle of your own. For example, if they spend twenty minutes complaining about their boss, you might say, "I totally get that. I've been feeling totally overwhelmed by my workload lately, too." 

Watch how fast they pivot. A friend who values you will pause, validate your experience, and ask a follow-up question. A friend who only wants an audience will brush past your comment with a quick "Yeah, totally," and immediately follow it up with, "Anyway, another thing that happened to me today was..." If they treat your life updates like commercial breaks in the middle of their movie, you have your answer.

## Signs You've Become an Unpaid Therapist

It is completely normal to lean heavily on friends during major life events, like a divorce, a grief, or a sudden job loss. But when the crisis is chronic and everyday life is treated as an emergency, the dynamic becomes unsustainable. 

Here are a few concrete indicators that you have slipped out of friendship and into a support-staff role:
* You feel a physical sense of dread or exhaustion when you see their name pop up on your phone screen.
* You rehearse what you are going to say to them in advance, or censor your own good news to avoid triggering their jealousy or complaints.
* They explicitly tell you things like, "I only call you because you're the only one who really gets me," which feels like a compliment at first, but is actually a cage.
* They never ask for your specific advice; they just want your unconditional agreement that they are right and everyone else is wrong.

## How to Reset the Dynamic

Once you realize you are being used as a sounding board, you don't necessarily have to blow up the friendship overnight. Sometimes, people are genuinely clueless about how much they dominate the airwaves, and they will adjust if you set a clear boundary.

Start by shortening your availability. If you usually text back within two minutes, wait two hours. If they launch into a massive rant over text, reply with a supportive but brief boundary: "I'm so sorry you're dealing with this. I'm actually heading into a super busy afternoon and can't jump on a call right now, but I'm thinking of you." 

If you are hanging out in person and they dominate the first hour of conversation, gently interrupt them. Say something like, "I want to make sure we talk about this, but I also have some heavy stuff on my mind today that I could use a sounding board for." 

Their reaction to these small adjustments will tell you everything you need to know. A true friend will apologize for hogging the spotlight and eagerly ask about your life. Someone who only wants an audience will get defensive, pull away, or accuse you of being unsupportive.
