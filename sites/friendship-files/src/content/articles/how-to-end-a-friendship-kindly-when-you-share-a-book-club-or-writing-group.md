Leaving a friendship is hard enough when you only see each other one-on-one. But when your connection is tied to a shared creative space—like a monthly book club or a weekly critique circle—stepping away feels less like a simple goodbye and more like pulling a load-bearing beam out of a community you both rely on. 

I’ve been there. A few years ago, I needed to step back from a close friendship with someone who was also a core member of our four-person writing group. Every time I tried to picture the conversation, I panicked about what would happen to the critique group. Would it dissolve? Would it make our monthly meetings unbearably awkward? 

If you are staring down a similar crossroads, you know that handling this requires more than just a standard "we're growing apart" text. Here is the step-by-step approach I used to untangle the friendship without blowing up the group we both loved.

## Separate the Friendship from the Shared Space

Before you say a word to your friend, get clear on what is actually ending. Are you stepping away from the entire dynamic, or do you just no longer want a close, personal friendship with this individual? 

If you still want to be cordial and participate in the group alongside them, your strategy is different than if you need complete distance. In my case, the friendship had turned toxic—relying heavily on one-sided emotional venting that drained my creativity—so I knew I needed to step back from both the personal bond and the group itself. 

Map out your boundaries ahead of time. Knowing exactly what you want the future to look like makes the actual conversation much shorter and less prone to circular arguments.

## Have the Conversation Privately and Early

Do not wait until the night of the book club meeting to address this in the kitchen while everyone else is pouring wine. And definitely do not drop the news in the middle of a critique session. 

Reach out to your friend privately a few days before the next gathering. Ask to grab coffee or jump on a quick phone call. Keep the invitation neutral so they do not spend 48 hours spiraling. You can say something like, "Hey, I’d love to chat for ten minutes before our meeting next week just to catch up on things."

When you speak, focus the conversation entirely on the personal dynamic, not the group logistics. 

*   **Be direct about the shift:** "I’ve realized our friendship has shifted, and I don't feel like we’re connecting the way we used to."
*   **Avoid listing past grievances:** Bringing up a laundry list of past arguments invites a debate. Stick to the present state of the relationship.
*   **Own your perspective:** Use "I" statements. "I need to take some space from our one-on-one dynamic" lands much better than "You always make me feel exhausted."

## Address the Elephant in the Room: The Group

Once you’ve addressed the friendship, you immediately need to address the elephant in the room: what happens to the book club or writing group. This is where most people freeze up because they are terrified of collateral damage.

Take the pressure off your friend by offering a clear path forward for the group. If you are the one leaving, state your intentions clearly: "I know we both care a lot about this writing group. Because I need some personal space, I’ve decided to step down from the group so you can both continue to share your work comfortably."

If *they* are the ones who should theoretically leave, or if you want to take turns attending different months, you will need to negotiate that. But usually, if you initiated the friendship breakup, offering to exit the shared space is the kindest way to preserve their comfort and dignity.

## Communicate with the Rest of the Group Professionally

You do not need to broadcast the intricate details of your personal fallout to the other members of the book club. In fact, doing so usually makes everyone else deeply uncomfortable.

Send a brief, neutral message to the rest of the group a day or two after your private conversation. Keep it focused on logistics and appreciation. For example: "Hey everyone, I’m writing to let you know I’m stepping away from the book club starting next month. I’ve loved our discussions, but I need to clear some time in my schedule. I hope you all keep meeting and reading great books!"

If anyone presses you for details later, a simple boundary-setter works wonders: "We just realized we were heading in different directions personally, but I'm really glad the group is still going strong."

## Manage the Aftermath at Future Gatherings

If you aren't leaving the group—perhaps it's a massive ten-person book club where you can easily avoid each other—the dynamic will still feel strange for the first few meetings. 

Accept that there will be a transition period of awkwardness. Keep your interactions during group time polite, brief, and entirely focused on the book or the writing prompts. Do not try to force a fake cheerfulness, but do not ice them out in a way that forces other members to pick sides. Treat them the way you would treat a polite acquaintance in a professional setting. 

Navigating this kind of overlap requires a delicate balance of honesty and boundary-setting, but keeping the logistics clean prevents unnecessary fallout.
