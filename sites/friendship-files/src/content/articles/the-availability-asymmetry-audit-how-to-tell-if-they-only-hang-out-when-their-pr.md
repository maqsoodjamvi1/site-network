Have you ever noticed that a certain friend only seems to text you on a Friday afternoon around four o'clock? They ask what your weekend looks like, maybe suggest grabbing a drink, and act genuinely thrilled to see you. But if you try to reach out on a Tuesday morning to lock in plans for the following week, you hit a wall of vague replies or total silence. 

I spent a long time rationalizing this exact pattern. I told myself my friend was just "spontaneous," whereas I was "rigid." I assumed they hated planning ahead while I suffered from a control freak streak. But after tracking our social interactions over the course of three months, I realized the truth had nothing to do with personality types and everything to do with convenience. 

I call this the Availability Asymmetry Audit. It is a simple, slightly uncomfortable way to look at your friendships and figure out whether you are someone’s priority or just their backup plan when better options dry up.

## Map the Timeline of the Invitation

The easiest way to run this audit is to look at *when* you get invited out. Backup-friend dynamics operate on a very specific calendar. When someone views you as their primary circle, they lock in time with you days or weeks in advance because they actively want to secure your company. 

When you are the fallback option, your phone will start buzzing in predictable windows. Look back at your text history for the last ten hangouts. Do the invitations almost exclusively arrive between 4:00 PM and 7:00 PM on Thursday or Friday? Do they happen when a rainstorm ruins an outdoor concert they bought tickets for with someone else? 

If your social life together consists entirely of last-minute rescue missions for their empty weekends, take note. Spontaneous hangs are fun occasionally, but if *every single* interaction requires you to drop your current plans with zero notice just to fill a sudden hole in their schedule, you are acting as an emotional placeholder.

## Test the Future-Planning Boundary

To confirm your suspicions, you need to run a live experiment. The next time this friend reaches out for a last-minute Friday night get-together, do not say yes automatically. Instead, look at your actual calendar and try to pivot the interaction to a future date.

Say something like: "Ah, I’m already deep into a quiet night at home tonight and can't swing it, but I’d love to see you next Tuesday for coffee. Are you free then?"

Pay close attention to what happens next. A friend who genuinely wants to see *you* will express disappointment about missing you tonight and immediately try to pin down Tuesday, or at least offer a reasonable alternative. A person who only wants a warm body to occupy a vacant seat will usually do one of two things: they will ghost your counter-offer entirely, or they will reply with, "Ah, super busy next week, let's play it by ear!" 

If they consistently refuse to lock down future dates while still expecting you to jump when they snap their fingers on short notice, the asymmetry is clear.

## Audit the Cancellation Rate

Another major tell is how your plans dissolve. Everyone has emergencies, gets sick, or occasionally needs to bail because life gets overwhelming. But in a lopsided friendship, the cancellation direction always flows one way.

*   **You cancel:** They act distant, act put out, or make you feel guilty for changing plans, even with a week's notice.
*   **They cancel:** It always happens at the eleventh hour, usually via a text sent twenty minutes before you were supposed to meet. The excuses often sound vague ("something came up," "work ran late") yet they manage to post Instagram stories later that night hanging out at a trendy new restaurant with entirely different people.

When someone bails on you at the last minute because a "better" or more high-status invitation materialized, they are communicating their valuation of your time. They are banking on the fact that you are reliable, forgiving, and won't make a fuss. 

## Decide What to Do With the Data

Running this audit isn't about starting a dramatic confrontation or sending a long, angry text message breaking up with the friendship. In many cases, people who do this aren't cartoon villains; they are just deeply self-absorbed and lack social spatial awareness. They know you will always be there, so they put zero effort into maintaining the connection.

Once you have the data, you get to choose how to spend your own social energy. You can quietly match their level of investment—stop dropping your plans for them, stop being available at a moment's notice, and direct that free time toward people who actually put you on their calendar ahead of time.
