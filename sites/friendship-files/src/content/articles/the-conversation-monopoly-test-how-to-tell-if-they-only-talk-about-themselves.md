Have you ever hung out with a friend, gotten back to your car, and realized you spent two hours talking exclusively about their job drama, their dating life, or their apartment hunt? You replay the last ninety minutes in your head and remember thinking, *Wait, did they even ask how my surgery went last week?* 

When this happens once, it is easy to write off as a bad night. Maybe they are going through a genuinely rough patch. But when it happens every single time you get together, you are dealing with a conversation monopoly. 

Over the years, I have had to look at my own social circle and figure out which friendships are genuinely mutual and which ones are just unpaid therapy sessions where I hold the clipboard. To help you figure out where your relationships stand, I created a mental framework I call the Conversation Monopoly Test. Here is how to run it on your own friendships before you decide whether to pull back, speak up, or let the connection fade.

## The Three-Question Recall Check

The first step of the test takes place right after your next phone call or coffee date. Before you turn on the radio or start scrolling your phone, ask yourself three specific questions:

*   Did they ask at least two follow-up questions about something major happening in my life right now?
*   Did the conversation pivot back to them every time I tried to share a personal update?
*   If a stranger listened to our entire meetup, would they assume I was a close friend or a hired life coach?

If you find that you spent the entire time nodding, offering advice, and validating their feelings without ever getting past the surface of your own week, you are dealing with a pattern. Chronic monopolizers do not just talk a lot; they treat the friendship as a one-way street where they hold all the toll booths.

## The Interruption and Pivot Experiment

If you suspect a friend is a conversational hog, you do not have to guess. You can test it in real-time during your next hangout. It requires a tiny bit of bravery, but the results are always crystal clear.

Next time they ask you a question—say, "How was your weekend?"—give a brief, honest answer, and then watch what happens. For example, you might say, "It was actually pretty exhausting. I spent Saturday packing up my mom's house." 

A reciprocal friend will usually pause and lean in: "Oh wow, how did that go? Is she officially moved?" 

A conversation monopolizer, however, will use your sentence merely as a springboard to launch back into their own narrative. Within five seconds, their response will look like this: "Packing is the worst. When I moved last year, movers stole my TV. Speaking of which, I've been looking at apartments in your neighborhood..."

Notice how your heavy, personal topic was instantly erased to make room for their grievances. If this happens consistently, it is not an accident. It is their default operating mode.

## The Silent Treatment Test (The Low-Stakes Audit)

Another way to gauge the health of the dynamic is to look at your text message history. Scroll back through the last two weeks of chats on your phone. 

Look at the bubbles. Do you see long paragraphs of advice, listening ears, and emojis from you, paired with massive walls of text from them detailing their daily crises? Now, look for the moments where you shared a photo of your pet, a promotion at work, or a funny meme. Did they acknowledge it with a quick thumbs-up emoji and immediately change the subject back to themselves?

When someone only reaches out when they need an audience, your text thread starts to look like a monologue with occasional punctuation marks from you. A balanced friendship features a natural ebb and flow where both people take turns initiating plans, sharing mundane updates, and celebrating wins.

## Deciding What to Do Next

Realizing that a friend only cares about their own orbit is a heavy feeling. It forces you to make a choice. You can accept the friendship for what it is—realizing this person is an acquaintance who needs an audience rather than a true confidant—or you can step back and invest that emotional energy elsewhere. Before you make a final call on whether to downgrade the relationship or have an awkward, honest conversation about how one-sided things feel, take a moment to look at your options for setting healthier boundaries with high-maintenance friends.
