Sharing a physical office with an ex-partner is a specific kind of modern purgatory. Every time you grab your travel mug from the breakroom or hear a familiar laugh down the hallway, your body reacts as if a mild alarm bell is ringing. Now, layer on top of that the very normal adult desire to expand your social circle, grab weekend drinks, or find someone to complain to about your workload. 

When your past personal life is permanently on display near the water cooler, making new friends feels risky. You worry about office gossip, awkward run-ins, and whether a new friend will accidentally bring up your ex in casual conversation. 

If you are trying to build a fresh social life while tethered to a ghost from your romantic past at work, you need a strategy. Here is how I navigated the exact same messy dynamic and successfully built a new circle of friends without turning my office into a daytime soap opera.

## Quarantine Your Social Life From the Building

The golden rule of making friends when you work with an ex is that your workplace cannot be your primary fishing pond. In an ideal world, you might bond with a cubicle neighbor over a shared hatred of payroll software. But when your ex is three rows over, inviting a coworker out for post-work beers comes with a dangerous side effect: proximity.

Instead, look outward immediately. If you want to make genuine friends who know nothing about your dating history, you have to leave the zip code of your employer. 

*   **Choose third spaces outside your neighborhood:** Pick a gym, a pottery studio, or a trivia night at least twenty minutes away from your office. This ensures you will not accidentally bump into your ex or your ex's curious coworkers on a Tuesday night.
*   **Use activity-based groups over networking mixers:** Board game nights, running clubs, and cooking classes give you something to do with your hands and eyes so you do not have to perform forced small talk.
*   **Keep your work address private initially:** When meeting new people at local hobby groups, default to talking about where you live rather than where you work. You do not need to lie, just pivot the conversation to your neighborhood, local coffee shops, or weekend hiking trails.

## Craft a Neutral Script for the Inevitable Questions

At some point, a new friend is going to ask, "So, what do you do all day?" You are going to have to name your company. When you do, your new friend might eventually ask how you like your job, and your ex might occasionally come up if you need to explain a weird work anecdote. 

You need a boring, deflating script that kills curiosity instantly. Gossip thrives on mystery and emotion. If you respond with clinical boredom, people lose interest immediately.

When an acquaintance asks about my company dynamic, I learned to say: "Oh, it's a small place, but we're in completely different departments, so our paths barely cross." It is technically true, entirely uninteresting, and completely closes the door on follow-up questions about our breakup, who got the apartment, or why things ended. Practice saying this out loud in front of your bathroom mirror until your voice sounds as flat as a weather report. 

## Set Boundaries With Coworkers Who Pick Sides

The hardest part of making friends when your ex is in the office is dealing with the collateral damage: office politics. Well-meaning coworkers will try to act as emotional buffers, carrying messages, offering sympathetic looks in the hallway, or subtly fishing for details. 

To make real friends inside or outside the office, you have to drop the drama. If a coworker tries to gossip about what your ex was doing at the Friday company lunch, do not take the bait. 

*   Change the subject immediately to a mutual work project or a mundane topic like weekend weather.
*   Decline invitations to happy hours where you know your ex will be present and drama is guaranteed to brew.
*   Seek out colleagues in entirely different divisions—like accounting or IT—who have zero social overlap with your ex's department.

## Reclaim Your Professional Identity

Often, the hesitation to make new friends stems from feeling small or defined by a breakup. When you see your ex every day, it is easy to feel like everyone in the building is watching you through the lens of that failed relationship. 

To break this psychological loop, invest heavily in your individual competence at work. Speak up in cross-departmental meetings. Volunteer for a presentation. When you build a reputation based on your actual skills rather than your romantic history, you shift how people see you. This confidence naturally spills over into your social life, making it much easier to approach strangers at social clubs or strike up conversations with people in your apartment building.

Navigating this transition takes time, and some days will feel more awkward than others. Focus on small, consistent steps outside your normal routine to build a world that exists entirely separate from your 9-to-5.
