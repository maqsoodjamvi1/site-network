Have you ever looked at your phone, saw a specific person’s name pop up, and felt an immediate knot in your stomach? You already know what is coming. They aren't calling to see how your weekend went or to invite you out for coffee. They are calling because they need something. Maybe they are moving this Saturday, need a ride to the airport at 5:00 AM, or want you to proofread their resume. 

I learned this the hard way with a friend named Marcus. For two years, I helped him pack up apartments, lent him tools I never got back, and listened to him vent about his love life for hours. But the moment I asked him to help me pick up a second-hand bookshelf from Facebook Marketplace—offering pizza and gas money, mind you—he suddenly had a scheduling conflict. 

That was the day I invented what I call the Favor Bank Balance Test. It is a practical, no-nonsense way to figure out if you are dealing with a reciprocal friendship or if you are simply unpaid labor for someone else's convenience. 

## Audit the Last Five Interactions

To run the Favor Bank Balance Test, open your text message history with this person and scroll back through the last five times they reached out to you. Do not guess or rely on your general impression; look at the actual text thread. 

What initiated those conversations? Were three or more of those texts requests for a favor, a complaint that required your emotional labor, or a last-minute ask for a massive convenience? If the ratio is heavily skewed toward them needing an assist, your Favor Bank account is deeply in the red. 

In a healthy friendship, the ledger balances out over time. One week I help you move; a few months later, you help me paint a room. But when one person is perpetually the lender of time, money, and energy, and the other is a permanent borrower who never pays interest, the dynamic turns toxic. 

## Run the Unannounced "No" Experiment

If your text audit leaves you suspicious, you need to test the boundary. The next time this person asks you for a favor, use the magic word: "No." 

You do not need to invent an elaborate, dramatic excuse or lie about having a prior commitment. A simple, polite decline is the ultimate diagnostic tool. Try saying, "I can't make it work this weekend," or "I'm keeping my schedule really light right now so I won't be able to help with that."

Pay close attention to what happens next. A genuine friend might be slightly disappointed, but they will bounce back quickly, say "No worries at all," and continue talking to you about other things. They value your presence, not just your utility.

On the flip side, someone who only uses you for favors will react very differently. They might try to guilt-trip you, act coldly, stop responding to your messages for a week, or immediately end the conversation once they realize you aren't going to solve their problem. Their reaction will tell you everything you need to know about what they actually value in the relationship.

## Shift to Low-Effort, High-Reward Invitations

Sometimes people fall into a one-sided rut out of sheer laziness rather than malice. To see if they are capable of being a real friend, try shifting the dynamic entirely. Stop offering heavy favors, and stop being available for midnight crisis calls. Instead, extend low-stakes, social invitations.

Text them and say, "Hey, I'm grabbing tacos on Thursday at 7 PM, want to join?" or "I'm going for a walk at the park tomorrow afternoon if you want to tag along." 

Notice two things about their response: do they show up, and do they try to initiate plans back? If they consistently ghost your casual hangout invites but still text you out of the blue when their car breaks down, you have your answer. They aren't looking for companionship; they are looking for an emergency contact.

## Deciding What to Do With Your Findings

Realizing that someone only values you for what you can do for them stings. It makes you question your own judgment and feel foolish for being so accommodating. But identifying the truth gives you back your power. You get to decide how much of your precious free time and energy you want to invest going forward. You can choose to slowly step back, stop picking up every call, and redirect that saved energy toward people who show up for you just as much as you show up for them. 

As you weigh how to handle this dynamic and figure out your next steps, it helps to look at your broader social circle to ensure your other relationships are built on genuine mutual support.
