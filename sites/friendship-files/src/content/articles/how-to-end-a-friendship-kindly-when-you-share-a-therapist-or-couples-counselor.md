Realizing a friendship has run its course is heavy enough. But when you share a therapist or a couples counselor with that person, the emotional math changes entirely. Suddenly, you aren't just managing your own grief or discomfort; you are worrying about collateral damage in a professional space that is supposed to be your safe haven. 

I’ve lived through this exact awkwardness. A few years ago, I had to step away from a very close friendship with someone who recommended my individual therapist to me. For months, I stayed in the friendship long past its expiration date simply because I was terrified of walking into my therapist’s office and feeling like the air had changed. 

If you are trying to figure out how to untangle yourself from a friend when your mental health provider is the common denominator, here is a practical, step-by-step way to handle it without blowing up your own support system.

## Check in with your therapist first

Before you say a single word to your friend, book a session with your shared counselor. You do not need to ask for their permission to end a friendship, but you do need to give them a heads-up so they can prepare for the clinical implications. 

When you sit down in that office or log onto Zoom, be direct: "I need to step back from my friendship with [Name], and since you see both of us, I wanted to discuss how we manage confidentiality and scheduling moving forward."

A good therapist will appreciate this transparency. They will review their dual-relationship policies with you, remind you of confidentiality boundaries (they cannot discuss you with your ex-friend, and vice versa), and help you game-plan your exit strategy. If your therapist acts weird about it, gets defensive, or makes you feel guilty, that is a massive red flag about the therapist—not you.

## Decide if you need to name the shared provider

When you finally have the conversation with your friend, keep it as simple as possible. You do not owe them a 10-point presentation on why you are drifting apart. In fact, the less ammunition you provide, the smoother the separation will be.

However, you *do* need to decide whether to bring up the shared therapist during the breakup talk. Ask yourself: is your friend likely to weaponize the therapist, call the office in a panic, or accuse you of gossiping about them in session? 

If the answer is yes, or if it simply feels too complicated to untangle, leave the therapist entirely out of it. Stick to standard friendship-ending language. 

*   "I’ve been feeling like our lives are moving in different directions, and I don't have the bandwidth to maintain this friendship right now."
*   "I need to take some space from our dynamic. I wish you the best, but I don't think we are a good fit as friends anymore."

If your friend presses you about who you are going to talk to now, or tries to make it about your mutual circles, hold your boundary. You don't have to justify growing apart.

## Protect your scheduling buffer

Logistics are usually where shared-therapist breakups get messy. If you and your friend used to compare notes about your appointments, or if you regularly ran into each other in the waiting room, you need to change your routine immediately.

*   Ask your therapist to shift your appointment day or time if you suspect you might accidentally cross paths in the waiting room.
*   If your therapist offers virtual sessions, switch to telehealth for a few months to eliminate the physical awkwardness of the lobby.
*   Block your ex-friend on calendar apps, shared task boards, or any wellness apps where your schedules might accidentally overlap.
*   If you share a group chat with other mutual friends, mute it for a while so you don't have to watch your ex-friend casually mention their upcoming session time.

## Keep your counseling sessions strictly about you

Once the friendship is officially over, the temptation in therapy will be to spend the entire hour venting about how unfair your ex-friend is being, or obsessing over what they might be saying in *their* sessions. Fight this urge. 

Your therapist cannot and will not confirm whether your ex-friend is talking about you. Trying to read between the lines of your therapist's neutral expressions will drive you crazy. Reclaim that hour of your life. Use your time to focus on your healing, your patterns, and your future—not on the post-mortem of a dead friendship. 

Ending a friendship when your worlds are intertwined takes courage, but you do not have to hold your breath and stay stuck in a toxic dynamic just to keep the peace in someone else's waiting room.
