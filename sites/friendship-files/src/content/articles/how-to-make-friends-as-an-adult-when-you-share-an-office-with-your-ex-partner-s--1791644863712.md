If your morning commute involves walking past your ex’s new spouse’s desk on the way to yours, congratulations: you are playing adult friendship-making on the absolute hardest difficulty setting. When my company merged with my ex-husband's firm last year, I found myself sharing a cramped open-plan office space with him and the woman he left me for. Finding friends in a new city was already daunting, but making connections when my professional and personal baggage was on daily display felt completely impossible. 

Yet, I managed to build a solid, supportive local network without having to quit my job or spend my lunch breaks hiding in the supply closet. If you are stuck in a similar professional purgatory, here is how you can navigate the delicate art of making friends as an adult when your daily environment is this complicated.

## Separate Your Office Persona from Your Social Life

The biggest mistake I made in the first three months of this nightmare was trying to find friends inside the office. When your ex and their new spouse are twenty feet away, every conversation you have with a coworker gets filtered through that awkward lens. People gossip, walls are thin, and office friendships can quickly turn into high-stakes political games. 

To protect your peace, draw a hard geographical line. Treat your office strictly as a place to earn a paycheck, and treat your social life as something that happens entirely outside of it. 

*   Do not attend happy hours held within walking distance of the building.
*   Avoid confiding in cube-mates about your personal life, no matter how trustworthy they seem.
*   Dedicate your lunch hour to leaving the premises entirely—go to a nearby park, a library, or a coffee shop three blocks over.

By keeping your workplace isolated, you ensure that your budding friendships are built on who you are, not on office drama or shared morbid curiosity about your dating history.

## Mine Niche Hobbies Where Your Ex Doesn't Exist

When you work with reminders of your past life, you need hobbies that give you a totally clean slate. This means stepping away from general social mixers and leaning into hyper-specific, skill-based communities. 

Instead of going to a generic "New in Town" meetup, I signed up for a Friday night ceramics class at a studio across town. Because it was physically distant from my office neighborhood, there was zero chance of running into anyone from work. More importantly, focusing on centering clay on a wheel requires so much brainpower that I literally did not have the cognitive bandwidth to think about my ex or his wife for two hours. 

Look for activities where participation requires concentration: pottery, rock climbing, foreign language conversation circles, or community theater tech crews. These environments force you to talk to people about a shared external task rather than small talk about where you live or what you do for a living, which takes the pressure off.

## Use Proximity and Frequency to Your Advantage

In adult friendship, consistency is the cheat code. Psychologists call this the mere-exposure effect: people tend to develop a preference for things simply because they are familiar with them. 

Instead of trying to make a lifelong best friend immediately, pick one low-stakes setting and show up at the exact same time every week. For me, it was a neighborhood run club on Tuesday nights and a specific corner table at the public library on Saturday mornings. 

By seeing the same people week after week without the pressure of a formal "date," casual nods turn into quick chats, and quick chats eventually turn into grabbing a pastry after the run or studying together. You don't need to explain your office situation to these people; you just need to be a familiar, reliable presence in a space that has nothing to do with your day job.

## Manage the Inevitable Awkwardness Head-On

At some point, an acquaintance from your new hobby or neighborhood might ask, "So, what do you do for work?" 

When you share an office with your ex's new partner, the temptation to overshare or launch into a defensive monologue is high. Resist this urge. Have a boring, rehearsed pivot ready to go. 

I mastered this one-liner: "I work in corporate compliance downtown—it’s pretty standard desk work, but I love my neighborhood." If they push further about the commute or the area, just redirect the conversation back to them or to the current setting. People rarely want to dig into workplace drama; they are usually just making polite conversation. Keeping your answer brief and uninteresting protects your privacy and keeps the focus on building a normal, healthy dynamic.

Getting through a workday when your personal history is sitting across the aisle takes an immense amount of emotional energy. Protecting your downtime and looking outward into entirely separate communities is the only way to build a fresh social circle that belongs strictly to you. 

As you start taking steps to expand your circle outside of your professional bubble, having the right guidance can help you navigate awkward social dynamics and build genuinely lasting connections.
