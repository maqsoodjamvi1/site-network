Drifting away from a friend is tough under any circumstances, but the tension skyrockets when your financial lives are intertwined. A few years ago, I found myself in this exact position. My close friend of six years and I had started using the same wealth manager—a referral from her, actually—because we both valued the same boutique firm's approach. When our friendship dissolved due to fundamental differences in our life goals, the looming shadow of our quarterly portfolio reviews made a simple fade-out impossible. 

If you are sharing a financial advisor, wealth manager, or even an accountant with a friend you need to step back from, you cannot just block their number and hope for the best. Money is deeply personal, and a shared professional team creates accidental logistics that require careful handling. Here is how I navigated it, step by step, so you can preserve your sanity and your investments.

## Review Your Account Structure and Agreements First

Before you have any awkward conversations or contact your advisor, log into your accounts and look at the paperwork. Are your accounts entirely separate, or are there any joint investments, shared trusts, or co-signed assets? 

In my case, my friend and I had completely individual accounts, meaning my money was legally untouched by our falling out. However, we were both listed as referral credits for each other, and our wealth manager handled our annual client appreciation dinners together. If your assets are entirely independent, you have total freedom to make a change without affecting the other person's actual money. If you do share any financial entities, you will need to untangle those legally before you address the interpersonal side. Knowing your exact account status gives you the confidence to act without guessing.

## Decide Whether You Need to Change Advisors

The burning question you are probably facing is whether you *have* to find a new financial planner just because you are ending the friendship. The short answer is: not necessarily, but it requires boundaries.

Wealth managers are bound by strict fiduciary duty and privacy laws. They cannot legally share details about your portfolio, net worth, or meeting schedules with your former friend. If you love your advisor's investment strategy and do not want the hassle of moving your funds, you can stay. 

However, you need to call your advisor privately and give them a heads-up. You do not need to bash your former friend or air dirty laundry. A simple, professional script works best: *"I want to let you know that [Friend's Name] and I are no longer in touch. I value our working relationship and want to continue as your client, but please ensure our scheduling and communications are kept entirely separate moving forward."* A good advisor will be discreet, professional, and entirely unfazed by this request; they see interpersonal drama among clients all the time.

## Move Your Accounts If the Shared History Is Too Heavy

Even if privacy laws protect you, staying with the same advisor might still feel emotionally exhausting. If seeing your ex-friend’s name on the holiday party guest list or knowing your advisor has lunch with both of you gives you a knot in your stomach, it is time to switch firms.

Moving your money to a new wealth manager is much easier than most people think—your new firm usually handles the transfer paperwork directly. When you notify your current advisor that you are leaving, you can keep the exit reason entirely focused on your own evolving financial needs. You do not need to weaponize your departure or give your former friend any ammunition to claim you are acting out of spite. 

## Communicate the Separation Without Mentioning the Advisor

If your former friend reaches out to schedule a joint meeting, or if you know you need to initiate the breakup of the friendship, keep the wealth manager entirely out of the initial conversation. Bringing up investments or shared professionals during a friendship breakup just muddies the waters and introduces unnecessary logistical anxiety.

* Focus the conversation entirely on the friendship dynamic, not financial logistics.
* Keep your language concise and definitive so there is no room for negotiation.
* Avoid meeting in public spaces where you might run into the advisor or office staff.
* Do not use the shared financial ties as an excuse to delay ending the friendship.

When I finally stepped back from my friendship, I kept my text message brief: *"I've realized our lives are heading in different directions, and I think it's best we step back from our friendship."* I handled the professional separation with our wealth manager a week later, entirely separate from the personal text. This kept the boundaries clean.

## Prepare for the Inevitable Overlap

Even with the best planning, small overlaps might happen. Your advisor might accidentally mention an upcoming charity event you used to attend together, or you might spot your former friend's car in the firm's parking lot on review day. 

Accept these moments as minor bumps rather than major crises. Take a breath, remind yourself that your financial security is entirely yours, and let the coincidence pass without comment. By separating your personal boundaries from your professional financial team, you take back control of your peace of mind.
