Moving in with a partner is a huge milestone, but the living situation gets a lot more complex if your partner still shares a house with their ex. Maybe they are waiting out a lease, navigating a slow real estate market, or co-parenting under one roof while things get sorted. Whatever the reason, you are in this unique dynamic. 

Now, add adult friendship into the mix. When you are trying to build your own social circle, bringing new people home can feel awkward, restricted, or downright stressful. How do you invite a new work friend over for coffee when your partner's ex is walking around in the kitchen? 

Making friends as an adult is already hard enough without adding domestic complications. Here is how I navigated building a local social life while living in a house that still included my partner’s past.

## Take the Action Outside Your Front Door

The most practical shift you can make right away is to stop trying to host people at your house in the early stages of friendship. When you live with your partner's ex, your home is a high-sensitivity zone. Even if everyone is polite, the vibe is rarely relaxed enough for a casual hang-out with someone you barely know.

Instead, shift your default setting to "out of the house." 

*   **Claim neutral territory:** Suggest coffee shops, neighborhood parks, local libraries, or casual restaurants for your first few meetups. 
*   **Embrace activity-based hangouts:** Go to a pottery class, join a running group, or try a trivia night at a local pub. These settings take the pressure off making forced small talk and keep the focus on a shared task.
*   **Protect your sanctuary:** Until your living situation changes, treat your home strictly as a place for you and your partner, and keep your nascent friendships in the public sphere. You do not need to explain your living arrangements to a casual acquaintance over a first cup of coffee.

## Vet Your New Friends Before Over-Sharing

When your living situation is unconventional, it is easy to over-compensate by telling new people everything right away. You might feel the urge to clear the air immediately: "Just so you know, my partner's ex lives with us." 

Pause. Over-sharing can overwhelm a brand-new connection. People you have just met at a dog park or a book club are looking for low-stakes fun, not an intensive emotional processing session. 

Keep initial conversations focused on shared interests, local recommendations, and light banter. As the friendship deepens over a few months, you can share more about your personal life. When you eventually do tell them about your housing dynamic, choose someone who has proven they are trustworthy and non-judgmental. If they react with drama or gossip, they are an acquaintance, not a close friend material anyway.

## Build Autonomous Friendships That Don’t Depend on Your Partner

It is very common to lean on your partner's existing social circle when you move to a new place. But when your partner's ex is part of that background, hanging out with mutual friends can feel like walking through a minefield of shared history, inside jokes, and lingering awkwardness.

You need friendships that belong entirely to you. Make a point to seek out people your partner has no connection to. 

Look for local groups that cater to your specific hobbies. If you love board games, find a local game store night. If you love hiking, join a regional trail club. When you meet someone new through these avenues, the dynamic is fresh. There is no baggage, no history, and no mention of the past unless you choose to bring it up. This gives you a mental break from your domestic reality and lets you just be yourself.

## Manage Your Time and Emotional Energy

Living in a house with your partner's ex takes a toll on your emotional bandwidth. You might find yourself constantly reading the room, managing your own discomfort, or dealing with subtle tensions. 

Because of this, you might not have the energy for a high-volume social life. That is completely normal. 

Quality matters far more than quantity. Instead of trying to join five different clubs and make ten new best friends, focus on nurturing one or two solid connections. A single, low-key coffee date every week with someone who makes you laugh can do wonders for your mental health. Give yourself permission to decline social invitations when your social battery is drained by your home life. True friends will understand if you need to take a rain check.

Navigating an unusual living arrangement while trying to build a community takes patience and strong boundaries. By keeping your early interactions outside the house, protecting your privacy, and focusing on independent hobbies, you can build a thriving social life on your own terms. As you think about your next steps and how to reach out to potential new friends this week, consider what kind of local activity or group fits best into your current routine.
