If you have been scrolling through interior design feeds lately, you have probably noticed those moody, tactile, old-world plaster walls everywhere. Real Venetian plaster looks incredible, but hiring a specialist to skim-coat a room can easily cost thousands of dollars. Even buying specialty lime-based paint kits online usually sets you back at least $80 to $120 a gallon. 

A few months ago, I wanted that exact high-end, cloudy texture for my home office accent wall, but I refused to spend a fortune on a room that mostly just sees my desk chair. After some experimenting with leftover supplies in my garage, I figured out a dead-simple mixture using standard interior flat latex paint and joint compound. The whole project cost me about $15, and honestly, guests ask me which artisan I hired every time they visit. 

Here is exactly how I pulled it off, and how you can do it this weekend.

## Gather Your Supplies

Before you start slathering mud on your drywall, you need to make sure your toolkit is ready. The beauty of this hack is that you probably already own half of these items if you have ever hung a picture frame or patched a hole.

*   **One tub of lightweight joint compound (drywall mud):** Get the premixed lightweight version in the green or blue tub. Heavyweight mud is too dense and harder to sand down if you make a mistake.
*   **A quart of flat interior latex paint:** Choose your base color. I used a soft greige (gray-beige), but a warm off-white or a deep olive green looks stunning. Make sure it is completely flat (matte); eggshell or satin will look too shiny and ruin the plaster illusion.
*   **Mixing tools:** A clean five-gallon bucket and a large kitchen spoon or a drill paint mixer attachment.
*   **Application tools:** A 6-inch or 8-inch drywall taping knife and a damp microfiber cloth.
*   **Finishing supplies:** 220-grit sandpaper and a good quality clear interior matte sealer.

## Mix Your DIY "Plaster"

The secret to getting that subtle, cloud-like movement on the wall is consistency. If your mixture is too thick, you will leave heavy ridges that look like bad drywall patching. If it is too thin, it will just look like normal paint.

Scoop about three large trowels of joint compound into your empty bucket. Pour in about a cup of your flat latex paint. Mix them thoroughly. You are looking for the consistency of thick Greek yogurt or soft-serve ice cream. If it feels stiff and pulls away from the spoon, add a tiny splash of water. If it is runny and drips off the spoon instantly, add a bit more joint compound. 

The paint tints the mud and slows down its drying time just enough, giving you a longer working window so you don't end up with harsh lap lines where sections dried at different rates.

## Apply the Mud Using the "Feathering" Technique

Clear everything off the wall, lay down a drop cloth, and wipe the drywall clean with a damp rag. You do not need to prime bare drywall if it has already been painted, but if you are patching over raw builder's flat paint, you are good to go.

Dip the edge of your drywall knife into the mixture. You only need a very small amount on the blade. Start in one upper corner of the wall and sweep the knife across the surface in a wide, arcing motion, holding the blade at a very flat angle against the wall. 

The goal here is not to cover the wall completely solid. You want the old wall color to peek through in small patches. Apply strokes in alternating directions—some going up and down, some diagonally. This cross-hatching technique creates the depth and visual movement that makes limewash and plaster look authentic. 

Don't panic if it looks messy while it's wet. Joint compound shrinks slightly as it dries, and you will have a chance to soften any harsh edges in the next step.

## Sand, Soften, and Seal

Let the wall dry overnight. Because you applied the mixture in thin, sweeping layers, it shouldn't take long to cure. Turn off your overhead lights and shine a flashlight parallel to the wall—this is called "raking light," and it will highlight any sharp ridges or trowel marks that need attention.

Take your 220-grit sandpaper and lightly buff the entire surface. You are not trying to sand the wall flat; you are simply knocking down any sharp little peaks and ridges so the wall feels smooth and suede-like to the touch. Wipe away the dust with a dry microfiber cloth or a soft brush.

Finally, because joint compound is porous and water-soluble, you need to seal it. Roll on a coat of clear, ultra-matte interior polyurethane or a specialty flat masonry sealer. This step is non-negotiable if your wall is in a high-traffic area, as it prevents scuffs and allows you to wipe down the surface later.

Taking on a textured wall treatment yourself can feel intimidating when you look at high-end design magazines, but drywall mud is surprisingly forgiving. If you mess up a section, you can just scrape it off with your knife while wet or sand it down later and try again. Before you buy expensive specialty coatings for your next room refresh, consider trying out this budget-friendly method.
