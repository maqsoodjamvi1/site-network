If you have ever thrifted a mirror or bought a cheap, lightweight wall accent from a big-box store, you probably know the pain of the glossy plastic frame. They usually come in a shiny, unconvincing champagne silver or a flat bronze that looks decidedly like painted resin up close. I have dragged home dozens of these over the years, usually because the glass shape was gorgeous, but the frame looked like it belonged in a college dorm. 

Spray paint is the usual fix, but it comes with a mess. You have to tape off the glass, drag everything outside, worry about weather humidity, and deal with runny drips. That is why I stopped using spray paint for small decor items and switched entirely to a little metal tube called Rub 'n Buff. It is a wax-based metallic finish that you apply with your fingers, and it completely tricks the eye into thinking a plastic piece is solid, heavy antique brass.

## What You Need to Get Started

Before you touch your mirror, you want to gather a few basic supplies. The beauty of this method is that you do not need a garage, a drop cloth, or fancy brushes. 

*   **The mirror:** Look for molded plastic, resin, or even wood frames with intricate details like beading, acanthus leaves, or vintage scrolls. The more texture the frame has, the better this technique works.
*   **Rub 'n Buff in European Gold or Antique Gold:** European Gold is a bright, warm yellow gold, while Antique Gold has more of a green-brown undertone. I almost always use Antique Gold because it looks instantly aged, but you can mix them on a paper plate if you want a custom shade.
*   **A damp microfiber cloth and mild dish soap:** For cleaning off decades of thrift store grime or factory dust.
*   **Blue painter’s tape:** Even though this method is precise, a little tape on the mirror glass saves you from scraping stray wax later.
*   **An old t-shirt scrap or a clean rag:** You can use your bare finger (and I often do), but a scrap of cotton fabric gives you a bit more control when buffing.

## Preparing the Frame

Skip the sandpaper. One of the biggest mistakes beginners make with Rub 'n Buff is treating it like traditional paint. You do not need to prime plastic frames, and you definitely do not need to sand them down. In fact, the slight sheen on cheap plastic actually helps the wax grip the surface.

Start by giving your mirror a thorough scrub. Wash the frame with warm soapy water to remove any greasy fingerprints or oils, then dry it completely. If your mirror has a cardboard backing that looks fragile, tape over the edges so water doesn't seep inside while you clean. 

Next, run a strip of painter's tape along the inner edge of the frame where it meets the glass. Press it down firmly with your thumbnail. While you can scrape off stray dots of Rub 'n Buff with a razor blade later, saving yourself the cleanup step makes the project much more satisfying. Lay the mirror flat on a table covered with an old newspaper or a brown paper grocery bag. 

## The Technique: Less Is More

Squeeze out an amount of Rub 'n Buff no larger than a grain of rice onto your rag or your index finger. Seriously, that is all you need for a surprisingly large surface area. This product is heavily pigmented, and the most common error is globbing it on like frosting. If you use too much, it gets gummy and fills in the delicate crevices of the frame, hiding the very details you want to highlight.

Rub the small dab of wax onto the frame using a circular motion. You will immediately notice it looking streaky and dull. Do not panic. The magic happens in the second step: the buffing. 

After you have worked the wax into a section of the frame, take a clean part of your rag (or a clean finger) and rub the surface firmly. The friction melts the wax slightly and buffs it into a brilliant, metallic luster. It stops looking like a craft project and starts looking like gilded metal right before your eyes. 

## Layering for an Authentic Antique Look

To make cheap plastic look like a heavy, time-worn antique, you want to lean into the imperfections of the frame. Real antique gold leaf wears down unevenly over decades, showing darker shadows underneath. 

I like to leave a hint of the original black or brown plastic showing through in the deepest recesses of the carved details. Do not try to get 100% solid coverage on your first pass. Instead, apply a very light coat everywhere, buff it to a shine, and then go back with a tiny dab on your fingertip to highlight the highest raised edges of the frame. 

If your mirror frame is bright white or silver underneath, you can dab a tiny bit of dark brown acrylic paint into the deepest corners first, let it dry for five minutes, and then apply your Rub 'n Buff over the top. This creates instant faux-patina that tricks anyone who walks past it. 

Once you finish buffing the entire frame, let the piece sit undisturbed for a few hours. The wax cures and hardens slightly over 24 hours. When it is fully dry, it won't rub off on your hands when you dust it. 

Taking on a project like this is one of the easiest ways to customize your space without spending a fortune on high-end decor, and it opens up a lot of possibilities the next time you spot a cheap piece at a garage sale.
