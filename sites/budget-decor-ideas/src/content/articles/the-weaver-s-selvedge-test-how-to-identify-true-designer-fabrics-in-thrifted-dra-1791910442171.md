I used to make a living rescuing dusty pinch-pleat drapes from estate sales and heavy, fringed armchairs from the back of charity barns. In the beginning, I lost money on yardage that felt amazing in the dim church basement sale, only to realize it was cheap rayon blended with polyester that disintegrated the second it hit my washing machine. 

After years of cutting up discarded textiles for custom pillows and slipcovers, I learned the one trade secret that commercial interior designers rely on before they ever check a label: the selvedge test. High-end textile mills leave distinct fingerprints on the outer edges of their fabrics. If you know how to read them, you can spot thousand-yards-a-roll designer upholstery in a pile of bargain bin linens.

## What Is a Selvedge and Why Does It Matter?

The selvedge is the tightly woven, finished edge of a bolt of fabric that prevents it from unraveling during manufacturing. On cheap mass-market textiles, the selvedge is usually a narrow, sloppy line of white threads. On high-end home decor fabrics—think Schumacher, Kravet, or Lee Jofa—the selvedge is a miniature work of art and information. 

When mills weave heavy-duty jacquards, Belgium linens, or cut-velvet upholstery, they invest heavily in the outer edge because it has to withstand high tension on industrial looms. Next time you are hunting through thrift store curtains, ignore the front pattern for a minute. Pull the fabric apart at the hem and look for the raw, finished vertical edge. 

## Reading the Secret Codes on the Margin

High-end designer fabrics almost always stamp or weave specific markers directly into the selvedge. Mass-market manufacturers rarely bother with this because their supply chains are too fast and cheap. 

Look for these specific details along the outer white margin:
*   **Color registration dots:** A series of tiny colored circles that correspond to the inks or dyes used in the pattern. If you see a precise rainbow of dots, it means the fabric was hand-screen-printed or block-printed, rather than digitally printed on an inkjet machine.
*   **Mill names and collection titles:** Many luxury European and American houses print their brand name, pattern name, and country of origin right on the edge every 36 inches. 
*   **Fiber content breakdowns:** While consumer laws require tags on finished products, designer bolts weave the exact percentage of raw materials (like 55% linen, 45% cotton) right into the weave itself.
*   **Copyright symbols and lot numbers:** Commercial upholstery must track dye lots so large hotels or interior designers can match colors across different rolls. A stamped lot number is a dead giveaway that you are holding trade-only merchandise.

## The Weight and Drape Check

Once you spot a promising selvedge, grab a handful of the fabric and let it drop. True designer upholstery and drapery have a specific heft to them. 

Mass-market curtains are often weighted down with cheap synthetic sizing to make them feel heavy on the shelf, but that stiffness washes right out. Designer textiles get their weight from dense, high-count natural fibers. A vintage French cotton-blend damask should feel substantial, cool to the touch, and drape in deep, heavy folds rather than crinkling up like paper. 

If you are looking at velvet, run your palm against the grain. Cheap rayon velvets feel slick and plastic-y. True mohair or heavy cotton-velvet upholstery velvet feels dense, rich, and bounces back immediately when you press your fingers into the pile. 

## Testing Fiber Burn and Pilling

If you are really unsure about a piece of thrifted fabric and there are a few stray threads at the hem, I always carry a small lighter in my thrifting bag. Pulling a single vertical thread and burning it on a concrete floor will tell you the truth instantly. 

Natural fibers like linen and cotton burn with a bright orange flame, smell like burning paper, and turn into a fine gray ash. Synthetics like polyester and nylon melt into a hard, chemical-smelling plastic bead. If you find a pair of thrifted drapes labeled as "pure linen" that melt into hard plastic, you just saved yourself twenty dollars of regret.

Hunting for high-end textiles in secondhand shops is half detective work and half patience. Once you train your eye to bypass the polyester prints and spot the telltale signs of a professional mill finish on the selvedge, your home decor game changes completely.
