Thrifting a solid wood dresser or a mid-century side table is one of the most rewarding parts of home decorating. You get character, history, and often better construction than what is sold in big-box stores today. However, the thrill of the hunt comes with a major trap: the painted-over veneer. 

Many well-meaning DIYers paint over damaged wood veneer to hide chips, lifting edges, or water rings. When you find a piece covered in a thick, trendy coat of matte black or sage green paint, it is easy to assume you are scoring a solid hardwood antique underneath. Sometimes you are, but often you are buying a fragile particle board core wrapped in a paper-thin layer of wood that will splinter the moment you try to strip it down to the natural grain.

Over years of haunting estate sales, garage sales, and thrift shops, I developed a simple physical routine to determine what is really hiding under a painted or dark-stained surface. Here is the exact method I use to inspect a finish before I ever hand over my cash or haul a piece home.

## Check the Grain Continuity Across Planes

The single biggest telltale sign of veneer versus solid wood is how the grain behaves when it turns a corner. On a piece of solid wood, the grain pattern on the top surface will wrap around the edge and continue down the side, because the entire piece was cut from the same board. 

Veneer does not work this way. Veneer is a very thin slice of decorative wood glued over a cheaper secondary wood, like plywood, pine, or MDF. Manufacturers apply veneer to the top panel, then apply a separate strip of veneer or solid wood molding to the edges. 

When inspecting a painted or dark piece, look closely at the corners and edges. If you can see or feel a distinct seam where the top surface meets the side panel, or if the grain direction changes abruptly at a 90-degree angle, you are looking at veneer. If that veneer is painted over and showing cracks or bubbling near the seams, stripping it back to bare wood will likely turn into a massive repair project.

## Look for the Hardware and Screw Clues

Original factory finishes tell a story through the hardware placement and interior construction. If a piece has been painted over outside of a factory setting, you will usually find physical evidence left behind by whoever did the job. 

Open the drawers and look at the drawer pulls or handles. Amateurs and quick-flip painters almost never remove the hardware; they simply tape around it or paint right over the backplates. If you unscrew a handle and find globs of paint trapped underneath the metal, you are dealing with a DIY paint job, which means you need to look closer at the wood condition underneath.

Next, check the bottom and interior panels. Original factory finishes are typically applied uniformly before final assembly, meaning the underside of a tabletop or the interior framing will match the wear patterns of the rest of the piece. If you find raw, unsealed plywood or MDF on the interior shelves while the exterior is heavily painted, the piece was likely constructed with budget-friendly composite materials wrapped in veneer.

## Feel for Temperature and Texture

Your hands are often better detectors than your eyes when evaluating furniture finishes. Solid hardwood feels different to the touch than painted veneer over composite wood.

*   **Temperature:** Solid wood tends to hold room temperature and feels neutral or slightly warm when you press your palm against it. Dense particle board or MDF cores covered in thick paint often feel noticeably colder and denser.
*   **Flexibility:** Press your thumb firmly into the center of a large flat surface, like a cabinet door or the top of a dresser. Solid wood will have zero give. If you feel even the slightest dip, flex, or spongy bounce, you are dealing with a hollow core or a thin veneer over a composite substrate.
*   **The Tap Test:** Gently tap the surface with your knuckles. Solid hardwood produces a tight, solid thud. A hollow or veneer-over-particle-board piece often produces a slightly more hollow, echoing sound.

## Inspect the Wear Patterns on High-Traffic Zones

Original factory finishes—whether lacquer, shellac, or conversion varnish—wear down in predictable ways over decades. They develop a soft patina, fine micro-scratches, and gradual fading in areas exposed to sunlight. 

Painted-over veneer wears entirely differently. Because latex or chalk paint sits entirely on top of the surface rather than penetrating the pores of the wood, it tends to chip off in flakes rather than wearing down smoothly. If you spot a chip in the paint on a table corner or a drawer edge, look at what is exposed underneath. Do you see raw white MDF, brown pressed wood, or a pale, paper-thin layer of wood grain? That is your definitive answer. 

If the exposed layer looks like paper or has a distinct striped grain pattern that looks identical across multiple flat panels, it is manufactured veneer. If it reveals a warm, dense hardwood with interlocking grain, you may have just uncovered a hidden gem.

Before you invest your time and energy into stripping, sanding, and refinishing a thrifted piece, taking two minutes to run through these physical checks will save you from a major disappointment. Knowing what lies beneath the surface ensures you only bring home projects that match your skill level and vision.
