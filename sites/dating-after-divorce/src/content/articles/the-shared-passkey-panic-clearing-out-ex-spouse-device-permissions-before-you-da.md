When I finally decided to dip my toes back into dating after my divorce, I spent hours agonizing over the usual things. I worried about how to write a dating app bio that didn't sound desperate, what to wear for a first coffee date, and whether I even remembered how to flirt. But I completely overlooked the glowing glowing ghost of my past marriage sitting right in my pocket: my digital ecosystem. 

A few weeks in, I went to show a new person a funny photo on my phone. Before I could swipe to the image, a prompt popped up offering to autofill a password using a shared passkey I hadn't thought about in two years. My ex-spouse’s old iPad was still silently syncing notes, location data, and saved credentials from my Apple and Google accounts. Panic set in immediately. I didn't want my new dating life colliding with my old legal settlement, and I certainly didn't want someone I barely knew stumbling onto remnants of my married past simply because I handed them my phone to look at a menu.

Clearing out your ex-spouse's lingering device permissions isn't just about digital hygiene; it is a crucial boundary-setting step for your peace of mind. Here is the exact checklist I used to scrub my tech life clean so I could start dating with a completely clear slate.

## Audit Your Apple ID and Google Account Devices

Most of us stay logged into the same tech ecosystems for years, long after relationships end. If you used a shared family computer, an old tablet left behind in the move, or your ex’s personal laptop to log into your main email, those devices might still have trusted status.

Open your smartphone settings right now and go to your account profile (Apple ID or Google Account). Look for a section labeled "Devices." You will likely be shocked by what you find. I discovered an old MacBook and an Apple TV that I hadn't seen in over a year, both of which still had full access to my messages, photos, and password manager. 

Tap on every single device that doesn't currently live in your immediate possession and select "Remove from Account" or "Sign Out." If you see a device you no longer recognize, change your primary account password immediately and toggle on two-factor authentication if it isn't already active.

## Purge Shared Passkeys and Saved Passwords

Passkeys and password managers are wonderful for convenience, but they are landmines for people starting over after a divorce. If you and your ex ever shared a browser profile or used a family password vault, your login credentials for banking, social media, and even dating apps might still be accessible to them.

Take thirty minutes to do a deep clean of your saved passwords:

*   **Review browser settings:** Check Chrome, Safari, or Firefox on all your devices to ensure no shared profiles or extension syncs are active.
*   **Audit password vaults:** If you used a service like 1Password, Bitwarden, or iCloud Keychain, look for shared vaults or family sharing groups and revoke access immediately.
*   **Check passkey registries:** Go into your phone's security settings and review active passkeys. Delete any that were created during your marriage, especially those tied to shared home Wi-Fi, smart locks, or joint financial accounts.
*   **Update high-risk logins:** Change the passwords for your email, banking, and any active dating profiles, ensuring recovery codes go exclusively to a device only you control.

## Sever Location Sharing and Find My Networks

During a marriage, sharing locations often feels like a practical safety measure. After a breakup, it becomes a privacy violation waiting to happen—especially when you are starting to go on dates in new neighborhoods. 

Open your device's location-sharing settings, such as Apple's "Find My" app or Google Maps location sharing. Check the list of people who can see your real-time whereabouts. I found that my ex and I still had mutual sharing enabled from a family road trip we took before separating. 

Remove your ex from your sharing list and check your device permissions for third-party apps as well. You do not want a forgotten shared grocery list app or fitness tracker broadcasting your movements while you are sitting across from someone new at a restaurant.

## Secure Your Messaging and Cloud Storage

Photos and notes are often the hardest things to let go of, but they are also the most common culprits when a new partner accidentally sees something they shouldn't. Shared photo albums, joint cloud storage folders, and synced messaging apps can easily resurface old memories at the worst possible times.

Go into your cloud storage provider (iCloud, Google Drive, or Dropbox) and check for shared folders. Delete or download your personal copies and revoke access for anyone else. For messaging apps like WhatsApp or Telegram, check your linked devices list in the settings menu to ensure no desktop app on an ex's computer is currently mirroring your active chats. 

Taking these steps takes less than an hour, but it changes how you feel when you step out the door for a date. You want to focus entirely on the person in front of you, without that nagging worry in the back of your mind about what digital doors are still left unlocked. Once your tech is truly yours alone, you are ready to figure out your next steps in the dating world with total confidence.
