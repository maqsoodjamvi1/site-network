Sitting in a high-stakes meeting, my shoulders start creeping up toward my ears. The fluorescent lights hum a little too loudly, the projector fan has a rhythmic whine, and my colleague’s clicking pen sounds like a timer ticking down. By the ten-minute mark, I am entirely overwhelmed. For years, I assumed I was just a classic "empath," soaking up the collective anxiety of the conference room. But I eventually learned that my nervous system was actually hitting a wall due to sensory overload—a hallmark of being a Highly Sensitive Person (HSP).

If you find yourself dreading the weekly team sync or needing an hour of silence after a thirty-minute Zoom call, you might be wondering whether you are dealing with sensory processing sensitivity or true emotional absorption. While these two experiences overlap, understanding the difference changed how I survive the modern workday.

## What is Sensory Processing Sensitivity (HSP)?

Coined by Dr. Elaine Aron, the term Highly Sensitive Person describes a distinct neurological trait found in roughly 20 percent of the population. If you are an HSP, your nervous system processes physical and environmental data much more deeply and thoroughly than other people do. 

In a professional setting, this looks like physical and cognitive friction. You aren't necessarily feeling your coworkers' moods; rather, your brain is working overtime to filter out the competing inputs around you. 

*   **The meeting room temperature:** You are the only person sweating or freezing, acutely aware of the draft from the AC vent.
*   **Visual clutter:** A messy whiteboard, flickering overhead lights, or someone constantly tapping their foot in your peripheral vision pulls your focus away from the agenda.
*   **Auditory fatigue:** Multiple side conversations, overlapping voices on a conference speakerphone, or the high-pitched whine of office equipment drain your cognitive battery long before the meeting ends.

An HSP gets drained because the environment is too loud, too bright, or too fast-paced. Your brain is sorting an immense amount of sensory data without a filter, leading to classic overstimulation.

## What is Emotional Absorption (The Empath Experience)?

Emotional absorption is a different beast. While an HSP reacts primarily to the physical environment, an empath reacts to the emotional climate of a room. When you absorb emotions, you take on the feelings of others as if they were your own. 

In a meeting, this usually manifests as a sudden, unexplainable shift in your internal state. You walk into the room feeling completely fine, but five minutes after the project manager starts speaking, a heavy knot forms in your stomach. 

I used to mistake this for my own anxiety. Then I started playing detective. I would look around the table and realize the project manager was secretly terrified about an upcoming deadline, or two coworkers were quietly harboring a silent grudge. My nervous system had bypassed my rational mind and picked up their emotional frequencies, acting like a sponge for feelings that didn’t belong to me.

## How to Tell Which One Is Driving Your Meeting Fatigue

To figure out whether your discomfort is sensory or emotional, you have to run a quick diagnostic check the next time you feel drained at work. Ask yourself these specific questions:

*   **Did the physical room trigger it?** If your irritation started because the chair was uncomfortable, the room smelled like someone's leftover tuna sandwich, and the projector hummed, you are likely experiencing HSP overload. 
*   **Did a person's mood trigger it?** If you felt fine until a specific colleague sat down and started radiating frustration, defensiveness, or panic, you are likely experiencing emotional absorption.
*   **Does solitude fix it?** If sitting in your car in silence for ten minutes helps you recover, your issue was likely sensory overload. If you still feel emotionally heavy or anxious even in a quiet, dark room, you likely absorbed someone else's emotional baggage and need to actively shed it.

## Practical Strategies for Surviving Both

Once you know what you are dealing with, you can stop fighting your biology and start managing your workday more strategically. 

If you lean heavily HSP, your main goal is environmental modification. Claim a seat with your back to the wall so you don't have movement behind you. Wear high-fidelity, discreet earplugs that dull background office noise without blocking out human speech. If you have a say in the meeting format, advocate for agendas sent out beforehand so your deep-processing brain isn't forced to formulate complex opinions on the spot.

If you lean toward emotional absorption, your strategy needs to be energetic boundary-setting. Before stepping into a meeting, I picture myself zipping up a light-colored jacket or wrapping an invisible shield around my torso. It sounds simple, but this mental cue reminds my brain where my energy ends and other people's begin. If I notice a sudden wave of panic or anger washing over me during a presentation, I mentally pause and ask myself: "Is this mine?" Just labeling the emotion as borrowed is often enough to loosen its grip.

Figuring out whether your workplace fatigue stems from sensory processing sensitivity or emotional absorption takes a bit of self-observation, but it is entirely worth the effort. When you stop treating your sensitivity as a flaw and start recognizing it as specific operational data, you can build a workday that protects your energy.
