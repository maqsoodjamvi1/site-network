When I first started seriously trying to learn Spanish on my phone, I hit a frustrating wall. I could match pictures of apples to Spanish words and tap glowing tiles to put "The cat drinks milk" in the correct order. But the second I tried to write my own sentence about what I did over the weekend, my brain froze. I knew a lot of vocabulary, but I had no idea how the language actually glued itself together. 

If you are an adult learner staring down the app store, you probably want to avoid that same trap. You aren't just looking for a fun game to pass the time on your commute; you want to know how grammar actually gets taught. Among the free language apps, Duolingo, Busuu, and Memrise take three wildly different approaches to grammar. Here is how they actually stack up when you want to move past memorization and understand the mechanics of a new language.

## Duolingo: The Implicit Learning Approach

Duolingo is the undisputed king of casual language apps, and its approach to grammar is entirely implicit. If you open a Spanish or French path on Duolingo, you will not find a traditional grammar lesson with charts of verb conjugations or declension rules. Instead, you learn grammar the way a child does: through heavy repetition and pattern recognition.

For example, Duolingo will feed you sentences like "Yo hablo español" (I speak Spanish), then "Tú hablas español" (You speak Spanish), and eventually "Él habla español" (He speaks Spanish). You notice the endings changing through sheer exposure. If you tap the little lightbulb icon before starting certain units, you will get a brief explanation of the rule, but these tips are often hidden or absent in the newer path updates. 

**The Verdict:** Duolingo is great for building a daily habit because it feels like a mobile game. However, if you are an adult who likes to understand *why* a rule exists before applying it, Duolingo can feel painfully slow and mysterious. You will learn grammar, but it might take you months of tapping tiles before the underlying system finally clicks in your head.

## Busuu: The Traditional Textbook Approach

Busuu feels much more like a traditional classroom, which is a massive relief if you prefer structure. Unlike Duolingo, Busuu explicitly teaches grammar rules upfront. Before you do any matching exercises, a typical Busuu lesson often starts with a concise, plain-language chart explaining a concept like direct object pronouns or the past tense. 

Once you read the rule, you immediately practice it through fill-in-the-blank exercises and sentence composition. Furthermore, Busuu includes a community feature where native speakers can correct your written exercises. If you write a sentence using a newly learned grammar rule, a native speaker might leave a note saying, "We don't use that preposition here; use this one instead."

* Explicit grammar cards with clear English explanations before practice exercises.
* Targeted units addressing common stumbling blocks, like gender agreement or formal versus informal "you."
* Writing prompts that force you to construct your own sentences rather than just tapping word banks.
* Community feedback loops where real humans correct your application of grammar rules.

**The Verdict:** If your brain appreciates structure, rules, and clear definitions, Busuu handles grammar better than almost any other free-tier app. It bridges the gap between raw vocabulary memorization and actual sentence building. 

## Memrise: The Context-First Approach

Memrise takes a completely different route. While it started strictly as a flashcard app, its modern iterations focus heavily on real-world video clips of native speakers talking naturally. When it comes to grammar, Memrise operates on a "context is king" philosophy.

You won't see grammar charts here. Instead, you watch short videos of locals on the streets of Madrid or Tokyo rattling off natural phrases. The app relies on you picking up grammatical patterns organically by seeing how native speakers actually string thoughts together in casual conversation. The focus is heavily weighted toward listening comprehension and high-frequency phrases you can use right away.

**The Verdict:** Memrise is fantastic for getting used to the rhythm of a language and hearing different accents. But if you are looking for a systematic explanation of grammar, Memrise will leave you guessing. It is less about teaching you *how* to build sentences and more about teaching you whole chunks of language to memorize.

## Which One Should You Choose?

Deciding which app deserves a spot on your home screen comes down to how your specific brain likes to learn. 

If you just want to build a five-minute daily habit without thinking too hard, Duolingo is hard to beat, even if its grammar instruction is entirely accidental. If you want actual explanations, clear rules, and practice writing your own sentences, Busuu is the clear winner for structural learners. If your main goal is training your ear to understand native speakers in the wild, Memrise is worth a download. 

As you weigh your options and decide which platform fits your daily routine, consider taking a moment to look closely at what your ultimate language goals look like and which app structure best matches your learning style.
