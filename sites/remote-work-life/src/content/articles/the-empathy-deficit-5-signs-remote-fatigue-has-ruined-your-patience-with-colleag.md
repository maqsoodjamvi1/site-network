I used to pride myself on being the easygoing teammate. Back when we all sat in a fluorescent-lit bullpen, someone could drop by my desk unannounced with a chaotic last-minute request, and I’d just smile and help them sort it out. 

Three years into managing a hybrid and remote schedule, that version of me is gone. 

Last Tuesday, a colleague pinged me on Slack with a basic question about a shared spreadsheet—a question answered by simply scrolling up three messages. Instead of replying kindly, I stared at my monitor, felt a hot spike of irrational rage, and typed back a response so passive-aggressive I had to delete it twice before hitting send. 

If you find yourself treating normal workplace friction like a personal attack, you might be dealing with the remote work empathy deficit. When your entire career happens through a glass screen, your emotional bandwidth slowly leaks away. Here are five specific signs that remote fatigue has eroded your patience with your colleagues, and what you can actually do to fix it.

## 1. You Interpret Neutral Slack Messages as Aggressive

When you lose face-to-face cues like posture, vocal inflection, and eye contact, your brain has to fill in the blanks. And when you are tired, your brain defaults to paranoia. 

If a coworker sends a message that just says "Can we talk?" or "Did you see this?", your tired brain immediately translates it as: *They think you messed up and they are coming for your job.* You start drafting defensive paragraphs before they even type their second sentence. You spend twenty minutes analyzing whether a period at the end of a Slack reply means they hate you, completely forgetting that they might just be typing on a phone with one hand while holding a cup of coffee.

## 2. You Dread Any Meeting That Requires Small Talk

Remember when chatting about weekend plans at the coffee machine felt natural? Now, forced camaraderie on video calls feels like an interrogation. 

When your calendar is a Tetris board of back-to-back Microsoft Teams sessions, the mandatory two minutes of "How was your weekend?" before diving into the agenda feels like a cruel waste of time. You find yourself staring blankly at your webcam, gritting your teeth, counting down the seconds until someone finally says, "Let's share screens and get started." You aren't just tired of meetings; you are tired of performing humanity on demand.

## 3. Your Immediate Reaction to Mistakes is Irritation, Not Curiosity

In an office setting, if someone misses a deadline, your default assumption is usually contextual: *They must be swamped, or something came up.* 

In remote work isolation, every mistake feels intentional. When a teammate forgets to CC you on an email or uploads the wrong version of a presentation, your internal monologue instantly brands them as incompetent or lazy. You stop asking "What went wrong in the process?" and start thinking, "Why can't these people just do their jobs?" You’ve stopped extending the benefit of the doubt because protecting your own energy has become your full-time job.

## 4. You Audit Other People’s Calendars and Output

Working from home breeds a weird, toxic brand of surveillance culture, even among people who aren't managers. 

Do you find yourself keeping mental tabs on when your coworkers go "away" on status indicators? Do you feel a flash of resentment when someone logs off at 4:30 PM, or when a colleague takes twenty minutes to reply to an afternoon ping? When your own boundaries feel fragile, you start policing everyone else's. You begin keeping score of who works harder, measuring your worth by your visibility and weaponizing it against people who are just trying to live their lives.

## 5. You Experience "Camera Fatigue" as Physical Repulsion

It goes beyond regular tiredness. There is a specific physical dread that comes with seeing your own face in the corner of a monitor while trying to listen to someone talk about quarterly projections. 

You start hiding behind off-camera excuses, or worse, you sit there with your video on while mentally checking out entirely—nodding mechanically like a malfunctioning bobblehead while scrolling through online shopping tabs. When you actively avoid looking at your colleagues' faces because the mere sight of a grid of video tiles makes your shoulders tense up, your capacity for empathy has hit absolute zero.

## How to Reset Your Empathy Switch Today

You cannot willpower your way out of remote fatigue by just "trying to be nicer." Burnout requires structural changes to how you interact with your digital workspace. Try these practical steps this week:

*   **Implement a 60-second rule:** Before hitting send on any message that feels slightly snappy, walk away from your desk for one minute. Drink a glass of water. If it still sounds aggressive when you get back, rewrite it.
*   **Kill the self-view:** On your video platform of choice, right-click your own video feed and select "Hide Self-View." Staring at your own facial expressions all day drains your cognitive energy and makes you hyper-aware, which fuels irritability.
*   **Batch your asynchronous communication:** Stop leaving chat apps open all day like a live wire. Set specific blocks (e.g., 10:00 AM and 2:00 PM) to check and reply to messages, rather than reacting in real-time while you are trying to do deep work.
