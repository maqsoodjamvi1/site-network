Three years into working entirely from my living room, I realized I had a text message problem. 

It didn’t start as a problem. When I first transitioned to remote work, I was terrified that out of sight meant out of mind. To prove I was actually working, I adopted a strategy of hyper-responsiveness. If a Slack notification pinged at 8:00 PM, I answered it immediately. If I finished a simple task, I didn't just mark it done in Asana; I wrote a three-paragraph explanation of my thought process in the project channel. 

By year two, I was exhausted. My Slack status was practically welded to "Active," and I felt a creeping, stomach-churning anxiety whenever my inbox sat empty for more than twenty minutes. 

That is when I recognized the trap. I wasn't communicating because my team needed the information. I was over-communicating because remote burnout was making me panic. Without the physical cues of an office—like seeing your manager walk past your desk—my brain substituted endless typing as proof of my own productivity and safety. 

If you find yourself constantly typing long explanations no one asked for, you might be caught in the same loop. Here are four specific signs that remote burnout is causing you to overshare in Slack, and what you can do to fix it today.

## 1. You Narrate Every Micro-Task of Your Day

Do you find yourself typing messages like, "Just stepping away for a glass of water, back in five," or "Finished the spreadsheet, now opening the slide deck"? 

In an office, people leave their desks constantly without announcing it to the floor. But in a remote setting, burnout warps our perception of visibility. We start to feel like invisible ghosts, which triggers a subconscious need to narrate our every move. 

When you are deeply fatigued, your cognitive load is shot. Instead of trusting your overall output, you rely on a constant stream of minor updates to prove you are still alive and working. This doesn't reassure your boss; it usually just adds noise to their day and drains the last of your mental energy.

**The Fix:** Implement a "silent break" rule for yourself. Unless you are stepping away during a time you are specifically scheduled to lead a meeting or cover urgent support, you do not need to announce micro-breaks. Trust that your deliverables speak louder than a status log.

## 2. You Write Essay-Length Explanations for Simple Yes/No Answers

Look at your last ten direct messages. Are most of them three lines or longer? 

When remote burnout sets in, our confidence takes a hit. We start second-guessing our competence, which translates into defensive writing. If a colleague asks, "Did you update the client file?", a burned-out worker won't just reply, "Yes." Instead, they write: "Yes, I updated the client file, and I also checked the historical notes from last Tuesday just in case, and I reformatted the headers because I noticed they were slightly off, let me know if you need anything else!"

You are preemptively defending your work because your tired brain assumes someone is about to find a flaw in it. 

**The Fix:** Practice the one-sentence rule. Before you hit send on a reply, delete everything past the direct answer. If they need more context, they will ask. 

## 3. You Apologize Constantly for Normal Timelines

Count how many times you start a message with "Sorry for the delay!" or "Sorry to bother you with this!" 

When you are burnt out, every interaction feels like an imposition. You feel guilty simply for existing in the same digital workspace as other people. This leads to an avalanche of pre-emptive apologies for things that require no apology, such as replying to an email within two hours or asking a clarifying question about a vague assignment. 

Oversharing your guilt in Slack creates a weird dynamic where you inadvertently train your team to view you as fragile or overwhelmed, even when your actual workload is completely manageable.

**The Fix:** Do a find-and-replace sweep in your drafts. Delete every single "sorry" that isn't attached to an actual mistake. Replace it with a simple "thank you," such as, "Thanks for waiting on this," instead of, "Sorry it took me so long."

## 4. You Overshare Personal Anxiety to Force Connection

Remote work can be deeply isolating, and burnout magnifies that loneliness. Sometimes, this manifests as dropping heavy personal stress into professional channels—oversharing about your insomnia, your family drama, or your existential dread about the state of the world to coworkers you barely know.

While building rapport is important, using work chat channels as an emotional pressure valve often backfires. When you log off, a wave of embarrassment hits you because you realize you just unloaded your deepest anxieties into a thread about quarterly software updates. 

**The Fix:** Channel your need for connection outside of work hours, or set aside specific time for casual coworker chats (like a virtual coffee break) rather than leaking personal stress into project-based channels. 

Breaking the over-communication habit is uncomfortable at first. It requires sitting with the quietness of a closed Slack window and trusting that your actual work output is enough. But once you stop wasting energy narrating your life, you will find you finally have the mental margin to actually enjoy your remote workday.
