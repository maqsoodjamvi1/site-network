If you work from home, you probably know the feeling. Your alarm goes off, you walk ten feet to your desk, and you open your laptop. At first glance, your calendar looks manageable: a ten o'clock project sync, a noon check-in, and a three o'clock retro. But then you look closer. There are fifteen-minute slivers of white space wedged between them. Suddenly, your day looks less like a schedule and more like a game of high-stakes Tetris where every block is dropping too fast.

When you work remotely, the boundary between your workday and your life is made of pixels. Without the natural pauses of an office—like walking to a conference room or grabbing a coffee in the breakroom—meetings pile on top of each other until your brain stops processing them as communication and starts seeing them as threats. 

Over the last few years of working from home full-time, I’ve hit this wall harder than I’d like to admit. At my worst, the sound of a Microsoft Teams chime would send a jolt of genuine panic through my chest. If you are starting to dread simple, low-stakes chats that used to roll off your back, you might be dealing with a specific type of remote fatigue. Here are four hidden signs that calendar gridlock is burning you out, and what you can actually do to fix it.

## 1. You Start Guarding Micro-Slivers of Time Like Gold

When your schedule is packed back-to-back, a ten-minute gap between calls stops being a break. Instead, it becomes a desperate race against the clock. Can you answer two Slack DMs, switch your laundry, and swallow a handful of cold lunch before the next camera turns on? 

When you find yourself intensely protective of a stray twelve-minute block on your calendar—and genuinely angry when someone tries to drop a quick sync into it—you are deep in the Tetris loop. You aren't resting during those gaps; you're bracing for the next impact. 

*   **The Fix:** Stop letting people book your day down to the minute. Block out mandatory "buffer time" on your calendar every single day. Make these blocks non-negotiable, visible to your team, and at least 30 minutes long. Treat this time as a meeting with yourself that cannot be moved.

## 2. The "Pre-Meeting Dreading" Ritual Consumes Your Morning

Do you find yourself thinking about a routine 2 PM meeting at 9 AM? Even if the meeting requires zero preparation and carries zero risk, your exhausted brain treats it like an impending performance review. You spend hours subconsciously rehearsing what you'll say, worrying about your internet connection, or simply resenting the fact that the meeting exists at all.

This anticipatory anxiety is a classic symptom of remote burnout. Because your home is supposed to be your sanctuary, the intrusion of endless virtual faces creates a low-grade, persistent stress response that lingers long before the call actually starts.

*   **The Fix:** Audit your recurring meetings ruthlessly. If you attend a weekly sync where you only provide a two-sentence update, ask your manager if you can submit that update asynchronously via text or a Loom video instead. Opt out of anything where your presence is purely passive.

## 3. Camera Fatigue Makes You Feel Like an Actor on Stage

In a physical office, you don't stare directly at your own face for eight hours a day. You don't have to worry about whether your lighting makes you look tired, or whether your background looks professional enough, or if your eye contact looks natural through a glass lens.

Remote meetings force us into a state of constant self-monitoring. When every conversation happens through a glowing rectangle, you are effectively performing. If a simple five-minute check-in feels as exhausting as giving a TED Talk, it is because your brain is expending massive amounts of energy just managing your digital presence.

*   **The Fix:** Normalize turning your camera off for routine internal calls, or institute "audio-only Fridays" with your team. If your company culture makes that difficult, use the "hide self-view" feature in your video software. You don't need to watch yourself talk in order to do your job well.

## 4. You Hide Behind "Async" as an Escape Hatch

When burnout gets severe, your communication style changes. You might find yourself aggressively pushing every single conversation into text, email, or project management boards—not because async communication is always better for the project, but because you simply cannot bear the thought of one more video call. 

While asynchronous work is a great tool, using it purely as a defensive shield against human interaction usually means your workflow has become unsustainable. You end up spending hours typing long, defensive explanations because you're too depleted to spend ten minutes talking it out live.

*   **The Fix:** Balance your communication channels intentionally. Schedule one or two days a week with zero external meetings so you can dive into deep work, but keep open lines of communication so you don't isolate yourself completely. Burnout thrives in isolation, and hiding behind a wall of text can sometimes make the mental weight heavier.

Digging yourself out of the calendar Tetris loop doesn't happen overnight, and it requires setting boundaries that might feel uncomfortable at first.
