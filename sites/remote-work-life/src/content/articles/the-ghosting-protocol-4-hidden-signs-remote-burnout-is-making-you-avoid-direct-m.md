Working from home gives you total freedom until your nervous system quietly decides it has had enough. If you have spent the last three days staring at a blinking Slack notification or an unread Microsoft Teams ping, feeling a cold spike of dread in your chest, you are not just procrastinating. You are experiencing remote burnout, and your brain has deployed a very specific coping mechanism: the ghosting protocol.

When your office is your living room and your commute is a 10-step walk from the bed, the boundary between work and life dissolves completely. Without physical exits, interruptions, or natural pauses, your brain treats every digital ping as a low-level threat. Eventually, avoiding your direct messages becomes less about laziness and more about self-preservation. 

Here are four hidden signs that remote burnout is making you ghost your coworkers, along with practical ways to break the cycle before it damages your career.

## 1. The 10-Second Draft-and-Delete Loop

You open a direct message from a manager or a teammate. You type out a perfectly reasonable, polite response: "Sure, I can take a look at that spreadsheet by 3 PM." 

Then, you stare at it. You start overthinking the tone. Does "sure" sound too passive-aggressive? Is an exclamation point too eager? You delete the entire message, close the app, and walk away to make another cup of coffee, promising yourself you will reply in ten minutes. Two hours later, the message is still sitting there, unsent, and the dread has only multiplied.

This drafting loop happens because cognitive fatigue robs you of your executive function. Simple social choices feel like high-stakes diplomatic negotiations. 

**The fix:** Lower the bar for communication. Give yourself permission to send raw, unpolished text. A simple "Got it, looking into this now" sent immediately is ten times better than a polished paragraph sent three hours too late. 

## 2. Setting Up Fake "Deep Work" Blocks to Hide

Look at your calendar. Do you have recurring, multi-hour blocks titled "Focused Work," "Project Alpha Prep," or "Do Not Disturb" that you actually spend scrolling on your phone or staring blankly at your monitor? 

When remote burnout sets in, calendar manipulation becomes a survival tactic. You block off time not because you have high-level strategic tasks to complete, but simply to stem the constant tide of incoming demands. You use these fake barriers to buy yourself breathing room, but the guilt of hiding makes the burnout worse.

**The fix:** Be honest with your manager about your actual capacity. Instead of hiding behind fake calendar blocks, block off genuine downtime and label it "Lunch" or "Offline." If you need a morning without interruptions to actually get things done, talk to your team about establishing core communication hours rather than playing hide-and-seek on Google Calendar.

## 3. Treating Every Notification Like a Physical Intrusion

Pay attention to your body the next time your chat app makes a chime or a banner pops up on your screen. Do your shoulders jump up to your ears? Do you feel a sudden flash of irritation toward the person messaging you, even if they are just asking a normal, friendly question?

When you are deeply burned out, your nervous system is trapped in a constant state of low-grade fight-or-flight. Because your work is tied to your computer, every digital notification feels like someone barging into your bedroom uninvited. You aren't mad at your coworker; you are mad at the constant intrusion of work into your personal sanctuary.

**The fix:** Change your notification landscape entirely. Turn off all desktop banners, audio chimes, and phone push notifications for work apps. Set specific, scheduled times during the day—say, every 90 minutes—when you open the app, process your messages in a batch, and then close it again. Take your power back by deciding *when* you look, rather than reacting to every bell and whistle.

## 4. Crafting Elaborate Excuses for Minor Delays

When you finally do reply to a message you have avoided for hours, do you find yourself typing out elaborate justifications? *"So sorry for the delay, my internet dropped out for a second and then my dog had an emergency vet visit and I was also pulled into an unexpected emergency sync..."*

Burnout breeds paranoia. When you know you are ghosting people, you assume everyone else knows you are slacking off, even if you are just paralyzed. You over-explain to protect your reputation, which drains even more of your limited energy.

**The fix:** Stop apologizing for taking time to breathe. A simple, confident "Thanks for reaching out, catching up on this now" is usually all that is required. Most coworkers are too busy with their own burnout to analyze the exact timestamp of your reply.

Breaking the ghosting protocol starts with admitting that your current remote setup is draining your battery faster than it can recharge.
