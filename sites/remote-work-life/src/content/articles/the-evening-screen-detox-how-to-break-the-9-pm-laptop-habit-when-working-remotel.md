Working from home is supposed to give you your life back, but for most of us, it just means the office moved into the bedroom. When your dining room table doubles as your desk, the physical commute disappears, and the mental commute goes with it. 

For the first year of working remotely, my laptop lived on my kitchen island. By 9 PM, while watching a show with my partner, I would hear the familiar chime of a Slack notification or notice a red badge on my email icon. I’d tell myself I was just clearing out tomorrow’s to-do list, but three years in, that five-minute habit had expanded into a nightly two-hour second shift. 

Breaking the 9 PM laptop habit isn't about having ironclad willpower. It’s about building friction into your environment so that ignoring your work is actually easier than engaging with it. Here is the exact system I used to reclaim my evenings.

## Move the Hardware Out of Sight

The biggest trap of remote work is visual availability. If your laptop is sitting on the counter open and glowing, your brain registers it as an active task. Willpower is a finite resource at the end of a long day; if you have to walk past your workspace to get a glass of water, you will eventually stop and check your notifications.

I started a rule called the "Hard Close" at 5:30 PM. Once my final task is done, the laptop goes into a vertical charging dock inside a closed cabinet in my hallway. The power cord stays coiled up. 

If I want to check something at 9 PM, I now have to walk to the hallway, open a cabinet, pull out the laptop, plug it in, and wait for it to boot up. That physical friction introduces about sixty seconds of inconvenience. That sixty-second delay is usually all the time my brain needs to realize, "Actually, this email can wait until 8 AM tomorrow."

## Separate Your Digital Environments

When your personal life and professional life run on the same device, the boundary blurs completely. I used to browse Reddit or watch YouTube on my work laptop, which meant my brain associated that machine with relaxation just as much as stress.

To fix this, I set up a strict digital firewall:
* **Create a personal user profile:** Log out of all work email accounts, Slack channels, and project management tools on your personal browser.
* **Remove the apps:** Delete work-related communication apps from your smartphone entirely, or configure your phone's "Focus" modes to automatically hide them after working hours.
* **Stop borrowing devices:** If you need to check a personal spreadsheet or order groceries, use your phone, a tablet, or a personal computer—never open the work machine after hours.

By starving your work laptop of personal utility, it stops feeling like a companion device and starts feeling like what it actually is: an employer-owned tool.

## Establish a Fake Commute

When you work in an office, the train ride or the drive home serves as a decompression chamber. Working remotely robs you of that transition. Without it, you step away from your desk at 5 PM and immediately try to cook dinner or talk to your family while your nervous system is still processing client demands.

When you don't decompress, your evening anxiety spikes, which often triggers the compulsion to open your laptop at 9 PM just to feel a false sense of control. 

I replaced the physical commute with a mandatory twenty-minute ritual. At the end of the workday, I put on my running shoes and walk around the neighborhood without my phone. No podcasts, no music—just silence. It signals to my brain that the workday is officially over. When I walk back through my front door, the workday feels like it happened yesterday, not twenty minutes ago.

## Plan Tomorrow Before You Close the Lid

The urge to check your work late at night usually stems from anxiety about the unknown. You are worried you forgot something important, or you are dreading a difficult conversation waiting for you in the morning. That lingering dread acts as a magnet pulling you back to the screen.

You can short-circuit this by writing a "Tomorrow List" before you finish your workday. Do not make a massive wishlist of fifty tasks. Write down the top three non-negotiable things you need to tackle first thing in the morning. Put a sticky note over your closed laptop with those three items written on it. 

When you know exactly where you are starting tomorrow, your brain doesn't need to chew on your job while you are trying to relax on the couch. 

If you find yourself stuck in a cycle of evening burnout and late-night screen time, remember that building a sustainable remote career requires treating your rest with the same discipline you apply to your deliverables.
