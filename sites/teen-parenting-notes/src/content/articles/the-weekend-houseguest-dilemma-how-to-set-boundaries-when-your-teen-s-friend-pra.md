It starts innocently enough. Your teenager asks if their friend can stay the night on Friday. You say yes, because you want your home to be the "safe house" where your kids and their friends actually want to hang out. Fast forward three weeks: that same friend has slept over four weekends in a row, is eating your groceries like a teenage locust, and has left a trail of wet towels in your downstairs bathroom that seems permanent. 

If you are currently wondering how a middle schooler or high schooler has essentially established residency in your guest room without paying rent or signing a lease, take a deep breath. You are not alone, and you are not a bad person for wanting your weekend house back. 

## Spotting the Shift from Guest to Resident

There is a distinct line between a welcome guest and an unintended roommate, and it usually gets crossed around week two. At first, you are happy to provide pizza and a spot on the basement futon. But when the friend stops bringing a toothbrush because "they have a spare one here," or starts casually opening your refrigerator to make custom paninis without asking, the dynamic has shifted.

You might notice yourself feeling a sudden spike of annoyance when you hear the front door open on a Friday afternoon. That gut reaction is valuable data. It is your nervous system telling you that your home space—your sanctuary—feels compromised. Teenagers are notoriously bad at reading social cues, so waiting for the friend to realize they have overstayed their welcome is a losing strategy. They will not pick up on hints. You have to use actual words.

## The Family Meeting Before the Weekend Starts

The biggest mistake parents make is trying to address the "semi-permanent houseguest" situation in the heat of the moment, usually while staring at a mess in the kitchen on a Saturday morning. Instead, hit pause during the week. 

Pull your teenager aside for a private chat—not to yell or accuse, but to recalibrate. Acknowledge that you love that their friend is close by, but lay out the reality of household logistics. 

*   **Establish a frequency cap:** Decide what is reasonable for your family. One weekend sleepover every two weeks, or one night per weekend, is a standard boundary. 
*   **Define expectations:** Make it clear that staying over means chipping in. Guests who are practically family still need to clear their plates, keep their gear out of the main hallways, and respect family quiet hours.
*   **Check in on the friend's home base:** Sometimes, kids gravitate toward one house because things are chaotic or strained at their own. Knowing the background doesn't mean you have to host indefinitely, but it helps you frame the conversation with empathy.

## How to Talk to the Friend Without Making It Awkward

You do not need to sit the friend down for a formal, terrifying intervention. In fact, casual, breezy directness works best. Teenagers respect adults who are straight with them without being mean.

The next time the friend arrives on a Friday with an oversized duffel bag, pull them aside for a quick, friendly chat in the hallway. You can say something like, "Hey, just so you know, our family has a lot going on this weekend, so Sunday morning is going to be a hard wrap for sleepovers. We’ll need you to head home around 10 AM so we can get our weekend chores done." 

By making it about your household schedule rather than their annoying habits, you remove the personal attack. You are simply reporting the weather: "It's raining, and the sleepover ends Sunday morning."

## Resetting Your Grocery and Privacy Boundaries

If the financial strain of feeding an extra human is getting to you, or if you are tired of walking past a teenager lounging on your couch in your bathrobe at noon, it is time to adjust your home culture. 

Designate specific food zones. If certain snacks are off-limits or reserved for school lunches, label them or put them in a specific pantry basket. Tell your teen and their friend that dinner is provided, but weekend breakfast is fend-for-yourself using whatever basics are available. 

Similarly, claim your physical space. If you want to drink your Saturday morning coffee in your pajamas without making small talk with your kid's best friend about video games, institute a "no one comes upstairs before 9 AM" rule. Your bedrooms and upstairs hallways are private family zones.

Setting these boundaries doesn't make you an unfriendly neighbor or a harsh parent. It teaches your teenager a vital life lesson about consent, hospitality, and knowing when a good thing has run its course. When you protect your own peace and energy, you model how healthy adults manage their homes.
