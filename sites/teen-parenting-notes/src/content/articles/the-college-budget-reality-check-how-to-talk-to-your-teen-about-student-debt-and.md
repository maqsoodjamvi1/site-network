When my oldest son sat down at the kitchen table last November with a stack of college acceptance letters, we were both riding a high of excitement and pride. Then, reality hit. We opened the financial aid portals, looked at the total cost of attendance, and realized the gap between what the colleges offered and what we could actually afford was massive. 

If you are staring down similar college letters, you know that the sticker price is only the beginning. Between meal plans, dorm supplies, textbooks, and the occasional pizza run, the hidden costs add up fast. Having an honest, grounded conversation with your teenager about student debt and living expenses is one of the most important things you can do right now, before deposits are paid. Here is how we navigated that conversation, and how you can do the same.

## Shift the Focus from "Getting In" to "Paying For It"

For years, the narrative in our house was simply about getting good grades so the doors would open. Once the doors actually opened, we had to completely pivot the conversation. Teens often view college acceptance as the finish line, when it is actually just the starting line of a major financial contract.

I sat down with my son and asked him to log into the financial aid portal with me. Instead of looking at the school names, we looked strictly at the math. We broke down the difference between "subsidized loans," "unsubsidized loans," and parent-facing loans like Parent PLUS. I made sure he understood a very sobering statistic: a standard ten-year repayment plan means that borrowing $30,000 results in a monthly payment of roughly $350 right when he will be trying to start his adult life, rent an apartment, and buy a reliable car. Putting real numbers to the debt made it abstractly terrifying into something concrete he could grasp.

## Build a Realistic Living Expense Budget Together

Tuition and room-and-board are fixed costs, but living expenses are where budgets usually fall apart. Most colleges publish an "estimated cost of attendance" that includes books and personal expenses, but those estimates are often wildly optimistic. 

To give my son a taste of real-world budgeting, we built a line-item monthly expense sheet based on the actual town where his preferred school is located. We looked at:

*   **Groceries and snacks:** Dorm mini-fridges need restocking, and dining hall food gets old by October.
*   **Transportation:** Flights home for Thanksgiving, rideshares, or parking passes if freshmen are allowed cars.
*   **Laundry and toiletries:** Detergent, shampoo, and paper towels are expenses teenagers rarely think about when living at home.
*   **Social spending:** Movies, club dues, and weekend outings.

We decided that I would cover a specific base amount for groceries and school supplies, but any extra spending money had to come from a part-time campus job or his own savings. Giving him ownership of that variable spending forced him to prioritize what he actually needed versus what he just wanted.

## Set Hard Boundaries on Student Borrowing

Before my son fell in love with a specific campus, we established a firm boundary regarding debt. We looked at his projected starting salary for his intended major (computer science) and used the rule of thumb that total undergraduate debt should not exceed the expected first-year salary. 

For schools that required borrowing beyond that threshold, we crossed them off the list together. It was a heartbreaking moment, but it prevented us from making an emotional decision that would haunt him for a decade. Setting these limits early takes the emotion out of the choice; it turns the decision into a math problem rather than a measure of his worth or my love as a parent.

## Create a Roadmap for the Next Four Years

A college budget is not a one-time conversation you have in the spring of senior year. It is a living document that needs checking in on as your teen transitions to campus life. We agreed to review his actual spending at the end of his first semester to see where our estimates were off. If he overspent on takeout, we adjust the following semester. 

Navigating the financial realities of higher education with your teenager is daunting, but it is also a powerful lesson in financial literacy that high schools rarely teach. By pulling back the curtain on debt and living expenses now, you are giving your teen the tools to graduate not just with a degree, but with financial independence.
