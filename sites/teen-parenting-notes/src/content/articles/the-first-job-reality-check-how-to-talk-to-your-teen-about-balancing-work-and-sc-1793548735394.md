When my oldest son came home waving a hiring sheet from the local grocery store, my first reaction was pure pride. He was sixteen, eager for independence, and ready to earn his own spending money. My second reaction, however, was quiet panic. I pictured his grades sliding, his sleep schedule evaporating, and his weekends swallowed by 15-hour shifts behind a deli counter. 

Balancing a first job and high school is a massive rite of passage, but it is also a high-stakes tightrope walk. If you are navigating this milestone with your own teenager right now, you know the anxiety of wanting them to learn responsibility without burning out. Here is how we managed the transition in our house—and the practical steps that kept both school and sanity intact.

## Set a Hard Cap on Weekly Hours

The biggest mistake I see parents make—and almost made myself—is letting the employer dictate the teenager's schedule. Managers naturally want reliable kids who can close the store on a school night, but high school is your teen's primary job right now. 

Before your teenager signs any employment contract, sit down together and establish a strict maximum number of work hours per week during the school year. For us, that magic number was twelve. Twelve hours meant two six-hour shifts—usually a Friday evening and a Saturday afternoon. 

When the hiring manager asked if he could work twenty hours a week, my son pointed to our family rule and negotiated twenty max. That boundary protected his study time, his track practice, and his downtime. If your teen is taking multiple AP classes, you might need to drop that cap to eight or ten hours, or limit shifts to weekends only. 

## Build a Calendar That Both of You Can See

Teenagers live in the moment. To them, a shift on Thursday night doesn't feel like it conflicts with a big history project due Friday morning until it is far too late. 

We solved this by ditching paper planners and setting up a shared digital family calendar on our phones. Every school assignment, club meeting, track meet, and work shift goes into that single calendar. 

Make it a habit to review the upcoming week every Sunday evening over dinner. Ask specific questions: "I see you work until nine on Wednesday, but your chemistry lab report is due Thursday morning. When are you planning to finish writing it?" This shifts the mental load onto them while you act as the safety net, catching potential crashes before they happen.

## Teach Them How to Say "No" to Extra Shifts

Once a teenager proves they are responsible, managers will invariably ask them to cover shifts for coworkers who call in sick. It is flattering for a sixteen-year-old to be needed, and the extra cash is tempting. 

However, this is where burnout happens fastest. Role-play this scenario with your teen before it happens in real life. Give them a script they can use without feeling guilty. 

For example, practice saying: "I’d love to help out, but I have a major math test tomorrow and I need to study tonight." Remind them that establishing boundaries early tells their employer they are reliable professionals who take their commitments—both academic and professional—seriously. 

## Protect Sleep and Downtime Like Your Job Depends On It

Sleep deprivation is the silent killer of teen productivity. If your kid works until 10:00 PM, gets home at 10:30, winds down on their phone until midnight, and has to catch a bus at 7:00 AM, their brain simply will not function in the classroom.

We instituted a non-negotiable tech curfew on work nights. Work bags, uniforms, and phones go on the kitchen counter thirty minutes after they walk through the door. 

*   Pack lunch and lay out school clothes the night before to eliminate morning chaos.
*   Block out at least one full weekend day per month where zero work and zero school obligations are allowed.
*   Keep meals consistent; grabbing fast food in the car between school and work wreaks havoc on their energy levels.

## Watch for the Hidden Warning Signs of Burnout

Teens rarely walk up and say, "I am overwhelmed and need to quit my job." Instead, burnout leaks out sideways. 

Keep an eye out for subtle behavioral shifts. Are they snapping at their siblings? Are grades that were usually Bs slipping into Ds? Are they complaining of frequent headaches or stomachaches on the days they have to go to work? 

If you spot these red flags, do not wait for the semester to end. Step in immediately to scale back their hours or talk to their manager about a temporary schedule reduction. No minimum-wage job is worth your child's mental health or high school diploma.

Helping your teenager navigate their first job is less about micromanaging their shifts and more about coaching them through the art of time management. As you weigh how to support your teen through this busy season, consider the tools and structures you can put in place to keep them on track.
