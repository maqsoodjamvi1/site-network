Last year, I found myself staring at my fifteen-year-old son’s bedroom door, wondering if I was raising a classic lazy teenager or someone quietly falling apart. He was dragging himself to the kitchen at noon, homework was piling up unstarted, and his favorite hobbies sat gathering dust. When I asked him what was wrong, the answer was always the same: "I'm just tired."

If you are reading this, you are probably seeing the same thing in your own home. Teen exhaustion has a way of hijacking family life. The tricky part is that adolescent burnout and depression often wear the exact same mask. Both leave teens sleeping twelve hours a day, snapping at family members, and losing interest in the world. 

Figuring out which one you are dealing with changes everything about how you respond. Here is how I learned to spot the difference in my own home, and what you can look for in yours.

## The Clue of the Missing Joy

When my son finally started opening up, the biggest distinction between burnout and depression became clear through his relationship with things he used to love. 

Burnout usually stems from an overload of external pressure—AP classes, varsity sports, social media demands, and the relentless pressure to perform. When a teenager is burned out, they still *want* to enjoy their hobbies, but they simply do not have the cognitive or physical energy left to do them. If you suggest doing something fun, a burned-out teen might say, "I'd love to, but I'm too drained." They might feel relief if a soccer practice or a club meeting gets canceled because their schedule finally has breathing room.

Depression, on the other hand, steals the capacity for pleasure entirely, a symptom clinicians call anhedonia. A depressed teen does not just lack the energy for their favorite video game or art project; they stop caring about it altogether. Even if their schedule is completely cleared of obligations, the emptiness remains. They are not just tired of doing things; they have lost the internal spark that makes things feel worth doing.

## Look at the Weekend Reset

One of the most practical ways I learned to tell the difference was by watching how my son responded to downtime. 

Burnout responds to rest, though it usually takes more than a single weekend to fix. If your teen is burned out, a few days of zero responsibilities—sleeping in, watching movies, and ignoring homework—will usually yield a slight lift in their mood and energy. You might see a glimmer of their usual personality peek through by Sunday evening.

Depression does not reset over the weekend. You can give a depressed teenager a week of complete relaxation, and they will wake up on Monday morning feeling just as heavy, hopeless, and exhausted as they did before. Rest does not refuel a battery that is not just depleted, but disconnected from its power source entirely.

## Watch the Internal Narrative

The words your teen uses to describe their exhaustion can give you a window into their internal world. Pay attention to how they talk about their future and their daily life.

*   **Burnout language** is usually focused on capacity and overwhelm. You will hear phrases like "I can't keep up," "There's too much to do," or "I just need a break." The frustration is directed outward at circumstances, a packed schedule, or high expectations.
*   **Depression language** is usually focused on worth and identity. You are more likely to hear phrases like "What's the point?", "I'm so stupid," "Everyone would be better off without me," or "I can't do anything right." The frustration is directed inward at themselves.

If your teenager is complaining about the sheer volume of life, you are likely looking at burnout. If they are talking about their own fundamental inadequacy or expressing a sense of total hopelessness, depression is the more likely culprit.

## Setting the Stage for Real Help

Distinguishing between these two states is not just an exercise in labeling; it dictates your next move. If it is burnout, your job is to act as a fierce protector of their schedule, helping them drop commitments and learn the art of saying no. If it is depression, your job is to connect them with a professional who can help them navigate the heavy fog they cannot outsleep or schedule-manage away.
