When I first told my friends I was booking a flight to Albania, the responses were all the same. People blinked, asked where it was on a map, and assumed I was heading into some kind of rugged, off-grid adventure. 

They were wrong. Albania is one of the most accessible, visually stunning corners of Europe, and it happens to be astonishingly cheap. If you are an American traveler used to wincing at €10 coffees in Paris or $200-a-night hotels in Rome, the Albanian Riviera and its capital, Tirana, will feel like an absolute glitch in the global economy. 

Here is what you actually need to know to plan your first budget trip to this Balkan gem, based on the practical lessons I learned on the ground.

## Getting There Without Breaking the Bank

Direct flights from the United States to Tirana International Airport (TIA) are rare, so your best bet is flying into a major European hub like Rome, Milan, or London, and booking a separate budget carrier leg over to Albania. Airlines like Wizz Air and Ryanair frequently run routes into Tirana for under $50 one-way if you book a few weeks in advance. 

Once you land, skip the taxis, which love to quote inflated flat rates to tourists. Walk just outside the terminal doors to catch the Rinas Express shuttle bus. It runs hourly straight into downtown Tirana and costs roughly 400 Albanian Lek (about $4 USD). 

## What Your Money Actually Buys

Let's talk numbers, because this is where Albania shines. The local currency is the Albanian Lek (ALL), and the exchange rate typically hovers around 90 Lek to one US dollar. 

Accommodation is remarkably inexpensive. You can easily find clean, centrally located boutique guesthouses or private Airbnb apartments in Tirana or the coastal town of Sarandë for $35 to $50 a night. If you are backpacking or traveling solo, hostel beds regularly drop below $20 a night, often including breakfast.

Food is where you will save the most money. In Tirana, a massive sit-down dinner of grilled meats, fresh local cheese, Greek-style salad, and a local Korça beer will rarely cross the $12 mark per person. If you grab a *byrek* (a flaky pastry stuffed with spinach, feta, or minced meat) from a neighborhood bakery for breakfast, it will cost you less than a dollar.

## Getting Around the Country

Renting a car offers the ultimate freedom, especially if you want to drive the breathtaking Llogara Pass down to the coast, but Albanian driving has a reputation for being chaotic. Drivers pass on blind curves, and stray livestock on mountain roads are a legitimate hazard. 

If you want to save money and spare your nerves, use the local transportation network instead. Albania doesn't have a functional train network for tourists, but it has *furgons*. These are privately owned minibuses that serve as the backbone of the country's transit system. 

Here is how you actually use a *furgon*:
*   **Find the right station:** Tirana has regional bus terminals depending on which direction you are heading (North/South). Ask your accommodation host to pinpoint the exact station on your phone.
*   **Look for the sign:** There are rarely printed schedules online. You simply walk up to the vans, look at the sign in the windshield showing the destination city (like "Sarandë" or "Berat"), and ask the driver when the next departure is.
*   **Pay cash on board:** Fares are cheap—usually $5 to $15 for intercity travel—and you pay the driver or a conductor directly in Lek once the bus gets moving. 

## Where to Go on a First-Time Itinerary

You can easily see the highlights of Albania in a 7-to-10-day trip without rushing. Focus your itinerary on three distinct areas to get a complete picture of the country.

Start in **Tirana**, a colorful, chaotic city where Ottoman minarets stand side-by-side with Italian fascist architecture and giant brutalist murals. Spend a day exploring Skanderbeg Square, visit Bunk'Art 2 (a massive nuclear bunker turned history museum right in the city center), and ride the Dajti Ekspres cable car up the mountain for panoramic views.

Next, take a *furgon* south to **Berat**, known as the "City of a Thousand Windows." It is a UNESCO World Heritage site featuring stacked white Ottoman houses climbing up a steep hillside toward an ancient castle. You can stay in a traditional stone guesthouse run by a local family for a fraction of what you would pay elsewhere in the Mediterranean.

Finish your trip on the **Albanian Riviera**, basing yourself in Himarë rather than the heavily commercialized town of Sarandë. Himarë gives you access to stunning turquoise pebble beaches, cliffside seafood tavernas, and hiking trails through ancient olive groves, all without the massive crowds or inflated prices found just across the water in neighboring Greece.
