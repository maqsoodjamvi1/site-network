If you are tired of watching your savings disappear over a long weekend in Paris or Rome, it is time to look east. A few years ago, I was hunting for a European escape that wouldn't require me to eat instant ramen for a month afterward. That search led me to the Republic of Georgia, a country tucked between the Caucasus Mountains and the Black Sea. 

What I found blew my budget assumptions out of the water. While Western Europe has become notorious for twenty-dollar cocktails and triple-digit nightly hotel rates, Georgia offers a genuinely high-end travel experience for the price of a hostel-hopping trip in Spain. By splitting your time between the capital city of Tbilisi and the coastal resort of Batumi, you get two completely different vibes without breaking the bank. Here is how to plan your first budget trip there.

## Why Tbilisi is the Ultimate Low-Cost City Break

Tbilisi feels like a cross between Eastern European grit and Mediterranean warmth, wrapped in a blanket of incredibly low prices. On my first morning, I sat at a sidewalk cafe in the historic Sololaki neighborhood and ordered a massive, boat-shaped cheese bread stuffed with an egg and butter (khachapuri) alongside a rich Turkish coffee. The total came to about $4. 

Accommodation in Tbilisi offers incredible value. Instead of booking a tiny, overpriced chain hotel room, look for independent guesthouses or Airbnb apartments in the Mtatsminda or Chugureti districts. You can easily find charming, high-ceilinged apartments with historic wooden balconies for $40 to $60 a night. 

Getting around the city costs next to nothing. You will need to buy a rechargeable MetroCard for about 50 cents, and single rides on the subway or city buses cost less than 30 cents. The Soviet-era metro system is fast, deep, and surprisingly efficient, though walking is often better because Tbilisi is a city meant to be explored on foot. 

## Head to Batumi for Cheap Beach Days and Modern Architecture

After a few days of sulfur baths and cobblestone alleyways in Tbilisi, take the high-speed train west to Batumi. The journey takes about five hours, and a second-class ticket costs roughly $9 to $12. 

Batumi sits right on the Black Sea and acts as Georgia’s summer playground. It features a strange, fascinating mix of high-rise skyscrapers, leafy parks, and old-school Soviet flair. Unlike the packed, expensive beaches of the French Riviera or the Amalfi Coast, Batumi's six-mile beachfront boulevard is completely free to access, clean, and lined with cheap cafes and rental bikes. 

You can easily keep your daily expenses low in Batumi by following a few simple rules:
*   **Eat at local *supra* spots:** Look for neighborhood spots serving khinkali (soup dumplings) where locals eat. You can fill up on these meat-filled dumplings for about 50 cents each. 
*   **Skip the beach clubs:** Bring your own towel and relax on the pebble beaches rather than paying for private loungers.
*   **Rent a city bike:** Batumi has an automated bike-share system called Velobike that costs just a couple of dollars for an hour of riding along the coastal promenade.
*   **Shop the local markets:** Hit up the Batumi Farmers Market for fresh fruit, local cheeses, and churchkhela (the regional candle-shaped candy made of grape juice and walnuts) for pennies.

## How to Handle Money, Language, and Logistics

Navigating Georgia is straightforward if you know a few practical details ahead of time. The local currency is the Georgian Lari (GEL). While credit cards are widely accepted in Tbilisi's modern restaurants and supermarkets, you will definitely need cash for taxis, local bakeries, markets, and guesthouses in smaller towns. 

Language-wise, you will find that younger Georgians generally speak good English, while older generations often speak Russian. English is widely used in all tourism sectors, menus, and transit signs, so you won't need a phrasebook to get by. 

For internet access, skip expensive international roaming plans. Walk into a Magti or Silknet shop as soon as you land at Tbilisi International Airport, show your passport, and buy a local eSIM or physical SIM card. Unlimited data for a week usually costs around $10 to $15, making it simple to pull up maps and translation apps on the go.
