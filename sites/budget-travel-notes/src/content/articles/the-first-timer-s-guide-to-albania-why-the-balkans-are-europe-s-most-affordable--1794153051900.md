When I first told my friends I was spending two weeks in Albania, most of them had the same reaction: they pulled out their phones, typed the country into Google Maps, and asked if it was safe. 

If you grew up in the US consuming 1990s action movies, the Balkans might still sound like a war zone. But the reality on the ground is entirely different. Albania is peaceful, incredibly welcoming to Americans, and easily the most affordable corner of Europe right now. 

If you want a Mediterranean beach vacation or a mountain trekking trip without spending $200 a night on a hotel and $40 on dinner, this is where you need to go. Here is what you need to know before booking your first trip.

## Why Albania Is So Cheap (And How Long It Will Last)

To understand why your dollars stretch so far here, you have to look at recent history. Albania emerged from a harsh communist isolation only in the early 1990s, and its tourism infrastructure is still catching up to heavyweights like Italy or Greece. 

Because the country uses the Albanian Lek rather than the Euro, your purchasing power is significantly higher. A budget of $50 to $70 a day will not just cover your basics here—it will let you travel comfortably. 

Prices are low because the market is still local-facing. A coffee in downtown Tirana costs about $1.20, and a massive plate of local meat and salad at a neighborhood tavern rarely goes over $8. That said, prices are rising fast along the coast as more Europeans discover the "Albanian Riviera." If you want the best deals, go sooner rather than later.

## Getting Your Bearings: Tirana and the Coast

Most international flights land at Mother Teresa Airport in Tirana, the capital. Tirana is colorful, chaotic, and fascinating. The city center is walkable, and you can spend a couple of days exploring Soviet-era bunkers turned into museums, grabbing drinks in the trendy Blloku district, and riding the Dajti Ekspres cable car up the mountain for panoramic views.

Once you are ready to see the beaches, you will need to head south. The stretch of coastline from Vlorë to Sarandë is often called the Albanian Riviera, and for good reason: turquoise water, dramatic cliffs, and pebble beaches that rival neighboring Greece at a fraction of the cost. 

To plan your route efficiently, keep these logistics in mind:
* **Transportation:** Trains are practically non-existent for tourists, so you will travel by bus (locally called *furgons*) or rental car. Furgons are dirt cheap—usually a few dollars for a multi-hour ride—but they often leave only when full, and schedules online are unreliable. Renting a car gives you total freedom, but Albanian driving is aggressive; expect lots of passing on winding two-lane mountain roads.
* **Cash is King:** While hotels in Tirana and larger coastal towns take credit cards, you will need physical Lek for buses, small guesthouses, rural bakeries, and beach loungers. Keep an ATM card with no foreign transaction fees handy and withdraw cash as soon as you arrive.
* **Language Barrier:** Older Albanians generally speak Albanian and Italian, while younger generations speak very good English. Downloading an offline Google Translate pack for Albanian will save you a lot of guesswork at rural bus stations.

## Where to Stay and What to Eat

Accommodation is where you will save the most money compared to Western Europe. Instead of chain hotels, Albania's tourism is built around family-run guesthouses (*buzeqeshje* in local hospitality, though you will mostly just book private apartments on Airbnb or Booking.com). In smaller towns like Gjirokastër or Himarë, you can easily find clean, modern apartments with balconies for $30 to $45 a night.

The food is a delicious hybrid of Mediterranean and Ottoman influences. Because much of the agriculture is still traditional and small-scale, everything tastes remarkably fresh. 

When you sit down at a local restaurant, look for *byrek* (a flaky, savory pastry stuffed with spinach, cheese, or minced meat that costs about $1), fresh grilled seafood along the coast, and *tavë kosi* (baked lamb and rice in a tangy yogurt sauce). Tipping is not mandatory the way it is in the US, but leaving a 10% cash tip for good service at sit-down restaurants is standard practice.

## Planning Your Next Steps

Putting together a budget itinerary to Albania takes a bit more hands-on planning than booking a standard resort package in Mexico or a flight to London, but the payoff is entirely worth the extra effort. Map out whether you want a beach-heavy vacation in the south or a history-and-culture trip through the interior mountain towns, lock in your flights, and you will have an unforgettable European adventure that leaves your savings account mostly intact.
