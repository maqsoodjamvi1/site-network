If you have been scrolling through travel forums looking for an affordable North African getaway, your feed is probably flooded with content about Morocco. I used to think Morocco was the default and only choice for this region too. But after spending a few weeks splitting my time between Marrakech and a coastal road trip through Tunisia, I realized that Tunisia is the better pick for budget-conscious travelers who want high value without the constant tourist pressure. 

Tunisia delivers Mediterranean beaches, massive Roman ruins, and desert landscapes at a fraction of the cost you would spend in neighboring countries. Here is what you need to know to plan your first trip.

## Why Tunisia Wins on the Budget Front

When people ask me why Tunisia is so much cheaper than Morocco, the answer comes down to tourism infrastructure. Morocco has spent decades courting high-end luxury travelers alongside backpackers, which has driven up prices for lodging, guides, and transport in popular hubs. Tunisia, on the other hand, receives far fewer Western tourists. 

This translates directly to your wallet. In Tunis or the coastal town of Sousse, I regularly paid less than $30 a night for clean, centrally located private hotel rooms that included breakfast. A traditional meal of *brik* (a crispy pastry stuffed with egg and tuna) and a hearty plate of lamb couscous at a local neighborhood spot rarely cost more than $4 to $6. 

Even better, you do not have to fight off aggressive touts just to walk down the street. The pace here is slower, meaning you can actually enjoy your mint tea at a cafe without someone trying to sell you a rug or a guided tour every two minutes.

## Getting Around Without Breaking the Bank

Transportation in Tunisia is surprisingly efficient and dirt cheap if you avoid private taxis for long distances. 

To jump between major cities like Tunis, Sousse, and Sfax, skip the private drivers and head straight for the train network operated by SNCFT. A second-class ticket between Tunis and Sousse takes about two hours and costs roughly $3. It is a great way to see the countryside and chat with locals.

For shorter hops or towns not served by the rail line, look for *louages*. These are shared white-and-red microbuses that depart from designated stations only when all seats are filled. You simply walk up to the ticket window, tell the clerk your destination, pay a few dinars, and get assigned to a van. It sounds chaotic if you have never done it, but the system runs like clockwork once you get the hang of it.

## Must-See Stops for a First-Time Itinerary

You can easily see a massive variety of landscapes and history in a single week without spending a fortune. If I were planning a first trip again, I would structure it around these three stops:

*   **Tunis and Sidi Bou Said:** Spend a couple of days exploring the ancient Medina of Tunis, a massive UNESCO World Heritage site filled with spice markets and artisan workshops. Afterward, take the TGM commuter train a few miles north to Sidi Bou Said, the famous cliffside village of blue and white houses overlooking the Mediterranean. The train ride costs less than a dollar.
*   **El Jem:** Skip the crowded Colosseum in Rome and head south to El Jem. This is one of the best-preserved Roman amphitheaters in the entire world. Entry costs about $4, and unlike Rome, you can actually wander down into the underground chambers where gladiators and lions waited before entering the arena, with almost nobody else around.
*   **Tozeur and the Desert Oases:** If you want a taste of the Sahara without paying for a luxury desert camp, take an overnight bus or train down to Tozeur. Here, you can rent a bicycle to tour the massive date palm oases or take a shared transport out to the nearby mountain oases of Chebika and Tamerza. 

## Practical Money and Safety Tips

Before you book your flights, keep a few logistical realities in mind. The Tunisian Dinar is a closed currency, which means you cannot buy it outside of the country. You will need to bring euros, US dollars, or British pounds in cash to exchange at banks or authorized bureaus upon arrival, or withdraw dinars directly from local ATMs. Keep your receipts, as you will need them if you want to exchange any leftover dinars back into foreign currency before flying home.

As for safety, Tunisia is generally very secure for independent travelers, and violent crime against tourists is rare. The biggest hassle you will face is minor scams, such as someone offering to "help" you navigate the Medina only to demand an exorbitant tip at the end. A polite, firm "la, shukran" (no, thank you) and walking away with confidence solves this every time.
