When I realized I was drowning in venue contracts and catering proposals six months before my wedding, I did what a lot of overwhelmed couples do: I hired a partial planner. 

Most wedding planners pitch packages for full-service coordination from day one, or just month-of management where they swoop in thirty days before you walk down the aisle. But stepping in right at the six-month mark is a unique sweet spot. You have likely booked the big-ticket items—the venue, the photographer, and maybe the band—leaving a massive mountain of logistical details to climb. 

Stepping into a partial-planning contract midway through the process requires a very specific game plan. Here is the exact timeline and checklist I used to catch up, get organized, and make the most of my planner's expertise before the big day.

## Month Six: The Hand-Off and Audit

Your first official month with a partial planner should feel less like planning and more like a forensic audit. When my planner and I had our kickoff call, we didn't talk about napkin colors or floral arrangements. We went through every single vendor contract I had already signed.

Your planner needs to review these documents to spot hidden traps. For instance, my venue contract stated that all outside rentals had to be loaded out by midnight, but my band's contract showed they played until 11:30 PM. Without a professional catching that turnaround time, we would have faced heavy penalty fees. 

During this month, your action items are:
*   Forward every signed contract, deposit receipt, and vendor email thread to your planner.
*   Set up a shared digital dashboard (like Google Drive, Aisle Planner, or Notion) for all future documents.
*   Map out your remaining budget down to the exact dollar, accounting for taxes and gratuities that weren't in initial estimates.

## Month Five: Tying Up the Major Vendor Loose Ends

With your baseline established, month five is about filling the gaps. By the six-month mark, you usually have the venue and photo/video locked down, but you are likely missing essential puzzle pieces like hair and makeup artists, transportation, and cake bakers.

Because you only have a partial planner, they won't typically give you a blank slate of twenty options. Instead, ask them for their top three preferred vendors in each missing category who fit your aesthetic and your remaining budget. 

This is also the time to nail down your rental company. If you need specialty chairs, upgraded plates, or a specific lounge furniture setup, you must book these now. Waiting until month three or four means popular inventory will already be spoken for.

## Month Four: The Design and Menu Pivot

By month four, the structural bones of the wedding are set, and it is time to focus on the guest experience. This is where a partial planner earns their keep by translating your Pinterest boards into a cohesive floor plan and design deck.

Schedule your catering tasting this month. When you go, bring your planner if they are local and available. They will ask critical questions you might forget in a food coma—like asking the caterer how many dietary restriction plates they can comfortably execute simultaneously, or where their prep kitchen will actually be set up at the venue.

Once the food is squared away, finalize your rental orders for linens, glassware, and lighting. Your planner will use your venue's physical dimensions to draft a seating layout, ensuring guests aren't crammed too tightly near the bar or blocked by awkward structural pillars.

## Month Three: Paper Goods and Guest Logistics

Ninety days out is the official danger zone for paper goods. If you are mailing traditional paper invitations, they need to go out six to eight weeks before the wedding (or ten weeks if you have a lot of international or out-of-town guests). 

Work with your planner to finalize your invitation suite, and order your day-of stationery at the same time. Ordering your menus, place cards, and welcome signs simultaneously often saves you a rush-shipping fee from your printer. 

This month also requires tackling guest logistics:
*   Finalize your hotel room blocks and confirm the courtesy rate expiration dates.
*   Book shuttle buses or limos for wedding party transportation.
*   Draft the rough draft of your guest seating chart based on your RSVP expectations.

## Month Two: Final Walkthroughs and Vendor Confirmations

Sixty days out is when the heavy lifting shifts from creative decisions to operational execution. You and your planner will schedule an on-site walkthrough at your venue. Walk through the exact rain-plan path, the ceremony processional line, and where the vendor load-in zone is located.

During month two, your planner should take over direct communication with your vendors. Hand over the reigns so that florists, DJs, and photographers start sending their final timeline questions to your planner instead of your personal cell phone. 

You should also apply for your marriage license this month, keeping in mind the specific county rules regarding waiting periods and expiration dates.

## Month One: The Final Polish

The final thirty days are all about assembling the puzzle. Your planner will build the master timeline, minute by minute, detailing everything from when the bride sits in the hair chair to when the getaway car pulls away.

Put together your final vendor payment and tip envelopes in cash or pre-written checks, and assign a trusted family member or your planner to hand them out on the wedding day. Pack your personal items—guest book, cake cutting set, favors, and emergency kit—into labeled plastic bins so nothing gets lost in the chaos of moving from home to hotel to venue.

Hiring a partial planner halfway through the marathon of wedding planning can feel a bit like jumping onto a moving treadmill, but following a strict chronological checklist keeps the momentum going.
