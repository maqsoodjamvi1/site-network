Planning a wedding is stressful for anyone, but if you work by the clock, punch a timecard, or rely on a union contract, the logistics get a lot more complicated. Standard wedding advice usually assumes you have a flexible desk job with unlimited paid time off. If you are an electrician, transit worker, nurse, auto worker, or municipal employee, you know that is not how your world works. Seniority rules, vacation bids, and rigid shift coverage dictate your calendar. 

When my spouse and I planned our wedding while navigating union shift work, we quickly realized we couldn't just throw a dart at a calendar date. We had to treat our time-off requests like a major project. Here is the practical timeline and step-by-step checklist we used to secure the exact dates we needed without losing our minds or our standing at work.

## Twelve Months Out: Check the CBA and Seniority Bids

Long before you look at venues or cake tastings, open your Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA). Every union contract handles vacation time differently, and you need to know the rules of the road before you get your heart set on a specific Saturday. 

Look specifically at the annual vacation selection process. Many union shops run a seniority-based bidding system for prime time off, usually occurring in the late fall or early winter for the upcoming year. If you are low on the seniority totem pole for your specific classification, you need to know when the open-to-all residual vacation bidding starts so you can pounce on unchosen weeks.

Talk informally to your shop steward or union rep about your plans. They can give you the unwritten reality of your department—such as whether summer weekends are impossible to get off due to mandatory overtime, or if certain shifts are easier to trade. 

## Nine Months Out: Map Your Blackout Dates and Shift Rotations

Once you have a general season in mind, map your actual work calendar against potential wedding dates. If you work a rotating schedule (like four days on, two days off, or a swing shift), map out where your weekends fall months in advance. 

You will need time off for more than just the wedding day itself. Mark down these crucial windows on your personal calendar:
*   The rehearsal and rehearsal dinner (often a Thursday or Friday night)
*   The wedding day and recovery day
*   Your honeymoon travel window
*   The week leading up to the wedding for last-minute DIY projects, marriage license acquisition, and family arrivals

If your rotation puts you squarely on a mandatory double-shift or a graveyard rotation the Thursday before your Saturday wedding, you are setting yourself up for pure exhaustion. Use this nine-month mark to identify these conflicts early so you can plan your shift trades.

## Six Months Out: Submit Formal Requests and Line Up Trades

At the six-month mark, it is time to put pen to paper and submit your formal leave requests according to company policy. Do not rely on a verbal OK from a supervisor; get it in writing through your employer's HR portal or union request form. 

If your formal vacation request gets denied because too many people are already off that week, this is where your union network saves the day. Start talking to your coworkers about shift trading. 

When approaching a coworker for a trade, make it as easy as possible for them. Look for colleagues who might want the specific days you are working, or offer to cover a less desirable shift for them in return down the road. Keep your direct supervisor in the loop on any official shift swaps to ensure they comply with local labor laws and rest-period regulations. 

## Three Months Out: Handle the Hard Logistics and Contingency Plans

By three months out, your vacation time should be officially approved in writing. If it is still pending, schedule a meeting with your department head or HR representative to get a final answer. 

If a manager tries to pull your approved vacation due to "operational needs"—a common headache in manufacturing, healthcare, and transit—this is the time to involve your shop steward. Show them your written approval timestamp. Your contract is your shield here.

Simultaneously, build a backup plan for work-related stress. Wedding planning requires phone calls, vendor meetings, and final walkthroughs. If you work long shifts, block out specific off-days well in advance for these tasks so you are not trying to haggle with a florist over the phone during a 15-minute coffee break on the factory floor.

## One Month Out: Finalize Coverage and Disconnect

As the big day gets closer, finalize your coverage details. If you traded shifts with a coworker, double-check that their name is officially on the schedule for your assigned days so you do not get hit with an unexpected no-show flag. 

More importantly, set firm boundaries with your work phone or email if your job requires you to stay connected. Union jobs often require hard stops at the end of a shift, and your wedding week is the ultimate time to enforce that rule. 

Juggling a union job and wedding planning takes extra legwork, but treating your time-off process with the same structure you bring to your job makes all the difference. As you get ready to finalize your timeline and submit your requests, take a moment to look at how your specific contract handles personal days and emergency leave just in case the unexpected pops up.
