Planning a wedding often feels like a steep climb during the first two months. You got engaged, celebrated with family, booked the big venue, and realized that a piece of paper now costs more than your first car. Now you are staring down a twelve-to-eighteen-month calendar, wondering if you need professional help, but you aren't sure you want to hand over the keys to a full-service planner for the entire journey. 

I learned through my own wedding planning trials that you do not have to choose between doing everything yourself and hiring someone to manage the whole process from scratch. Month three is the sweet spot for hiring a fractional coordinator—often called a "month-of" or "day-of" coordinator, though they usually start working with you four to six weeks before the wedding. 

Bringing someone in at month three bridges the gap between the big-picture vision you locked down early and the granular logistics that pop up later. Here is how to use month three to vet, hire, and onboard your coordinator so you can actually enjoy the middle of your engagement.

## Assess Where Your Budget Actually Stands

By month three, you have likely received official pricing from your venue, caterer, and photographer. These three line items typically consume sixty to seventy percent of your total budget. Before you reach out to coordinators, pull up your spreadsheet and look at what cash you have left. 

Partial or month-of coordination generally costs between fifteen hundred and four thousand dollars, depending on your geographic market and the complexity of your event. If your remaining budget makes that price tag tight, you need to know now. Do not wait until month six to realize you cannot afford professional help. Cut back on floral minimums or skip the late-night taco truck now so you can protect budget for the person who will actually keep your wedding day running on time.

## Determine Your Coordination Blind Spots

Every couple has different strengths. My partner is a master logistics scheduler, but I am terrible at visualizing spatial layouts. Knowing your weaknesses dictates the exact type of coordinator you need to look for during month three.

Make a quick list of what is stressing you out right now. If the thought of reading standard catering contracts makes your eyes glaze over, you need a coordinator who offers contract review as part of their onboarding. If you are bringing in a tent, generator, and portable restrooms to a blank-slate family property, a standard "month-of" coordinator who only handles timelines won't cut it; you need someone who specializes in logistics-heavy outdoor venues and starts working with you at least three months out. 

## Run the Interview Circuit Like a Hiring Manager

By month three, you have enough concrete details to give coordinators a clear picture of your wedding. When you jump on discovery calls, skip the vague questions about their style and get straight to operational details. 

Ask them how many weddings they personally execute per weekend during peak season. If the answer is more than two, run the other way—one coordinator cannot be in two places at once. Ask them what software they use for floor plans and timelines. If they still use paper templates and hand-drawn seating charts, you might struggle with communication later. Finally, ask for a sample timeline they built for a wedding similar to yours. Looking at a real schedule will show you immediately if they understand how long hair and makeup actually takes for an eight-person bridal party.

## Hand Over the Reins and Set the Communication Rhythm

Once you sign a contract and pay your deposit in month three, resist the urge to drop all communication until thirty days before the wedding. The best fractional coordinators prefer a structured handover. 

Schedule an initial onboarding call for late in month three. Send them your master spreadsheet, every signed vendor contract, and a running list of design ideas. A good coordinator will review these documents and immediately spot red flags—like a photographer who only has thirty minutes scheduled for family portraits, or a caterer who hasn't accounted for vendor meals. Getting these items flagged in month three gives you plenty of time to adjust contracts and talk to vendors, rather than scrambling the week of the wedding.
