When I started pricing out my own wedding catering, I fell into the classic brunch trap. I saw "$35 per person for a breakfast buffet" and thought I had discovered a brilliant loophole to save thousands of dollars. Then I actually started reading the fine print of the catering proposals, and the math got a lot more complicated. 

If you are trying to decide whether a morning wedding with scrambled eggs and waffles will actually save you money compared to a traditional evening steak dinner, you have to look past the per-person sticker price. Catering companies structure daytime events and evening events entirely differently, and understanding those mechanics will save you from a nasty surprise.

## The Per-Person Trap: Why $35 Isn't $35

The most attractive part of brunch catering is the baseline food cost. Standard dinner catering often starts around $75 to $150 per person for a plated meal, while breakfast and brunch menus frequently sit between $30 and $60 per person. 

However, per-person pricing never tells the whole story. When you look at an itemized quote, you have to factor in the hidden multipliers that apply to any catering job, regardless of the time of day:
*   **Service and Labor Fees:** You still need chefs, prep cooks, and servers. Even if a buffet requires fewer servers than a plated dinner, labor is calculated by the hour, not by the egg.
*   **Administrative and Production Fees:** Most caterers tack on an 18% to 22% service charge right out of the gate.
*   **Sales Tax and Gratuity:** Depending on your state, food, service charges, and alcohol are all taxed differently, which can instantly add 10% or more to your bottom line.

Let's look at a realistic scenario. You have 100 guests, and your brunch buffet is priced at $40 per person. You might assume your food bill is $4,000. Once you add a 20% service fee ($800), local tax ($400), and staff gratuity ($500), your actual cost is closer to $5,700—and that is before you add coffee service, rentals, or a single drop of alcohol.

## Dinner Minimums: The Hidden Risk of Daytime Events

While per-person pricing looks friendly, dinner minimums are where venues and caterers protect their profit margins. Many popular wedding venues enforce a "Food and Beverage Minimum." This means that even if you host an event that ends at 2:00 PM, the venue requires you to spend a minimum amount—say, $10,000—on food and drinks.

This is where a brunch wedding can actually backfire financially. 

Imagine booking a premier venue with a $12,000 food and beverage minimum. If you have 100 guests attending your brunch, and your fancy pancake and frittata spread costs $50 per person, your total food spend is only $5,000. You are now on the hook to spend another $7,000. 

Unless you are buying top-shelf liquor for a daytime crowd, keeping an open mimosa bar flowing for hours, or upgrading every single rental item to luxury status, hitting a high minimum with breakfast food alone is surprisingly difficult. If you fail to meet the minimum, the venue simply pockets the difference as a fee anyway. Always ask for the venue's minimum spend requirements *before* you fall in love with their brunch menu.

## Alcohol Math: Mimosas vs. Open Bars

One area where brunch genuinely saves money is the bar tab. At a standard evening wedding, guests expect heavy pours of wine, craft beer, and spirits for four to five hours, which can easily average $30 to $50 per guest.

At a brunch wedding, the drinking culture changes. People generally drink less hard liquor in the morning and early afternoon. A well-stocked station featuring a prosecco-and-juice mimosa bar, a bloody mary station, and a few local beers will satisfy most crowds at a fraction of the cost of a full liquor license. 

Be careful with consumption-based bar tabs, though. Caterers often charge by the bottle or by the glass, and guests can easily surprise you by treating a 1:00 PM wedding reception with the same enthusiasm as a Saturday night gala. If you want predictable costs, opt for a flat-fee per-person package that caps out after two or three hours.

## How to Calculate Your Real Brunch Budget

Before you sign a catering contract, run through a practical audit of your actual guest count and venue rules. Do not rely on the baseline menu price to make your final decision.

Start by asking your caterer for a comprehensive estimate that includes taxes, service charges, equipment rentals, and staffing costs. If the venue has a minimum spend, calculate whether your guest count is high enough to naturally absorb that cost through a brunch menu, or if you will be forced to pad the bill with expensive add-ons you do not actually want.
