When I planned my own wedding, I quickly realized that while everyone talks about the 12-month wedding timeline, the rehearsal dinner is often treated as an afterthought. Couples usually scramble to pull it together in the final month. To save your sanity and actually enjoy the night before your wedding, you need a separate, structured countdown for the rehearsal dinner. 

Here is the exact practical timeline I wish I had from day one, broken down by months and weeks so you know precisely when to book your spaces, lock down your menu, and send out those invitations.

## 6 to 9 Months Out: Set the Budget and Draft the Guest List

Before you look at a single restaurant or backyard venue, you need to answer two questions: Who is paying, and who is coming? 

Traditionally, the groom's family hosts and pays for the rehearsal dinner, but modern couples often split the cost or pay for it themselves. Have an honest conversation early about the budget. If you have $1,000, that changes your options drastically compared to a $4,000 budget. 

Once you have a budget, draft your guest list. The golden rule of rehearsal dinners is that anyone who has to travel to the rehearsal and stand up at the altar—plus their partners—must be invited. This includes your wedding party, officiant, and immediate family. 

However, you also need to decide if you are hosting an "extended family and out-of-town guest" dinner, which can easily balloon your guest list from 15 people to 60 people. You need this headcount before you can book a venue.

## 4 to 5 Months Out: Book the Venue and Choose the Vibe

Finding a venue is usually the hardest part because rehearsal dinner spaces book up just as fast as wedding venues, especially during peak spring and fall wedding seasons. 

Think about the vibe you want. Do you want a relaxed backyard barbecue, a private room at your favorite local Italian restaurant, or a catered casual space at a local brewery? 

*   **Restaurant private rooms:** Easiest option. Food, alcohol, and staff are built-in, but ask about minimum spend requirements.
*   **Casual outdoor spaces:** Renting a park pavilion or backyard space requires you to hire separate caterers, tables, and porta-potties.
*   **Hotel hospitality suites:** Great if a large chunk of your guests are staying at the same hotel, though food options can feel limited.

Aim to sign a contract and put down a deposit by the four-month mark so you do not have to stress about availability later.

## 2 to 3 Months Out: Design and Send the Invitations

A common etiquette mistake is assuming wedding invitations cover the rehearsal dinner. They do not. 

Two to three months before the wedding, send separate rehearsal dinner invitations. You can do this digitally via platforms like Paperless Post, or order physical inserts to match your wedding invitation suite if you are mailing them out early. 

Your invitation needs to include the exact start time for the rehearsal itself (which usually happens an hour or two before the dinner at the actual ceremony venue), the address of the dinner location, and the dress code. Spelling out the dress code clearly—whether it is "casual denim" or "cocktail attire"—saves your guests from texting you panicked questions during wedding week.

## 6 Weeks Out: Finalize the Menu and Dietary Restrictions

By six weeks out, most of your RSVPs should be rolling in. Contact your venue or caterer to give a preliminary headcount and lock in your menu. 

This is the time to cross-reference your guest list with any dietary restrictions. If one of your bridesmaids has a severe gluten allergy or your future father-in-law is vegan, you need to make sure the restaurant can accommodate them with more than just a house side salad. If you are doing a set family-style menu, request a dedicated plated alternative for anyone with strict dietary needs.

## 2 Weeks Out: Confirm the Timeline and Final Headcount

Two weeks before the big day, submit your final, locked-in headcount to the venue. Most caterers charge based on this final number, so you will want to track down any lingering non-responders. 

At this point, also confirm the logistics. Who is bringing the wedding rings to the rehearsal? Who is bringing the marriage license? Who is giving speeches at the dinner? (Pro tip: Keep rehearsal dinner speeches short, and limit them to the hosts, the parents, and maybe the maid of honor and best man). Write down a quick run-of-show so the restaurant manager and your wedding party know what to expect.

## Wedding Week: Breathe and Hand Off the Details

By the time wedding week arrives, your rehearsal dinner planning should be entirely done. 

Print out a few copies of the seating chart if you are assigning seats, put the final payment and vendor tips into labeled envelopes, and hand those envelopes to a trusted family member or your day-of coordinator. On the night of the dinner, leave the logistics behind, eat some good food, and focus on catching up with the out-of-town relatives you haven't seen in months.
