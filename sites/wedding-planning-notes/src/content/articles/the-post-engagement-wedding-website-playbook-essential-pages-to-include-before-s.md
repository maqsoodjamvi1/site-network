When you get engaged, everyone tells you to immediately pick a date and find a venue. But as someone who went through the frantic scramble of planning a wedding, I quickly realized the very first thing you actually need to do is build your wedding website. 

Why? Because the moment you whisper to your mom or post a ring selfie, people are going to start asking questions. *When is it? Where is it? Can I bring my new partner?* You do not want to answer those questions individually fifty times. 

More importantly, your wedding website needs to be live *before* your save-the-dates hit anyone's mailbox. Your save-the-date cards will have your URL on them, and the second guests pull them out of the envelope, they are going to type that web address into their phones. If they land on a blank page or a password-protected site with no information, they will text you. Here is the exact playbook I used to build a useful, stress-saving wedding website before our save-the-dates went out.

## The Welcome Page and the Non-Negotiable Core Details

Keep your homepage simple. You don't need a sprawling, romantic novel about how you met just yet (save that for the "Our Story" page). 

When guests land on your homepage, they need to see four pieces of information immediately: your names, the wedding date, the city and state, and a countdown clock if you like that sort of thing. If you are getting married in a small town like Traverse City, Michigan, instead of a major hub like Chicago, spell out the region so out-of-town guests can start mentally budgeting their travel time.

If you have secured your venue, name it here. If you are still waiting on a final contract, just list the city. The goal of the homepage is to answer the immediate logistical question: *Am I free on this weekend, and where am I going?*

## The Schedule of Events Page

One of the best pieces of feedback I received from my guests was that our schedule page saved them a ton of anxiety. People want to know what they are committing to before they book flights. 

You do not need minute-by-minute itineraries yet, but you should outline the major anchor events. Break this down clearly:
* **Friday night:** Let guests know if there is a welcome party, whether it is open to everyone or just the bridal party, and if dinner will be served.
* **Saturday (Wedding Day):** Give a realistic estimate of when the ceremony starts (e.g., "Late afternoon, approximately 4:00 PM") and when the reception wraps up so guests know when they can head home or to the after-party.
* **Sunday morning:** If you are hosting a farewell brunch, state the time window (like 9:00 AM to 11:00 AM) so people can plan their departure flights accordingly.

Putting this up early stops guests from booking early morning Sunday flights and then panicking when they realize they are missing your breakfast.

## The Travel and Lodging Page

If you wait until invitations go out to post hotel information, your guests will likely miss out on room blocks. Hotels typically require you to set up room blocks months in advance, and those links need to live on your website before save-the-dates drop.

On your travel page, list two or three hotel blocks at different price points. We included a boutique hotel downtown, a standard mid-range hotel right off the highway, and an Airbnb-heavy neighborhood recommendation for families. 

Also, include airport logistics. If your venue is an hour away from the nearest major airport, explicitly state that. Tell guests whether they will need a rental car, if Uber is reliable in the area, or if you will be providing shuttle service from a specific hotel block. 

## The Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) Page

This is your defense shield against repetitive text messages. Anticipate the awkward questions and answer them politely in writing before anyone has to ask you directly. 

Address the dress code clearly. Instead of just saying "cocktail," give context: "Our ceremony is on grass, so you may want to skip stiletto heels." Address children: if it is an adults-only affair, state that kindly right away. Address plus-ones by explaining how the RSVP system will handle them (e.g., "Your specific invited guests will appear when you type your name into the RSVP tab"). 

By answering these logistics early, you save yourself weeks of individual back-and-forth conversations right when work and wedding planning overlap.

## The RSVP and Registry Pages (What to Do If They Aren't Ready)

You might be wondering how to launch a website before your registry is finished or before you are ready to collect RSVPs. 

For the registry, it is completely fine to leave the page live with a simple, polite placeholder note: *"We are currently curating our registry items. Please check back here soon, but your presence is the only gift we need right now!"* Guests will understand that the save-the-dates went out ahead of the final registry details.

For the RSVP page, leave it locked or disabled until you mail the actual formal invitations (usually six to eight weeks before the wedding). Your save-the-date should only direct people to the website for travel, schedule, and hotel information—not to RSVP. 

Taking the time to build out these foundational pages before your save-the-dates hit the mail will make your entire planning process smoother.
