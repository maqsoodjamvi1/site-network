When my partner and I decided to buy our own wedding alcohol to save a few thousand dollars, we thought the hardest part would be estimating how much wine and beer 120 guests would actually drink. We were wrong. The real hurdle wasn't the math; it was the sheer operational chaos of getting fifty cases of glass bottles from a suburban garage to a remote barn venue four hours before the ceremony, keeping it all cold without a commercial walk-in cooler, and managing the bartenders without getting shut down by the venue coordinator. 

If you are planning a BYOB wedding, you are essentially acting as a commercial beverage distributor for one afternoon. Venues love to say "yes" to BYOB because it saves them staffing headaches, but they slap strict liability rules on you to protect their liquor license. Here is the exact playbook I wish I had on hand to handle the logistics, chilling, and serving of alcohol without losing your deposit or your sanity.

## Decoding Venue Rules and Liability Insurance

Before you buy a single bottle of tequila, read your venue contract line by line regarding alcohol. Venues generally fall into two camps: those that allow you to bring everything, and those that require you to supply the booze while forcing you to hire *their* licensed bartender. 

If your venue allows you to hire an independent bartender, you must ensure that person carries General Liability and Liquor Liability insurance. Most traditional venues will not let anyone pour alcohol on their property without a certificate of insurance naming them as additionally insured. Furthermore, ask your venue about corkage fees versus flat service fees. If a venue charges a $15 corkage fee per bottle of wine, your BYOB savings might instantly evaporate. 

Never try to save money by having a DIY open bar where guests serve themselves. Aside from the obvious liability if someone drives home intoxicated, most event insurance policies and venue contracts explicitly ban self-service alcohol. 

## The Transport and Storage Strategy

Glass is heavy, fragile, and takes up an absurd amount of space. For 100 guests, you are looking at roughly 350 to 400 total drinks, which translates to multiple heavy cardboard boxes that will sag if they get damp. 

Do not put cases of wine and liquor in the trunk of a sedan during July in Georgia; the heat will literally cook the wine and pop the corks. Borrow an SUV or a pickup truck, or rent a cargo van for the weekend. Lay down heavy moving blankets or rubber mats so the boxes do not slide around and smash into each other during sharp turns. 

Store your haul in a temperature-controlled garage or spare room at least 48 hours before the wedding. Never put beer or white wine directly into ice until the day of the event; warm beer put into ice will melt the ice in twenty minutes, leaving you with lukewarm cans floating in tepid water.

## The Chilling Matrix: How to Keep It Cold Without a Fridge

Unless your venue has a commercial kitchen, you will have to engineer your own refrigeration system. The biggest amateur mistake is underestimating how much ice you need. The rule of thumb for a summer wedding is at least one to 1.5 pounds of ice per guest, *just* for chilling drinks, plus extra for actual in-glass consumption.

To keep everything crisp, organize your cooling station into a timeline:
* **The Night Before:** Move all white wine, champagne, and half of the beer into large galvanized steel tubs or rented portable chest freezers. 
* **Morning Of (6 Hours Before):** Fill your tubs with a 50/50 mix of water and ice. Water conducts coldness much faster than air, meaning cans and bottles submerged in an ice-water bath will chill in 20 minutes, whereas sitting in a dry ice chest takes hours.
* **Backup Stash:** Keep your backup booze in shaded coolers with tightly closed lids. Do not open these coolers until the active bar tubs start running dry.

## Streamlining the Bar Setup for Quick Service

If your bartender is standing there opening twist-off beer bottles one by one, your bar line will snake across the dance floor. Set your bar up for maximum efficiency by doing the grunt work beforehand. 

If you are serving signature cocktails, pre-mix them in large, food-grade drink dispensers or glass carboys the day before. Label them clearly so guests are not guessing whether the orange liquid is screwdrivers or margaritas. For wine, pull all the corks or switch to screw-cap bottles where possible—your bartender's wrists will thank you by the end of the night.

Designate a specific friend or family member—definitely not you, and preferably not in the wedding party—to manage the empty bottle disposal and ice replenishment. Venue trash cans fill up fast with heavy glass, and an overflowing recycling bin ruins the aesthetic of a clean bar area.
