Bringing home a new puppy is an incredible experience, but it also comes with a crash course in veterinary medicine. When I brought my golden retriever mix, Barnaby, home at eight weeks old, I thought the hardest part would be crate training. Instead, our second night together involved a frantic call to the emergency vet over roundworms that looked like strands of spaghetti on the living room rug. 

Puppies and parasites go hand-in-hand. Almost every puppy is born with worms or contracts them from their mother's milk. Dealing with this is simply part of the initiation rite for new dog owners. Knowing what to look for, sticking to a strict timeline, and working closely with your vet will keep your home clean and your puppy growing strong.

## Why Almost Every Puppy Has Parasites

Mother dogs can pass roundworms and hookworms to their puppies in the womb and through their milk. Because a puppy's immune system is still developing, their bodies cannot fight off these invaders on their own. 

Intestinal parasites live off the nutrients your puppy is supposed to be absorbing. Left unchecked, a heavy worm burden can cause severe weight loss, a dull coat, a potbellied appearance, and dangerous anemia—especially in smaller breeds. Outside of worms, single-celled parasites like Giardia and Coccidia lurk in damp soil and puddles, causing explosive, foul-smelling diarrhea that can quickly dehydrate a tiny dog.

## The First-Time Owner's Deworming Timeline

Deworming is not a one-and-done event. Because parasites have life cycles involving eggs, larvae, and adults, a single dose of medication will only kill what is currently active in the gut. 

Here is the general protocol most veterinarians follow for a healthy puppy:

*   **2 to 8 Weeks:** Puppies typically receive their first deworming treatments from their breeder or shelter every two weeks, starting at two weeks of age. 
*   **8 to 12 Weeks:** When you bring your puppy home, your first vet visit should include a fresh stool sample. Your vet will prescribe a broad-spectrum dewormer tailored to your puppy's weight.
*   **3 to 6 Months:** Monthly heartworm preventatives are usually started around 8 weeks of age. Most modern heartworm medications also include broad-spectrum protection against roundworms and hookworms, covering your bases month-to-month.
*   **Adult Maintenance:** Once your dog reaches adulthood, your vet will test their stool annually during their wellness exam and adjust parasite preventatives based on your geographic location and lifestyle.

## Red Flag Symptoms to Watch For

Sometimes, parasites are obvious. Other times, they hide beneath the surface until your puppy becomes genuinely sick. 

Keep an eye out for these practical warning signs around your house:

*   **The "Spaghetti" Test:** Seeing white, stringy worms in your puppy's stool or vomit is a dead giveaway for roundworms. 
*   **Scooting or Itching:** If your puppy is constantly dragging their rear end across the carpet, tapeworms (often transmitted by fleas) or full anal glands might be the culprit.
*   **Stool Changes:** Normal puppy stool should be firm and easy to pick up. Soft-serve consistency, mucus-covered poop, or stools with streaks of bright red blood mean it is time to collect a sample in a Ziploc bag and call the clinic.
*   **Lethargy and Potbelly:** If your puppy eats ravenously but still looks round and swollen around the middle while failing to gain proper muscle weight, internal parasites are stealing their food.

## Vet Protocols and Your First Clinic Visit

Do not rely solely on over-the-counter dewormers from the pet store aisle. These medications target specific worms and can be dangerous if dosed incorrectly based on a puppy's rapid weight gain. 

When you go to your first vet appointment, bring the freshest stool sample you can manage. Vets perform a fecal flotation test, mixing the stool with a special solution that causes parasite eggs to float to the top so they can be identified under a microscope. 

If your puppy tests positive for Giardia—which is notoriously difficult to clear—your vet will likely prescribe a multi-day course of medication alongside strict environmental hygiene. You will need to wash your puppy's bedding daily and pick up poop immediately to prevent reinfection, because puppies can easily reinfect themselves by sniffing or licking contaminated grass in the backyard.
