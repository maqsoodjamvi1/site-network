Bringing home a new puppy is thrilling, but the moment those tiny needle-like teeth sink into your favorite ankle boots, reality sets in. Teething is a completely normal biological phase, but it can test the patience of even the most dedicated pet parent. When my golden retriever mix, Buster, was four months old, he treated my living room rug like an all-you-can-chew buffet. I learned quickly that telling a puppy "no" does nothing if you don't give them a better option. 

Managing puppy chewing isn't about stopping the behavior entirely—that goes against their nature. Instead, it is about redirecting that relentless urge toward legal targets while protecting your fingers and furniture. Here is the practical checklist I wish I had on day one.

## Stock Your Arsenal with the Right Textures

Puppies chew because their adult teeth are pushing through sensitive, inflamed gums. Just like human babies, they seek out items that offer counter-pressure and cool relief. If you only buy one type of toy, your puppy will likely get bored and find a shoe instead. 

You need a variety of textures stationed in every room where your puppy spends time. Look for firm rubber toys that you can freeze—stuffing them with plain Greek yogurt or wet dog food and freezing them overnight provides absolute heaven for sore gums. Soft plush toys are great for gentle cuddling, but keep an eye on them so your puppy doesn't shred and swallow the squeaker. Rope toys are also fantastic for dental hygiene because the fibers act like a toothbrush as the puppy gnaws.

## Master the "Trade, Don't Chase" Rule

When your puppy locks their jaws onto something valuable, like a TV remote or a wool sock, your first instinct is probably to lunge and pry it out of their mouth. Resist this urge. To a puppy, lunging looks like a high-stakes game of keep-away, and it often turns destructive chewing into a runaway attention-seeking habit.

Instead, practice the trade. Walk over calmly, hold a high-value treat or a favorite squeaky toy right in front of their nose, and say "trade" or "give." The second they drop the forbidden item to grab the treat, praise them heavily and give them the treat. Eventually, they will learn that bringing you an object results in a reward rather than a struggle.

## Puppy-Proof Your Floor-Level Environment

Management is your best friend during the teething months. If your puppy cannot reach forbidden items, they cannot form the habit of chewing them. Walk through your home on your hands and knees—literally get down to puppy eye level—to spot hazards you might otherwise miss.

Keep laundry off the bathroom floor, tuck phone chargers behind heavy furniture, and put away low-hanging house plants (many common houseplants are toxic to dogs anyway). If you cannot supervise your puppy directly, use baby gates to keep them in a designated, puppy-proofed zone, or use a secure crate. Freedom in the house is an earned privilege that grows as your puppy learns the house rules.

## Set Up an Immediate Redirection Routine

When your puppy starts chewing on the coffee table leg or your pant cuff, you need a split-second response plan. Do not yell or physically punish them, as this only creates fear. 

* Interrupt the behavior with a sharp, neutral sound like a quick "Ah-ah!" or a hand clap.
* The moment they pause and look at you, immediately offer an approved chew toy.
* Once they take the toy into their mouth, offer a calm, quiet "good dog" and let them settle into gnawing on the appropriate item.

Consistency is the secret ingredient here. If you redirect them ten times in a row, the message eventually starts to stick.

## Know When It Is Teething and When It Is Boredom

Puppy chewing changes character depending on the root cause. Teething chewing is usually accompanied by excessive drooling, a distinct drop in appetite for hard kibble, and frantic gnawing on anything cold or hard. Boredom chewing, on the other hand, looks more destructive and calculated—shredding couch cushions, digging at carpets, or systematically chewing drywall corners.

If your puppy is chewing out of boredom, toys alone won't solve the problem. They need more physical exercise suited to their age and breed, as well as mental stimulation like snuffle mats, puzzle feeders, or basic obedience training sessions that tire out their rapidly developing brains.

Navigating the teething phase takes time, caffeine, and a lot of durable rubber toys, but this intense stage does eventually pass.
