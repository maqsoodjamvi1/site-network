When I first adopted my rescue dog, Buster, I thought separation anxiety only looked like one thing: howling for hours and shredding couch cushions. So, when I came home every day to a quiet apartment and an intact living room, I assumed he was totally fine being left alone during my workday. 

It wasn't until I set up an old smartphone as a makeshift security camera that I realized how wrong I was. Buster wasn't destroying my apartment, but he also wasn't relaxing. Within two minutes of me shutting the front door, his breathing would accelerate, he'd pace the hallway, and he would refuse to touch the puzzle toy I left him. 

Dogs rarely show stress in loud, obvious ways right away. Because our pets want to avoid conflict and conserve energy, their early distress signals are often whisper-quiet. If you want to catch separation anxiety before it spirals into full-blown panic—and costly property damage—you need to know what those subtle signs actually look like.

## Panting, Drooling, and the Tell-Tale Puddle

When we think of a hot dog, we think of panting. But if the air conditioning is set to a comfortable seventy degrees and your dog is panting heavily within minutes of you leaving, heat isn't the culprit. Stress triggers an adrenaline response that raises body temperature and heart rate, leading to heavy panting even in a cool room.

Similarly, look for unexpected moisture around your dog’s bed or crate. Stress-induced salivation can leave behind wet spots on blankets or rugs. You might also notice your dog frequently licking their lips, nose, or paws when you pick up your keys or put on your coat. This "whale eye" posture—where the whites of their eyes are visible as they track your movements—is another physical red flag that your departure is causing internal turmoil.

## The Relentless Pacing Routine

If you review footage from a pet camera, pay attention to your dog's movement patterns. A relaxed dog left alone will usually eat a bit, check out a window, and then settle down to sleep in a preferred spot, often shifting positions a few times. 

A stressed dog, however, tells a different story with their feet. You might notice them walking the exact same path over and over—back and forth along the living room rug, pacing the perimeter of the hallway, or constantly checking the front door. This repetitive movement is a physical outlet for nervous energy. If your dog never actually settles into a deep sleep while you are out, their nervous system is working overtime the entire time you're gone.

## Total Rejection of High-Value Treats

We have all heard the advice to hand our dog a stuffed Kong or a high-value chew bone right as we walk out the door. It is great advice, but only if the dog actually eats it. 

When a dog is experiencing acute anxiety, their digestive system essentially shuts down because the body is prioritizing survival mode over digestion. If you come home and find the expensive peanut-butter-stuffed toy untouched, don't assume your dog just isn't hungry. More likely, they were too panicked to eat. 

To test this, try giving your dog the exact same treat while you are sitting on the couch watching television. If they take it immediately indoors with you, but leave it alone when you exit the room, you have a clear indicator of separation-related stress.

## Excessive Guarding of the Exit

Pay close attention to what happens in the thirty minutes *before* you leave the house. Dogs are master observers and pick up on our micro-habits—the jingle of keys, the zip of a jacket, the putting on of work shoes.

Does your dog shadow your every move as soon as you start getting ready? Do they block doorways, whine softly, or press their body tightly against your legs? While some dogs are just naturally clingy, an escalating pattern of shadowing and obsessive monitoring means your dog's anxiety is spiking well before you even cross the threshold. By the time you actually walk out the door, they are already at maximum capacity for stress.

## How to Check Your Dog's Baseline Today

Reading about these signs is helpful, but seeing is believing. The single best step you can take today is to run a "lonely test." 

Set up a tablet, laptop, or old phone to record video, then walk out of your house or apartment for just ten minutes. Don't make a big deal of leaving or coming back. When you review the footage, watch for whether your dog settles down, how they respond to environmental noises, and whether they touch any comfort items you left behind. Catching these subtle indicators now gives you the power to adjust your routine, work on desensitization, and keep a minor worry from turning into a major behavioral hurdle.

As you start observing your dog's habits and looking into ways to build their confidence when left alone, it helps to have a structured game plan to guide your next steps.
