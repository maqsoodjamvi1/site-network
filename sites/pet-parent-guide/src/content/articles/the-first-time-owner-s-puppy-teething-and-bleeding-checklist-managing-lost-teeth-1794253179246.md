When I brought my first puppy home, I expected the occasional chewed-up shoe. What I did not expect was walking into the living room, looking down at my white rug, and seeing spots of actual blood. Panic set in immediately. Was my dog hurt? Is my puppy sick? 

It turns out, my six-month-old golden retriever mix was simply losing his baby teeth. Just like human children, puppies go through a teething phase where their tiny, needle-sharp deciduous teeth fall out to make room for their permanent adult teeth. Finding a speck of blood on a chew toy or a small tooth on the carpet is completely normal, but it can still catch a new pet parent off guard. 

If you are currently navigating this messy, mouthy milestone, here is a practical checklist to help you manage the puppy teething and bleeding phase with confidence.

## What Normal Puppy Teething Looks Like

Puppies are typically born without teeth. Their baby teeth start erupting around three to four weeks of age, and by the time you bring them home at eight weeks, they have a full set of 28 sharp little needles. Around the four-to-six-month mark, those baby teeth begin to loosen and fall out, replaced by 42 larger adult teeth. 

During this window, you might notice a few specific signs:
*   **Light bleeding:** You may see tiny pink smudges on rubber toys, ropes, or even your floors. This happens because the adult tooth pushes up from underneath, displacing the root of the baby tooth and causing minor gum bleeding.
*   **Swollen or red gums:** The gums right around the incoming teeth might look a bit inflamed.
*   **Obsessive chewing:** Your puppy's gums ache constantly. They will try to chew on table legs, baseboards, rugs, and your hands to find relief.
*   **Drooling:** Some puppies drool significantly more than usual while their gums are tender.

## What to Do When You Spot Blood

The first time you see blood in your puppy's mouth or on a toy, take a deep breath. Unless your puppy is bleeding continuously from the gums in a steady stream, a few stray drops are not an emergency. 

Here is my step-by-step approach to handling it safely:
1.  **Inspect the mouth gently:** Wait until your puppy is calm. Gently lift their lip to see where the blood is coming from. Usually, it is a single loose tooth or a slightly raw patch of gum where a tooth just fell out.
2.  **Remove hard or sharp hazards:** Take away any extremely hard nylon bones or antlers that could irritate bleeding gums further. 
3.  **Offer a cooling sensation:** Cold helps numb aching gums. Soak a clean washcloth in water, twist it, and stick it in the freezer for an hour. Let your puppy chew on the frozen cloth. The ice-cold fabric soothes the inflammation, and the texture helps loosen stubborn baby teeth safely.
4.  **Clean up gently:** If you see blood on your floors or furniture, standard pet-safe enzymatic cleaner will lift it right out without harsh chemicals.

## Where Do All the Baby Teeth Go?

One of the biggest mysteries for new owners is finding the actual teeth. You would think with 28 baby teeth dropping out, you would find a collection on the rug. In reality, most pet owners only ever find one or two. 

So, where do they go? Most of the time, puppies simply swallow them. They fall out while the puppy is gnawing on a chew toy or eating their kibble, and the dog swallows them without a second thought. Do not panic if your puppy swallows a baby tooth; their digestive system passes tiny puppy teeth without any issue at all. 

## When to Call the Vet

While bleeding from teething is normal, you need to know where to draw the line. Sometimes, baby teeth refuse to fall out even though the adult teeth are already erupting behind them. This condition is called retained deciduous teeth, and it is common in smaller breeds like Poodles, Chihuahuas, and Yorkies. 

Call your veterinarian if you notice:
*   **Double teeth:** An adult tooth is growing in right next to an existing baby tooth (often seen prominently with upper and lower canine teeth). Retained teeth can trap food, cause plaque buildup, and misalign the adult bite, often requiring a vet to extract them.
*   **Profuse bleeding:** Bleeding that does not stop after a few minutes, or blood coming from a broken tooth that exposed the nerve pulp.
*   **Extreme lethargy or refusal to eat:** While teething puppies might be slightly cranky, they should still be eager for mealtime. If your puppy refuses food completely, their mouth may be in severe pain.

Navigating the puppy teething phase takes patience, plenty of chew toys, and a high tolerance for finding soggy items on your floor.
