My palms used to sweat the moment a meeting leader said, "Let's go around the room and share our updates." While my coworkers smoothly talked about quarterly metrics, my brain went entirely offline. My heart hammered against my ribs, my throat tightened, and I became entirely focused on hiding the fact that I was panicking. 

If you deal with social anxiety, you know that telling yourself to "just calm down" is completely useless. When your sympathetic nervous system kicks into fight-or-flight mode over a casual introduction or a networking event, logical thoughts cannot override physical symptoms. 

Over the years, I stopped trying to think my way out of panic and started using my breath to trick my nervous system instead. Because you cannot always close your eyes, cross your legs, and meditate in the middle of an office or a crowded restaurant, you need tools that work in real-time. Here are three discreet breathing protocols I use before speaking up, designed so no one in the room will ever know you are doing them.

## The Under-the-Table Box Breath

When you have a few minutes of warning—say, you are sitting at a conference table waiting for your turn to present—the standard box breath is my go-to. It balances oxygen and carbon dioxide levels quickly, signaling to your brain that you are not actually running from a predator.

The challenge is doing it without looking like you are hyper-focusing on your breathing. Here is how I adapt it for public settings:

*   **Inhale for 4 seconds:** Draw air in slowly through your nose, expanding your belly rather than your chest. Keep your shoulders down.
*   **Hold for 4 seconds:** Keep your throat and shoulders relaxed while the air sits in your lungs.
*   **Exhale for 4 seconds:** Release the air smoothly through your mouth or nose.
*   **Hold for 4 seconds:** Keep your lungs empty for a brief, still moment before repeating.

By pacing the counts to the second hand of your watch or the rhythm of someone else's presentation, you give your hyperactive mind a neutral task to focus on instead of your impending turn to speak. Doing this for just two minutes visibly lowers your pulse.

## The Physiological Sigh for Immediate Panic

Sometimes anxiety hits you out of nowhere. You get called on unexpectedly, or you walk into a crowded room and your heart rate spikes instantly. Box breathing requires too much counting when your brain is already scrambled. For those moments, I rely on a reflex built into our biology: the physiological sigh.

Neuroscientists note that this is the fastest way to offload carbon dioxide and slow your heart rate in real-time. It takes about three seconds.

To do it discreetly, take a deep inhale through your nose until your lungs feel full. Then, without exhaling, take a sharp, quick second sip of air to inflate your lungs to their absolute maximum capacity. Follow that with a long, slow exhale through your mouth, letting your jaw drop slightly as you breathe out. 

You only need to do this twice. I use this exact technique right before I unmute my microphone on a video call or right as I step up to a microphone at an event. It drops your shoulders instantly and clears the adrenaline fog from your brain.

## Extended Exhales While Speaking

The hardest part of social anxiety isn't always the anticipation—it's the actual moment you open your mouth. Many of us make this worse by taking shallow, rapid breaths while we talk, which triggers more panic mid-sentence. 

When it is your turn to speak, your breathing strategy shifts from preparation to execution. Before you say your first word, take one deep breath, and make sure your exhale lasts twice as long as your inhale. 

As you start talking, consciously pace your sentences so you exhale slightly longer than normal. If you need to pause to gather your thoughts, do not panic and fill the silence with filler words. Instead, take a slow, quiet breath through your nose while looking at your notes or scanning the room. People perceive intentional pauses as confidence, not hesitation, giving you a built-in window to reset your nervous system while you are already on the floor.

Managing social anxiety isn't about eliminating the nervous feeling altogether; it's about learning how to function right alongside it. By keeping these micro-practices in your back pocket, you can quiet the physical chaos long enough to let your voice be heard.
