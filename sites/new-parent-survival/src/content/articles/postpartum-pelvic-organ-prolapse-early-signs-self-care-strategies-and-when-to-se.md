Giving birth changes your body in ways that textbooks rarely prepare you for. Weeks after you bring your baby home—long after the initial bleeding stops and the stitches heal—you might notice a strange, heavy pressure between your legs. When I first felt it while grocery shopping, it honestly felt like a small plum was sitting in my vagina, threatening to fall out if I walked too fast. 

That sensation is often the first clue of postpartum pelvic organ prolapse. If you are reading this because you feel a weird bulge, heaviness, or pulling in your pelvis, take a deep breath. You did not break your body, and you are far from alone. 

## What Postpartum Pelvic Organ Prolapse Actually Is

Pelvic organ prolapse happens when the muscles and connective tissues that hold your pelvic organs—your bladder, uterus, and bowel—in place become stretched and weakened. Think of these organs as resting in a supportive hammock made of muscle. Pregnancy puts nine months of heavy downward pressure on that hammock, and vaginal delivery can stretch or tear those supporting fibers. 

When the hammock sags, one or more organs can drop out of their normal position and press against the vaginal walls. Depending on which organ is sagging, doctors call it a cystocele (bladder), rectocele (bowel), or uterine prolapse. 

It is easy to panic when you realize something has shifted down there. However, mild to moderate prolapse is extremely common after childbirth. For many new parents, the tissues are simply exhausted and stretched out, and they need time, rest, and targeted support to regain their tone.

## Early Signs and Daily Symptoms to Watch For

The symptoms of postpartum prolapse usually sneak up on you during busy days. You might feel totally fine lying in bed, but by 4:00 PM—after hours of picking up laundry, rocking a crying infant, and pushing a stroller—the symptoms flare up. 

Common early signs include:
* A physical feeling of heaviness, dragging, or fullness in the vagina, often described as feeling like something is falling out.
* Actually feeling or seeing a soft bulge or lump at the vaginal opening, especially when wiping after using the bathroom.
* Urine leakage when you cough, sneeze, laugh, or lift your car seat (stress incontinence).
* A persistent dull ache in your lower back or pelvis that worsens as the day goes on.
* Difficulty fully emptying your bladder or bowels, sometimes requiring you to lean forward or press on the vaginal wall to finish peeing or pooping.

If these symptoms sound familiar, note when they happen. Do they disappear after you lie down for an hour? That pattern is a classic hallmark of early prolapse.

## Practical Self-Care Strategies You Can Start Today

While you wait for a medical appointment, you can take practical steps to reduce downward pressure on your pelvic floor and help your tissues rest. 

First, look at how you lift. When you pick up your baby, a basket of laundry, or an older toddler, stop holding your breath and bearing down. Instead, exhale on the exertion—blow out like you are blowing through a straw as you lift. Keep the weight close to your chest, and engage your core gently without sucking your stomach in rigidly.

Second, change your bathroom habits. Never strain or push when you have a bowel movement. Prop your feet up on a squatty potty or a small step stool to straighten the angle of your colon. Also, do not hover over public toilets; sit down completely to let your pelvic floor relax fully.

Third, prioritize horizontal rest. Gravity is not your friend when your pelvic hammock is sagging. Try to lie flat on your back or side with your hips slightly elevated by a pillow for 20 to 30 minutes once or twice a day. This gives your pelvic organs a literal break from gravity.

Finally, ditch high-impact exercise for now. Running, jumping jacks, heavy weightlifting, and intense bootcamps will only push weakened tissues further down. Stick to gentle walking on flat surfaces until a professional evaluates you.

## When to See a Specialist

If your symptoms do not improve with basic rest, or if the bulge protrudes past your vaginal opening, it is time to see a professional. Do not rely on your general OB-GYN for a quick five-minute checkup where they tell you "everything looks fine down there" while you are lying flat on a table (gravity hides prolapse, after all).

Look for a board-certified pelvic floor physical therapist. These specialists have advanced training in internal and external pelvic anatomy. They can accurately grade the severity of your prolapse, assess your muscle tone, and teach you how to properly contract and relax your pelvic floor. They can also discuss whether a temporary medical device called a pessary—a silicone ring inserted to support the vaginal walls—might help you function comfortably while you heal.
