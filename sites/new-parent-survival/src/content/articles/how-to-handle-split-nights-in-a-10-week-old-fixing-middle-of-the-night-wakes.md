If you are currently staring at the ceiling at 2:00 AM while your ten-week-old baby happily gazes at the mobile, coos at the wall, or treats the bedroom like it is mid-afternoon, you are dealing with a classic split night. 

Unlike a standard night waking where a baby cries for milk, goes right back to sleep, and leaves you back in bed within twenty minutes, a split night is different. Your baby is wide awake, often content, and simply refuses to sleep for one to two hours straight in the middle of the night. 

When this happened with my second child around the ten-week mark, I made the mistake of trying to play with him and turn on the lights, thinking he was ready to start the day. That backfired immediately. Fixing this frustrating phase requires understanding why it happens and making a few deliberate tweaks to your daily routine.

## Why 10-Week-Olds Have Split Nights

At ten weeks old, your baby's circadian rhythm is still maturing. Around this age, babies undergo a major developmental shift. Their sleep cycles are organizing, and their total sleep needs across a twenty-four-hour period begin to consolidate. 

The most common culprit behind a split night is simple: your baby has simply slept too much during the day. 

Imagine your baby has a twenty-four-hour "sleep budget." If they spend too much of that budget napping between 7:00 AM and 7:00 PM, they simply do not have enough biological sleep pressure left to stay asleep through the night. When they wake up around 2:00 AM after completing a sleep cycle, their brain thinks, "I've had plenty of rest, I'm ready to party." 

Another factor is undertiredness at bedtime. If the last wake window of the evening is too short, your baby goes down too easily at 7:00 PM, sleeps lightly for a few hours, and then treats the middle-of-the-night wake as an extended nap.

## Step One: Cap Daytime Sleep

The fastest way to fix a split night is to look honestly at your baby's daytime sleep totals. At ten weeks old, many babies do well on about four to five hours of daytime sleep spread across four naps. 

If your baby is regularly sleeping six or more hours during the day, you are likely looking at the root cause of your nighttime parties. 

*   **Set a daily daytime cap:** Do not let any single nap stretch past two hours. Gently wake your baby if they hit the two-hour mark.
*   **Track the daily total:** Aim for a maximum of five hours of total daytime sleep. If your baby hits that mark by late afternoon, keep them awake a bit longer before bedtime.
*   **Protect the last wake window:** Ensure your baby is awake for at least 75 to 90 minutes before bedtime so they build up enough sleep pressure to push through the night.

## Step Two: Keep the Middle of the Night Boring

When a split night strikes, your reaction dictates how long the phase will last. If you pick your baby up, turn on lamps, talk in an excited voice, or change a dry diaper under bright lights, you are accidentally reinforcing the wakefulness.

Keep everything strictly nocturnal. When you hear your baby awake and chatting away in the crib, give it ten minutes. If they are not crying, resist the urge to rush in. Often, babies will fuss for a few minutes and wind themselves back down.

If you do need to go in because they start fussing, keep the room pitch black. Do not make eye contact. Keep your voice to a low, boring whisper if you must speak at all. Feed them in the dark if they are genuinely hungry, but keep the interaction purely transactional. 

## Step Three: Adjust Your Expectations for the Phase

Even when you make all the right adjustments to daytime sleep and nighttime routines, a split night might not vanish overnight. Biological changes take time to settle. 

This phase typically lasts anywhere from a few days to a couple of weeks as your baby's internal clock catches up to their growing body. Treat these middle-of-the-night wakes as a temporary blip rather than a permanent new schedule. Keep your own environment as dark and quiet as possible so you can conserve your energy while your baby figures out day-night separation.

To help you put these adjustments into practice, take a close look at your baby's exact wake windows and daily sleep totals over the next forty-eight hours to pinpoint where the excess rest might be hiding.
