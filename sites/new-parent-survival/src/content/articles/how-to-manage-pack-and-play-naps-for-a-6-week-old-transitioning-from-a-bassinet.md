Bringing home a six-week-old baby is an exercise in constant adjustment, just about the time you figure out one sleep quirk, your baby changes the rules. If you are preparing to move your newborn out of their bedside bassinet and into a full-sized pack-and-play—perhaps because they are outgrowing the bassinet, or because you want to get them used to it before traveling—you might be feeling a bit nervous. At six weeks old, babies are just starting to emerge from the fourth trimester fog, and their daytime sleep habits are notoriously fragile. 

Making this daytime sleep transition smoothly is entirely possible if you approach it with a bit of strategy rather than expecting your baby to instantly love a new sleep space. Here is how I managed this exact transition in my own home, step by step.

## Why the Pack-and-Play Feels Different to a Newborn

Before you try putting your baby down for a nap in the pack-and-play, it helps to understand why your little one might protest. A standard bassinet is usually cozy, enclosed, and sits right next to your bed where your baby can smell you and hear your breathing. A pack-and-play, on the other hand, is wider, often sits lower to the ground, and might feel cavernous to a six-week-old. 

Furthermore, the mattress pad in a pack-and-play is typically firmer and sounds crinklier under a fitted sheet than a plush bassinet mattress. Recognizing these sensory differences helps you troubleshoot without second-guessing your baby's temperament.

## Step One: Prime the Sleep Environment

Do not just drop your baby into a brand-new, cold pack-and-play for the first time during their longest nap of the day. Instead, introduce the space gradually. 

Start by setting up the pack-and-play in the room where they will be napping—whether that is your living room for daytime supervision or their eventual nursery. Put a properly fitted, breathable sheet on the mattress. To make it smell familiar, you can place a worn t-shirt of yours over the mattress pad for a few hours before naptime to absorb your scent, and then remove it before you actually lay the baby down for safety. 

Keep the room dark and turn on a white noise machine. At six weeks old, babies lack the circadian rhythm to put themselves back to sleep easily, so blocking out household light and sound is crucial to helping them settle into a firmer mattress.

## Step Two: Start with One Nap a Day

When you are ready to make the switch, do not force every single sleep cycle into the pack-and-play right away. Pick just one nap—usually the first morning nap, when sleep pressure is naturally highest and babies are most cooperative—to be your trial run.

For all other daytime naps, stick to whatever is currently working, whether that is a contact nap, a baby carrier, or the bedside bassinet. Once your six-week-old successfully handles that single morning nap in the pack-and-play for three or four days in a row, you can gradually add a second nap to the routine. 

## Step Three: Master the Transfer

The hardest part of pack-and-play naps at this age is the transfer. Because the mattress sits lower down than a bassinet, you have to bend further over the rails, which can awkward-up your posture and wake a light-sleeping newborn.

*   **Warm the mattress:** If your house is drafty, the pack-and-play mattress might feel chilly compared to your arms. Lay a heating pad on the sheet for five minutes before naptime, and **always remove it** and test the sheet with the back of your hand before putting the baby down.
*   **The "butt-to-head" descent:** Lower your baby's bottom down first, followed gently by their torso, and finally their head. This prevents the startling falling sensation that triggers the Moro reflex and makes their eyes snap open.
*   **Keep a hand on them:** Once they are down, keep one hand resting gently on their chest and your other hand supporting their head for about 60 seconds until their breathing slows down and deepens. 

## What to Do When It Goes Sideways

Expect a few false starts. If your six-week-old wakes up screaming thirty minutes into a pack-and-play nap, do not panic and abandon the experiment forever. At this age, short naps are developmentally normal. 

If they wake up crying, give them one or two minutes to see if they can resettle on their own, though at six weeks, they usually need a little assistance. If they are truly upset, pick them up, soothe them back to sleep, and finish the nap as a contact nap or in the old bassinet to ensure they are not overtired for the rest of the day. Consistency matters more than perfection; try again tomorrow with that single morning nap.

As you look ahead to the coming weeks, having a portable sleep space that your baby accepts will give you immense freedom during daytime hours and travel.
