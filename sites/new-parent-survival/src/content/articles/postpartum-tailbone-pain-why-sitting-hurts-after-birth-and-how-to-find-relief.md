If you are currently trying to sit on a pile of pillows, a nursing pillow, or a folded-up fleece blanket just to feed your baby, I see you. When I brought my first child home, I expected the bleeding, the sleep deprivation, and the emotional roller coaster. What I did not expect was the sharp, stabbing agony in my tailbone every time I tried to sit down, stand up, or shift my weight on the couch. 

Postpartum tailbone pain—medically known as coccydynia—is remarkably common after vaginal delivery, yet it is rarely talked about in prenatal classes. If you are researching this right now because you are pregnant or recently gave birth and every chair feels like a bed of nails, you are in the right place. Here is a breakdown of why this happens and what you can actually do to find relief at home.

## Why Your Tailbone Hurts After Birth

Your tailbone (the coccyx) is a small, triangular bone at the very base of your spine. During pregnancy, your body releases a hormone called relaxin, which softens the ligaments and joints throughout your pelvis to prepare for birth. 

During a vaginal delivery, your baby has to pass right by this delicate structure. If your baby is on the larger side, if you push for a long time, or if your little one takes a specific angle on their way out, the coccyx can get severely bruised, strained, or even subluxated (partially dislocated). 

For me, the pain did not peak immediately in the hospital bed; it set in around day three, right when the adrenaline wore off and the reality of round-the-clock sitting and nursing really began. 

## The Everyday Triggers You Might Not Expect

When you have tailbone pain, the simplest daily tasks become mini-obstacles. You might find that sitting straight up in a dining room chair is impossible, forcing you to lean heavily to one side. 

Other common triggers include:
* Transitioning from sitting to standing (that sudden jolt when the weight shifts back onto the bone)
* Reclining on a soft couch that lets your lower back sink and puts direct pressure on the coccyx
* Coughing, sneezing, or having a bowel movement, all of which contract the pelvic floor muscles attached right near the tailbone

Recognizing these triggers is the first step because it stops you from accidentally aggravating the area over and over again throughout the day.

## Practical Ways to Find Relief Today

You do not have to just grit your teeth and wait months for this to heal on its own. While true healing takes time, you can drastically reduce your day-to-day pain with a few physical adjustments.

First, invest in or improvise the right seating. A standard donut pillow can actually make tailbone pain worse by letting the coccyx hang unsupported, putting more stretch on the surrounding ligaments. Instead, look for a wedge cushion or an ergonomic seat cushion with a cutout at the back. The cutout ensures your tailbone floats completely free over empty space, transferring your body weight entirely to your sit bones and thighs. 

Second, change how you sit and stand. When you sit down, lean your torso slightly forward before you make contact with the chair, putting your weight on your thighs rather than your lower spine. When you stand up, lean forward again to lift your weight off the tailbone before engaging your legs to rise. 

Third, pay attention to your pelvic floor. The muscles of the pelvic floor attach directly to the coccyx. If those muscles are in constant spasm from the trauma of birth, they will continuously pull on your tailbone. Gentle diaphragmatic breathing—inhale to let your belly and pelvic floor expand, exhale to gently release—can help calm that muscle tension. 

## When to Seek Professional Support

While mild to moderate coccydynia usually improves steadily over the first six to twelve weeks postpartum, you do not have to suffer in silence. If your pain is getting worse instead of better, or if it interferes with your ability to care for your baby, look for a pelvic floor physical therapist. 

Unlike a standard doctor's visit where you might just be told to take ibuprofen and wait, a pelvic floor physical therapist can perform internal or external evaluations of the coccyx and surrounding muscles. They can gently realign the joint, release tight pelvic floor muscles, and teach you specific taping or positioning techniques that completely change your daily comfort level.

As you navigate these first few weeks of postpartum recovery, remember that your body did something extraordinary and it deserves grace, patience, and targeted care. Exploring the right physical supports and professional therapies can help you get back to enjoying those long, cozy nursing sessions and cuddles without the wincing.
