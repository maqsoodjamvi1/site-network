If you are reading this right now, chances are you are trapped under a sleeping twelve-week-old baby. Your arms are numb, your phone battery is at four percent, and your bladder has been giving you urgent warning signals for the last twenty minutes. Contact napping is sweet for the first month, but by week twelve, the reality of being a human mattress sets in hard. You need your hands back, and your baby needs to learn how to sleep somewhere that doesn’t involve your chest. 

Transitioning away from constant contact naps at this age is entirely possible, but it requires a strategic shift in how you approach daytime sleep. At twelve weeks, babies are starting to come out of the fourth trimester fog. They are more alert, their circadian rhythms are stabilizing, and they are physically capable of stringing together slightly longer sleep cycles, provided the environment supports it. Here is the exact playbook I used to transition my own Velcro baby to the crib without descending into madness.

## Stop Cold Turkey on the First Nap of the Day

When you decide to make a change, the temptation is to throw out the rulebook and try for crib naps across the board, all day long. Do not do this. It will result in an overtired baby, a screaming match, and you giving up by noon out of pure exhaustion. 

Instead, target just one nap for the crib—preferably the very first nap of the day. At twelve weeks, sleep pressure is on your side first thing in the morning, meaning your baby's melatonin levels are still working in your favor. Start by doing your usual wind-down routine in the nursery, get them fully asleep in your arms, and then attempt to transfer them to the crib for nap one. Keep the rest of the day’s naps as contact naps. Once nap one is happening reliably in the crib for three or four days in a row, move nap two over. 

## Master the "Limp Limb" Test Before Lowering

The hardest part of the crib transition isn't getting the baby asleep; it's the gravity-defying drop into the crib that triggers the Moro reflex and wakes them up instantly. To beat this, you need to wait out the deep sleep phase. 

When your baby falls asleep in your arms, do not rush to the crib. Wait a full ten to fifteen minutes after their eyes close. Then, perform the limb test: gently lift one of their arms and let it go. If it drops heavily and limply back down like a dead weight, they are in a deep sleep cycle. If there is any tension, resistance, or a twitch of the eyelids, wait another five minutes. 

When you do lower them, do it horizontally—butt and back first, head last. Keep your hands resting firmly on their chest and tummy for about thirty seconds after they touch the mattress to provide continuous pressure, then slowly slide your hands out from underneath them.

## Optimize the Landing Zone

Babies at twelve weeks are sensitive to temperature and texture shifts. If you pull a warm, snug baby out of your cozy arms and drop them onto a freezing cold, unpadded plastic mattress protector, the shock will wake them up immediately. 

Warm the crib up before the transfer. You can place a heating pad on the crib mattress while you are rocking and nursing your baby, but **you must remove the heating pad completely** before you lay the baby down. Never leave a heating pad, blanket, or loose item in an unattended crib. Alternatively, rub the fitted sheet with your own hands for a minute to take the chill off, or dress your baby in a weighted-free, breathable sleep sack that keeps their body heat trapped against them during the transfer. 

## Lower the Bar on Nap Lengths

Here is the bitter truth about twelve-week-old sleep: crib naps are almost always shorter than contact naps. While you might get a glorious two-hour contact nap on your chest, your baby will likely wake up after a single sleep cycle—around thirty to forty-five minutes—when left alone in a crib. 

Do not panic and assume the transition has failed. This is developmentally normal. Their brains are waking up between cycles, and without the warmth of your body or the sound of your heartbeat to soothe them back down, they pop wide awake. If your baby wakes up crying after thirty minutes, go in and rescue the nap if you need them to get more day sleep, or just accept that a short nap is still a win. As they practice independent sleep, those cycles will naturally lengthen over the coming weeks.

Moving away from round-the-clock contact naps is a gradual process that tests your patience, but taking it one step at a time protects your sanity. By focusing on the morning nap first and perfecting your transfer technique, you can reclaim your afternoons without having to listen to hours of distress.
