When my wife was seven months pregnant with our first child, I spent an entire weekend obsessing over a brand-new car seat. I read every safety rating, watched installation videos on YouTube three times, and practiced clicking the base into place until my thumbs ached. But looking back, the most consequential preparation I did that weekend had nothing to do with nylon straps or plastic bases. It involved sitting at our kitchen table with a stack of legal forms, finally updating our wills.

Pregnancy has a way of shifting your perspective on mortality. Suddenly, every car ride feels a little riskier, and the future feels both infinitely long and terrifyingly fragile. Before our daughter was born, my wife and I had the standard twenty-something estate plan: basically, nothing. We figured that if something happened to us, our assets would just split between our parents. But once you have a dependent who cannot feed, clothe, or advocate for themselves, leaving things to chance is no longer an option. 

If you are currently expecting or preparing to adopt, updating your estate plan is just as critical as setting up the nursery. Here is the practical, step-by-step checklist my wife and I used to get our legal house in order before our baby arrived.

## Choose Your Child’s Guardian (The Hardest Decision)

This is the conversation most parents-to-be dread. Who would raise your child if both you and your partner passed away? It is an uncomfortable thought, but naming a legal guardian in your will is the single most important decision on this list. If you do not name a guardian, a family court judge will make that choice for you, and their pick might not align with your family values.

When my wife and I finally hashed this out over cold coffee, we looked at three specific criteria:
*   **Parenting philosophy:** Do they share our approach to discipline, education, and lifestyle?
*   **Location and stability:** Would our child have to move across the country, change schools, and leave their support network?
*   **Age and energy:** Are our parents too old to keep up with a toddler, or are our siblings too financially unstable right now?

Remember that you can name a primary guardian and a backup. Once you pick someone, have an honest conversation with them *before* writing their name in the legal document. You need to make sure they are actually willing to take on the responsibility if the unthinkable happens.

## Establish a Testamentary Trust for Minor Finances

Minors cannot legally own significant assets or property in the United States. If you pass away and leave your life insurance payout or savings directly to a newborn, the court will freeze those funds in a blocked account until the child turns eighteen. At that exact moment, they will receive a check for the full amount—which is rarely a great outcome for an eighteen-year-old’s financial future.

To prevent this, your will should establish a testamentary trust. This is essentially a rulebook for how your money will be managed for your child until they reach an age you deem responsible, such as twenty-five or thirty. 

You will also need to appoint a trustee. This is the person who manages the money, pays for your child’s shoes, braces, and college tuition, and makes sure the funds are used wisely. The trustee can be the same person as the guardian, but many financial advisors recommend separating the roles. For example, you might want your sister to raise your child daily, but your brother—who works in finance—to manage the trust fund. 

## Update Your Beneficiaries Outside the Will

A common mistake new parents make is assuming that writing a will covers everything. It does not. Assets like 401(k) plans, individual retirement accounts, and life insurance policies pass directly to the named beneficiaries on the account paperwork, regardless of what your will says.

A week after finalizing our wills, we spent an evening logging into our various financial portals. We added each other as primary beneficiaries, but we also designated our future children as contingent beneficiaries. That way, if something happened to both of us simultaneously, the funds would flow straight into the trust we set up in our wills. 

If you have employer-provided life insurance, check those forms too. Those defaults rarely update automatically when a baby arrives.

## Draft Healthcare Proxies and Financial Powers of Attorney

Estate planning is not just about what happens *after* you die; it is also about who makes decisions if you are incapacitated. Imagine you are in a severe accident and hospitalized while your newborn is at home with a babysitter. Who has the legal right to make medical decisions for you, or access your bank accounts to pay the mortgage while you recover?

A durable power of attorney gives someone you trust the authority to handle your financial affairs if you cannot. A healthcare proxy designates a medical decision-maker. When we updated ours, we made sure our designated proxy knew our exact wishes regarding long-term medical care, ensuring our family wouldn't have to guess during a crisis.

## Finalize the Paperwork with a Professional

While online legal software can work for very simple situations, having a child usually adds enough complexity that consulting an estate planning attorney is worth the investment. Local laws regarding trusts, probate, and guardianship vary significantly by state. An attorney can ensure your documents are properly witnessed, notarized, and legally airtight, saving your family from a costly probate nightmare later.

Preparing for a baby involves checking off a lot of boxes, from painting walls to installing car seats. Taking care of these legal safeguards won't just protect your growing family; it will give you the peace of mind to actually enjoy the newborn phase.
