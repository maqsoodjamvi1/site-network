When I first looked down at my midsection a few weeks after having my second baby, I panicked a little. My belly felt soft, and when I tried to sit up from the bed, I noticed a strange dome-like ridge pushing right down the center of my stomach. If you are noticing something similar, take a deep breath. You are likely dealing with diastasis recti, which is simply the separation of your outermost abdominal muscles. 

During pregnancy, your growing uterus puts immense pressure on your front body. This causes the connective tissue (the linea alba) running vertically down your center to stretch and thin out, allowing your left and right rectus abdominis muscles to drift apart. While it can feel alarming, it is an extremely common postpartum phenomenon. Knowing how to check for it yourself at home is the first step toward getting your core strength back safely.

## When Is the Right Time to Check Your Abdominal Gap?

You might be tempted to test your stomach the moment you get home from the hospital, but I recommend waiting a bit. In those first two to three weeks postpartum, your body is still in a massive state of fluid shift and involution, meaning your uterus is rapidly shrinking back down and your hormones are fluctuating wildly. 

Trying to check too early can cause unnecessary stress because your tissues are still too soft and swollen to give you an accurate reading. Around week six is usually a solid sweet spot for a baseline check, especially if you have been cleared by your healthcare provider for light activity at your postpartum checkup. If you are six months or even six years postpartum and never checked, that is completely fine too—you can perform this test at any stage of life.

## A Step-by-Step Guide to the At-Home Finger Test

You do not need any special medical equipment to check your abdominal gap. All you need is a flat surface like your bed or a yoga mat on the floor. Wear something comfortable where your bare abdomen is exposed, as you need to feel your muscle walls directly.

1. Lie flat on your back with your knees bent and your feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart.
2. Place the fingertips of one hand across your midline, just above your belly button, pointing your fingers toward your toes. 
3. Relax your abdominal muscles completely and take a normal breath in.
4. As you exhale, gently lift your head and shoulders a few inches off the floor, looking toward your knees—much like the beginning of a gentle crunch. Do not strain your neck or grip with your hip flexors.
5. As you lift, press your fingertips downward into your midline. You should feel the left and right sides of your muscle wall contract and close in around your fingers like a doorway.
6. Move your fingers up toward your ribcage and down toward your pubic bone to check the gap at different heights, as the separation is often wider near the navel than it is further up or down.

## Understanding Your Results: Width and Depth

When you perform the test, you are looking for two distinct measurements: width and depth. Width is measured by how many fingertips fit comfortably side-by-side into the gap between your muscles. A gap of one or two fingers is generally considered standard and functional. Anything wider than two fingers usually points to significant diastasis recti.

Depth is arguably more important than width. When you press your fingers into the gap, does it feel firm and shallow like a drum, or does it feel deep, mushy, and bottomless? A deep, springy gap means the connective tissue is still quite stretched and lacks tension. If your fingers sink deep into your abdomen before hitting resistance, your core is having trouble generating internal pressure. 

## What to Avoid While You Figure Out Your Next Steps

Once you have a better idea of your abdominal gap, you might feel eager to jump into a heavy ab routine to fix it. However, certain traditional exercises can actually make a separation worse by pushing your organs forward and straining the fragile connective tissue. 

For the time being, you should avoid heavy planks, standard sit-ups, traditional crunches, and heavy lifting that causes your belly to dome or cone outward. If you notice your stomach tenting upward in the middle while you lift a heavy laundry basket or get out of bed, your core is failing to manage intra-abdominal pressure. 

Checking your abdominal gap at home gives you a concrete starting point, but you do not have to guess your way through recovery.
