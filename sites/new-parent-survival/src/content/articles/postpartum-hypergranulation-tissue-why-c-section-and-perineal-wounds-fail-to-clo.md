When you give birth, everyone talks about the bleeding, the sleep deprivation, and the emotional roller coaster. What no one warns you about is looking at your healing incision a few weeks postpartum and realizing it just will not close. Instead of a fading pink line, there is a raised, angry-red, sometimes weeping bump of flesh sitting right in the middle of the wound. 

If this is happening to you, you are likely dealing with postpartum hypergranulation tissue. It sounds alarming, and it can be physically painful and emotionally exhausting when you are already running on empty, but it is a relatively common and treatable postpartum complication.

## What Is Hypergranulation Tissue?

During a normal healing process, your body fills a wound with granulation tissue—a rich network of tiny new blood vessels and connective tissue that acts as the foundation for new skin. It is supposed to be pink, firm, and flat. 

Sometimes, the body gets a little overenthusiastic. It keeps producing this tissue long after the gap is filled, causing it to spill out over the edges of the wound. This excess tissue prevents your skin cells from migrating across the surface to close the wound completely. 

Postpartum, this most frequently pops up in two places: along a Cesarean section scar, or on perineal tears and episiotomy repairs where the skin stitches didn't quite mesh smoothly. Instead of healing, the wound stalls out, remaining open, tender, and prone to bleeding whenever your underwear rubs against it.

## Recognizing the Signs Beyond Normal Healing

It is normal for postpartum scars to itch, feel tight, or look a bit angry in the first two weeks. However, hypergranulation tissue has a very distinct look and feel. 

If you notice any of the following, hypergranulation is likely the culprit:
* A bright red or dark pink, bumpy mass that looks a bit like raw hamburger meat or a tiny raspberry sitting inside or on top of the incision.
* The tissue is extremely fragile and bleeds easily when you wipe, shower, or change your maternity pad.
* A persistent, yellowish or clear dampness that isn't heavy enough to be an infection, but refuses to dry up.
* A wound edge that has remained stubbornly the exact same size for two or three weeks without shrinking.

It is worth noting that hypergranulation tissue is not an infection on its own, though an open, unsealed wound is always at a higher risk for one. If you notice spreading redness, foul-smelling discharge, or a fever, call your OB-GYN immediately to rule out an actual infection.

## What to Do (And What Not to Do) at Home

When you first spot a fleshy red bump on your incision, your instinct might be to scrub it, pick at it, or dry it out with rubbing alcohol. Do not do this. Aggressive home treatment will only irritate the fragile new blood vessels, causing more bleeding and further delaying healing.

Instead, take these practical steps while you wait to see a medical professional:
* Keep the area clean and dry. Gently rinse the wound with plain warm water during your shower and pat it completely dry with a clean paper towel rather than a bath towel, which can harbor bacteria and snag on the tissue.
* Protect the wound from friction. Wear loose-fitting, high-waisted cotton underwear that sits well above your C-section line, or loose, breathable cotton shorts if you are dealing with a perineal issue. Avoid tight waistbands that press directly onto the open spot.
* Stop using random ointments. Unless your doctor specifically told you to use a barrier cream, avoid putting heavy petroleum jellies or random leftover antibiotic ointments on hypergranulation tissue. Trapping excess moisture can actually encourage the tissue to keep growing.

## How Your Doctor Will Treat It

Hypergranulation tissue rarely resolves completely on its own because the physical barrier prevents the skin from closing. You will need to contact your OB-GYN, midwife, or a specialized wound care nurse. Do not feel embarrassed; they see this exact issue regularly following deliveries.

The treatment is usually quick and performed right in the exam room. The most common method involves applying a small amount of silver nitrate to the excess tissue using a specialized applicator stick. It sounds intimidating, but silver nitrate chemically cauterizes the overgrown tissue, shrinking it down and stopping the overproduction. You might feel a brief stinging or burning sensation for a few minutes, but it is entirely manageable. 

Depending on the size of the tissue, your provider might need to repeat this process once a week for a couple of visits. Once the hypergranulation is flattened and the wound bed is level, your body can finally finish the job of closing the skin.

Dealing with a stalled postpartum recovery can make you feel like your body is failing you when you are already working overtime to care for a newborn. Recognizing that this is simply a mechanical hiccup in your healing process can take the emotional sting out of the physical one. If your stitches aren't closing up as expected, reach out to your care team today to get a proper assessment and start the path back to a fully healed body.
