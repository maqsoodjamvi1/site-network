If you are staring at a massive pack of heavy-duty maternity pads wondering how on earth your body is still producing this much discharge, take a deep breath. You are deep in the fourth trimester, and what you are experiencing is called lochia. When I brought my first baby home, nobody prepped me for the sheer volume and longevity of postpartum bleeding. I thought it would act like a heavy period for a few days and then vanish. Spoiler alert: it does not. 

Understanding the normal trajectory of postpartum bleeding can save you a lot of late-night panic and unnecessary calls to the triage nurse. Here is the practical breakdown of what your body is doing, what is entirely normal, and when you actually need to pick up the phone.

## The First Few Days: Lochia Rubra

Right after you deliver—whether vaginally or via c-section—your body starts shedding the lining of your uterus, along with leftover tissue and blood. This initial phase is called *lochia rubra*. 

For the first three to four days, the bleeding is bright red, heavy, and often contains clots. When I say clots, I mean anything from the size of a blueberry to a small plum. This can feel alarming, especially when you stand up after lying down for an hour and feel a sudden gush. That is just gravity doing its job, pooling blood in the vaginal canal while you rest and releasing it when you move. 

During this phase, you should expect to go through a large pad every hour or two. Keep a dedicated stash of heavy overnight pads next to your bed, wear dark-colored sweatpants, and accept right now that you will be doing a lot of laundry. 

## The Second Week: Lochia Serosa

Around day four to ten, your bleeding will noticeably shift. This is *lochia serosa*. The bright red color fades into a watery, pinkish-brown discharge. 

The volume should be decreasing significantly during this week. Instead of soaking a heavy pad every couple of hours, you might find that a standard medium pad or even a panty liner is enough. You might also notice that the bleeding picks up temporarily when you breastfeed. Nursing releases oxytocin, which causes your uterus to contract back down to its pre-pregnancy size. Every time my baby latched in those first two weeks, I felt a sharp cramping sensation followed by a fresh surge of pinkish discharge. That is your uterus aggressively working overtime.

## Weeks Three to Six (and Beyond): Lochia Alba

By the third or fourth week, you enter the home stretch: *lochia alba*. This discharge is yellowish-white or cream-colored and much lighter in flow. 

For many people, this phase looks like occasional spotting or light discharge that comes and goes for weeks. With my second child, I had random yellow-tinged spotting all the way through week six, right up until my postpartum checkup. As long as it is tapering off in frequency and doesn't smell foul, this lingering discharge is just your uterine lining finishing up its final cleanup crew duties.

## Red Flags: When to Call Your Doctor Immediately

While postpartum bleeding is normal, postpartum hemorrhage and dangerous infections can happen weeks after you give home. You need to know the specific lines you shouldn't cross. Contact your OB-GYN, midwife, or head straight to labor and delivery if you experience any of the following:

*   **The Pad Test Failure:** Soaking through two or more large maxi pads per hour for two hours straight.
*   **Large Clots:** Passing blood clots consistently that are larger than the size of a golf ball.
*   **Color Reversion:** Your bleeding has turned light pink or white, but suddenly reverts back to heavy, bright red bleeding (often a sign that you are doing too much physical activity too soon).
*   **Foul Odor:** Lochia has a normal, slightly metallic or earthy odor (similar to a heavy period). If it smells foul, fishy, or remarkably putrid, it points toward an uterine infection (endometritis).
*   **Systemic Symptoms:** Bleeding accompanied by a fever of 100.4°F or higher, severe chills, dizziness, or a rapid heart rate.

## Practical Survival Tips for the Bleeding Phase

Do not use tampons or menstrual cups until your doctor clears you at your six-week postpartum visit. Your cervix is still dilated and your uterine lining has open wounds; introducing foreign objects creates a direct highway for bacteria. 

Instead, stock up on postpartum underwear (disposable briefs are vastly superior to mesh hospital underwear), keep a peri bottle filled with warm water next to every toilet for gentle rinsing, and give yourself permission to horizontal-parent. If you overdo it by vacuuming or walking too far on day ten, your body will literally bleed as a reminder to sit back down.

As you navigate this messy, exhausting phase of healing, keeping track of your symptoms day by day can bring incredible peace of mind.
