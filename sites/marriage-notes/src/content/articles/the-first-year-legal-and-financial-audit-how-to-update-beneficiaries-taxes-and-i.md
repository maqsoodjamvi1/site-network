Getting married changes your legal status overnight, but the government, your employer's HR department, and your bank accounts do not automatically get the memo. When my spouse and I sat down after our wedding, we realized we were still operating as two separate single units on paper. Fixing that took a few weekends, but getting it done early saved us from potential tax headaches and insurance gaps. 

If you recently tied the knot or are preparing for marriage and want to know what comes after the reception ends, treating your first year as a legal and financial audit is the most practical step you can take. Here is the exact checklist we used to get our shared house in order.

## Update Your Workplace Benefits and Beneficiaries Immediately

Your old life insurance policy, 401(k), and Roth IRA likely still list your parents, siblings, or even an ex as your primary beneficiary. A marriage certificate does not automatically override these documents. If something happens to you, the money goes to whoever is named on the form, regardless of what your new will says.

Log into your employee portal and update your life insurance, health savings accounts, and retirement accounts. Your new spouse should almost always be your primary beneficiary unless you are intentionally setting up a different estate plan with an attorney. Do the same for any external investment accounts you manage outside of work. While you are in your HR portal, look at your health insurance. Marriage is a qualifying life event, giving you a strict 30-to-60-day window (depending on your employer) to add your spouse to your plan or combine onto one policy without waiting for the annual open enrollment period.

## Tackle Your Name Change Paperwork in the Right Order

If either of you is changing your last name, do not walk into the bank on day one. There is a strict sequence required to make the bureaucratic gears turn smoothly. 

Start with the Social Security Administration. You need to update your Social Security card first because the IRS and state motor vehicle departments cross-reference their databases with it. Once your new Social Security card arrives in the mail, head to the DMV to update your driver's license. Only after you have your new ID and Social Security card in hand should you visit your bank branches, credit card companies, and mortgage lenders. Doing this out of order will result in teller confusion and rejected paperwork. Remember to also notify your employer so your payroll name matches your tax filings.

## Recalculate Your Tax Withholding 

The tax code treats married couples very differently than single filers, and the infamous "marriage penalty" or "marriage bonus" can blindside you in April if you do not adjust your withholdings. If both of you work, your combined income might push you into a higher tax bracket, meaning your current paycheck deductions might not cover what you actually owe.

Go to the IRS website and use their Tax Withholding Estimator tool. Plug in your most recent pay stubs along with your spouse's. If you both check the box indicating you are married filing jointly without accounting for dual incomes, you could easily face a surprise tax bill. Submit a new Form W-4 to your respective HR departments to adjust your withholding amounts so you are neither overpaying nor underpaying Uncle Sam.

## Combine Bank Accounts Strategically

You do not have to merge all your money on day one, but you do need a transparent system for shared expenses. My spouse and I kept our individual checking accounts for personal spending, but we opened a joint account specifically for household bills, groceries, and savings goals.

When opening a joint account, pay attention to the legal designation:
* **Joint Tenants with Rights of Survivorship (JTWROS):** If one account holder passes away, the remaining funds automatically transfer to the survivor without going through probate court.
* **Convenience Accounts:** These give someone access to spend, but they do not transfer ownership upon death. Avoid this for primary marital funds.

Set up automatic transfers from your individual accounts into your joint account proportional to your incomes if you make unequal salaries, or split down the middle if your earnings are similar. 

## Draft Essential Estate Planning Documents

Before marriage, a medical emergency meant your parents made decisions if you were incapacitated. After marriage, that legal right defaults to your spouse—unless there is a dispute. To prevent family friction and ensure your wishes are honored, you need a few foundational legal documents in place:

* **Financial Power of Attorney:** Grants your spouse the legal authority to pay your bills, manage accounts, and handle legal matters if you are unable to do so.
* **Healthcare Proxy (Medical Power of Attorney):** Designates your spouse to make medical decisions on your behalf if you are unconscious or incapacitated.
* **Living Will:** Lays out your specific end-of-life medical preferences.

You can draft these using reputable online legal software or hire an estate attorney, but do not skip them. Getting these documents signed and notarized protects both of you from the chaos of unexpected medical or financial crises.

Taking care of these administrative and legal items during your first year removes a massive amount of background stress from your daily life, letting you focus on actually enjoying your marriage. As you look at these action items, evaluate which deadline is approaching fastest on your calendar and tackle that one today.
