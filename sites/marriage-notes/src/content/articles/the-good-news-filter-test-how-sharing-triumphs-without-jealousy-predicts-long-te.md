When my spouse and I were trying to figure out if we were actually ready to get married, we didn’t look at our budget habits, our conflict styles, or how we handled grocery shopping. Instead, we accidentally stumbled onto a metric that I now tell every engaged couple to test: how we reacted to each other's random, everyday wins. 

Most pre-marriage advice focuses on how you fight. Can you de-escalate an argument? Do you use "I" statements? That matters, of course. But you spend a lot more of your life not fighting. You spend it watching your partner get a promotion, figure out a complex parking job, or come home excited because a stranger complimented their jacket. 

How your partner responds to those small triumphs tells you everything you need to know about the next thirty years. I call this the Good-News Filter Test.

## What the Good-News Filter Test Actually Looks Like

In psychology, there is a concept called capitalization—the way we share positive events and how our partners respond to them. When someone shares good news, a supportive partner doesn't just say "cool" and look back at their phone. They match the energy. 

Imagine this real scenario: You come home because you finally fixed a leaky faucet you've been avoiding for a month. 

*   **The Low-Filter Response:** Your partner says, "Finally, it only took you three weekends," or changes the subject to what is for dinner.
*   **The High-Filter Response:** Your partner stops what they are doing, looks at your slightly dirty hands, smiles, and says, "Seriously? Let me see! You saved us a hundred bucks and you didn't have to call that plumber who never shows up."

The difference isn't just about being nice. It is about whether your partner views your victories as a threat to their ego, an inconvenience to their schedule, or a genuine joy to be celebrated together. 

## The Four Ways People Ruin Good News

Before you commit to a lifelong contract, pay close attention to how your partner handles your moments of pride. People who struggle with long-term intimacy usually fall into one of four reaction patterns when faced with good news:

*   **The Diminisher:** They minimize your win. If you got a 90% on a tough exam, they immediately ask why you didn't get a 95%.
*   **The Hijacker:** They turn your story into an opportunity to talk about themselves. You mention a great meeting at work, and they launch into a twenty-minute story about their own terrible commute.
*   **The Spy:** They look for the hidden catch. You bought a nice jacket on sale, and they interrogate you about shipping fees and whether you actually needed it.
*   **The Ghost:** They offer a flat, unenthusiastic nod and immediately pivot back to logistics, like bills or household chores.

If you notice these patterns regularly before marriage, they rarely improve after the wedding. In fact, financial stress, house buying, and raising kids tend to amplify them because life gets busier and bandwidth shrinks.

## How to Run the Test This Week

You don't need a therapist or a compatibility workbook to try this out. You can run the Good-News Filter Test starting today with low-stakes scenarios. 

First, stop hiding your small wins. A lot of us downplay our successes because we don't want to sound braggy, even to the person we sleep next to. Force yourself to share the minor victories: finding a parking spot right in front of the post office, nailing a difficult recipe, or getting a kind email from a coworker.

Second, grade the response using a simple internal metric. Did your partner give you an active, constructive boost? Did their eyes light up? Did they ask a follow-up question that showed they cared about your happiness? 

Third, turn the mirror around. When your partner tells you they figured out how to fix their broken headphones or managed to clear their inbox by noon, catch your own internal monologue. Do you feel a tiny spike of annoyance that they are interrupting your quiet time? Do you feel a weird flash of competition? Catching your own jealousy is just as important as spotting theirs.

## Moving Forward With Clarity

Deciding to marry someone is less about finding a person you never fight with, and more about finding someone whose happiness genuinely increases your own. If you both can genuinely cheer for each other's mundane, everyday victories without keeping score, you have a solid foundation for whatever complications life throws your way.
