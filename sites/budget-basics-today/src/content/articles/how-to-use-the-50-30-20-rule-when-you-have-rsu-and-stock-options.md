Managing a budget when your compensation includes Restricted Stock Units (RSUs) or stock options feels entirely different from living on a fixed salary. Traditional budgeting advice often assumes a steady, predictable paycheck every two weeks. But when a chunk of your net worth is tied up in company shares that swing up and down with the market, fitting those windfalls into a neat financial plan gets complicated.

The 50/30/20 rule is a popular framework for a reason: it is simple. You split your after-tax income into three buckets: 50% for needs, 30% for wants, and 20% for savings and debt repayment. Yet, applying this to equity compensation requires a deliberate strategy. Here is how I adapted the 50/30/20 rule to make room for RSUs and stock options without throwing off my entire financial life.

## Treat Base Salary as Your Baseline Budget

The biggest mistake people make with equity compensation is building their lifestyle around the best-case scenario. If your company stock has been on a tear, it is easy to start spending as if your net worth is permanently higher. 

When I set up my budget, I completely exclude unvested RSUs and unexercised stock options from my monthly calculations. My 50/30/20 breakdown applies strictly to my take-home base salary. 

* **Needs (50%):** Rent, groceries, insurance, and minimum debt payments are covered entirely by my base salary. If I lose my job or the stock market crashes, my core survival expenses are secure.
* **Wants (30%):** Dining out, travel, and hobbies live here, funded only by base pay.
* **Savings (20%):** Standard retirement contributions and emergency fund transfers happen automatically from my base paycheck.

By treating base salary as 100% of my baseline income, RSUs and stock options become pure bonus material. They never fund fixed monthly expenses, which protects me if the stock takes a sudden downturn.

## Route RSUs Straight Into the 20% Bucket

RSUs are essentially cash bonuses paid in stock. The day they vest, your employer usually withholds a portion of shares to cover taxes, and the remaining shares land in your brokerage account. At that moment, those shares are treated as ordinary income.

Instead of letting vested RSUs sit in my checking account or treating them as extra spending money for wants, I route them directly into the 20% savings and wealth-building bucket. 

Here is what that looks like in practice. Imagine you have $10,000 worth of RSUs vest after taxes. Because my baseline living expenses are already covered by my salary, I treat that $10,000 as a massive accelerator for my financial goals. I immediately allocate it toward long-term priorities:

* Funding a backdoor Roth IRA.
* Boosting a taxable brokerage account for medium-term goals.
* Paying down any lingering high-interest debt.

By channeling 100% of my RSU vests into the savings category, my savings rate effectively spikes during vest months without disrupting my day-to-day lifestyle.

## Handle Stock Options Differently Based on the Type

Stock options—whether Non-Qualified Stock Options (NQSOs) or Incentive Stock Options (ISOs)—are more complex than RSUs because you have to spend your own cash to buy the underlying shares. Exercising options requires capital, and in the case of ISOs, it can trigger the dreaded Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT).

Because of this cash-flow hurdle, options do not fit neatly into a monthly percentage breakdown. Instead, I treat options as a separate project with a dedicated sinking fund.

When I plan to exercise options, I save cash specifically for the exercise cost and projected tax liabilities within my 20% bucket over several months. If I exercise and eventually sell the shares for a profit, the proceeds are split strategically: the original exercise cost goes back into my savings bucket to replenish it, and the capital gain is treated as a windfall. 

I use windfall gains to fund large, specific goals—like a home down payment or maxing out retirement accounts for the year—rather than inflating my monthly lifestyle inflation.

## Avoid the Single-Stock Trap

Holding too much of your company's stock is a double whammy of risk. If the company hits a rough patch, your stock price drops, putting your portfolio in the red. At the exact same time, your job security might be threatened. 

To keep my budget balanced and safe, I operate on a strict sell-to-diversify schedule. Whenever my RSUs vest, I evaluate my total portfolio. If my company's stock makes up more than 5% of my overall net worth, I sell a portion immediately upon vesting, regardless of whether the stock is up or down. 

The cash generated from that sale goes straight into low-cost, broad-market index funds within my 20% savings bucket. This keeps my asset allocation diversified and prevents my retirement plan from becoming overly reliant on a single employer.

Managing variable equity compensation within a structured framework takes discipline, but it prevents lifestyle creep and protects you from market volatility. By anchoring your core expenses to your base salary and letting your equity power your long-term wealth goals, you can make stock-based pay work for you. Take a look at your upcoming vesting schedule and decide today how you will allocate those shares before they even hit your account.
