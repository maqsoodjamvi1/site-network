When I first started growing my own food, I killed almost everything I touched. I bought expensive seeds, built overly complex raised beds, and stressed over every single yellow leaf. The turning point came when I stopped trying to grow fussy heirloom tomatoes and delicate greens, and instead planted vegetables that genuinely wanted to survive despite my lack of experience. 

If you are standing in the garden center wondering where to start, you need plants that give you a high return on investment without requiring a horticultural degree. More importantly, you need crops that forgive the most common beginner mistake: over-watering. Many vegetables rot and die when their roots sit in soggy soil, but these five heavy-yielding heavy-drinkers will keep producing even if you are a little too enthusiastic with the garden hose.

## Bush Zucchini

If you plant nothing else, plant a single zucchini. I usually tell beginners to start with just one plant because a healthy zucchini will easily produce ten to fifteen pounds of squash over the summer. Unlike vining winter squash, bush zucchini grows in a compact, manageable clump that fits easily into a large container or a corner of a backyard garden bed. 

Zucchini leaves are massive, which means they lose a lot of water through evaporation. They are exceptionally thirsty plants that actually thrive when the soil stays consistently moist. While you still should not turn your garden into a literal swamp, accidentally dumping an extra gallon of water on your zucchini every few days will not kill it. Plant it in full sun, give it plenty of compost, and be prepared to give away squash to your neighbors by mid-July.

## Radishes

If you have zero patience and want to see results this week, radishes are your best friend. You can drop a seed into the dirt on a Saturday, and you will often see green shoots poking through the soil by Tuesday or Wednesday. From seed to harvest takes as little as three to four weeks, making radishes the ultimate confidence booster for frustrated beginners.

Radishes love water. If they dry out, they turn woody, spicy, and almost unpleasantly hot to eat. Keeping the soil consistently damp keeps the roots growing fast, plump, and crisp. Because they grow so quickly, you can succession-plant them—meaning you drop a few new seeds into the dirt every week—so you have a continuous harvest all spring long without waiting months for a payoff.

## Swiss Chard

Leafy greens like spinach and head lettuce are notoriously dramatic. They bolt in the heat, turn bitter overnight, and collapse if they dry out for even a single afternoon. Swiss chard, on the other hand, is practically indestructible. I routinely plant the 'Bright Lights' variety, which features stems in neon shades of yellow, pink, and orange that look just as good in a flower bed as they do in the vegetable patch.

Chard handles heavy watering with ease, and its thick, rubbery leaves bounce right back if they take a beating from a heavy summer thunderstorm. The best part about chard is the "cut-and-come-again" harvest method. Instead of pulling up the whole plant, you simply snip off the outer leaves with a pair of kitchen scissors, leaving the center of the plant intact to keep growing new leaves for months.

## Green Bush Beans

Pole beans need intricate trellises, teepees, and vertical support systems that can overwhelm a first-time gardener. Bush beans, however, grow as sturdy, self-supporting little shrubs about eighteen inches tall. They require zero construction projects and start producing pods in under two months.

While beans generally dislike sitting in standing water for weeks on end, bush beans are remarkably resilient against over-watering compared to fragile root crops like carrots. They have strong root systems that anchor them firmly in the soil. 
* Pick a sunny spot in your yard or a wide patio container.
* Push seeds about an inch deep into the dirt, spaced roughly three inches apart.
* Water them thoroughly right after planting, and keep the soil damp until sprouts appear.
* Harvest the pods regularly every couple of days; the more you pick, the more beans the plant produces.

## Cherry Tomatoes

While slicing beefsteak tomatoes can be a finicky nightmare prone to splitting, blossom end rot, and fungal diseases, cherry tomatoes are absolute machines. Varieties like 'Sweet Million' or 'Sungold' produce hundreds of bite-sized fruits per plant, ensuring a steady harvest from mid-summer straight through the first autumn frost.

Tomatoes are heavy drinkers. If you have ever forgotten to water a standard tomato plant, you know how quickly they wilt into sad, drooping tragedies. However, cherry tomatoes bounce back from a heavy soaking better than almost any other fruiting vegetable. 
* Buy a sturdy tomato cage or a heavy stake to support the weight of the branches.
* Plant them deep—burying the lower third of the stem—to encourage a massive, drought- and flood-tolerant root system.
* Water deeply at the base of the plant whenever the top inch of soil feels dry.

Getting started with growing your own food does not have to be an expensive or stressful experiment. By sticking to these forgiving, heavy-yielding crops, you can build your gardening confidence and harvest a basket full of fresh produce within your very first season.
