Raised beds completely changed how I look at vegetable gardening. A few years ago, after spending an entire weekend battling my heavy, clay-heavy backyard soil—and barely harvesting a handful of stunted carrots—I built my first two raised cedar boxes. The difference was night and day. Because I could control the exact soil mix, I eliminated weeds, improved drainage, and stopped bending down so far. 

If you are planning your own raised bed setup this season, picking the right crops makes all the difference. While you can technically grow almost anything in a raised bed, some vegetables practically grow themselves, giving you a massive harvest with very little daily fuss. 

Here are six foolproof vegetables that I rely on every single year for easy, high-yield success.

## Bush Beans
If you want instant gratification and a high volume of food with almost zero effort, plant bush beans. Unlike pole beans, bush beans do not require trellises, netting, or complicated setup. You simply poke the seeds about an inch deep into your raised bed soil, water them in, and wait.

They germinate quickly in warm soil and grow into compact, bushy plants roughly eighteen inches tall. Within about fifty to sixty days, you will be out in your garden harvesting handfuls of crisp green beans. They also fix nitrogen in the soil, meaning they actually feed your raised bed while they grow. Pro tip: Plant a new small batch every three weeks through mid-summer to keep a continuous harvest going until the first frost.

## Radishes
If patience is not your strong suit, radishes are your best friend. From the day you drop the seed into your raised bed to the moment you pull a crisp, spicy radish, only about three to four weeks pass. 

Radishes thrive in the loose, fluffy soil typical of raised beds. Because you aren't fighting compacted earth, your radishes won't turn out crooked or woody. Plant them along the edges of your beds or as markers between slower-growing crops like tomatoes. They take up almost no space and get out of the way before larger plants need the room. Just make sure to pull them promptly when they reach about the size of a quarter; leaving them in the ground too long makes them hot and spongy.

## Lettuce and Leafy Greens
Supermarket greens get expensive, and they often turn to mush in your crisper drawer within days of buying them. Growing your own lettuce in a raised bed solves this completely. Varieties like black-seeded simpson, romaine, and loose-leaf mixes love the moderate moisture and loose structure of garden beds.

Instead of harvesting the whole head at once, use the "cut-and-come-again" method. Snip off the outer leaves for your evening salad with kitchen scissors, leaving the inner core intact. The plant will keep pushing out new leaves for weeks. Because raised beds sit higher off the ground, your greens stay cleaner, too—you won't find nearly as much splashed mud on the leaves after a heavy rain.

## Zucchini 
Planting zucchini in a raised bed feels like a bit of a magic trick, provided you give it enough space. One or two plants will easily feed an entire neighborhood by mid-summer. Zucchini seeds sprout rapidly, and the plants quickly form large, dramatic leaves that shade out any stray weeds trying to poke through your soil.

The main thing to keep in mind with zucchini is real estate. A single plant easily spreads three feet wide, so give it a dedicated corner of your bed. Check your plants every single day once they start producing; zucchini has a sneaky habit of hiding under big leaves and growing into baseball bats overnight. Pick them when they are about six to eight inches long for the best flavor and texture.

## Sugar Snap Peas
If you have a sunny spot with a fence, trellis, or even a simple wire cage attached to the back of your raised bed, sugar snap peas are an absolute joy to grow. They are cool-weather crops, meaning you can plant them very early in the spring—often several weeks before your last frost date—when other garden tasks are still on hold.

The seeds handle chilly soil with ease. Once the vines climb up your support structure, they produce delicate white flowers followed by crisp, sweet pods that you can eat straight off the vine. My kids rarely make it inside with a full basket because they eat the harvest while wandering around the yard. 

## Cherry Tomatoes
While slicing tomatoes can sometimes be finicky about watering schedules and fungal diseases, cherry tomatoes are notoriously forgiving. Pop a single indeterminate cherry tomato plant into a large raised bed with a sturdy tomato cage or stake, and let it do its thing.

Cherry tomatoes produce fruit in massive clusters all season long, right up until the autumn chill sets in. They bounce back quickly from minor neglect, and the sheer volume of fruit means that even if a few split from heavy rain, you will still have more than you can possibly eat. Pair them with a few basil plants tucked right at the base of the tomato for a classic, low-maintenance garden pairing.

Getting your raised beds up and running requires a bit of heavy lifting upfront when you fill them with soil and compost, but choosing the right crops ensures your hard work pays off immediately. As you start mapping out your layout for the season, consider which of these foolproof options match your family's favorite meals and local climate.
