Starting a vegetable garden for the first time can quickly become overwhelming. Seed packets promise the world, but if you plant something fussy like heirloom tomatoes or head cauliflower as a novice, you are likely to spend your weekends battling pests, blossom end rot, and split heads. When I first started growing my own food, I made the classic mistake of planting twenty different varieties. Half of them died, and the ones that survived yielded barely enough for a single salad. 

Over the years, I learned a simple truth: if you want a rewarding first garden, you need to focus on heavy-yielding, resilient crops that form the backbone of your kitchen pantry. You want vegetables that forgive a missed watering, fight off common bugs, and produce enough volume that you actually feel like you accomplished something. 

Here are five of the easiest, bulk-harvest vegetables I rely on every single year to fill my pantry without the headache.

## 1. Bush Beans
If you want instant gratification and a massive return on investment, plant bush beans. Unlike pole beans, which require tall trellises and complicated support structures, bush beans grow as sturdy, independent little bushes about two feet tall. 

I plant green bean seeds directly in the soil about an inch deep after the last spring frost. Within a week, the seedlings pop up. Because beans fix their own nitrogen in the soil, they do not demand heavy fertilization. Once they start producing, you will need to harvest every couple of days. A ten-foot row will easily yield enough beans to eat fresh all summer, with plenty left over to blanch and freeze for winter stir-fries and soups. 

## 2. Zucchini and Summer Squash
There is an old gardening joke that if you plant two zucchini seeds, you will soon be locking your doors at night so neighbors cannot sneak extra zucchini onto your porch. It is funny because it is true. Zucchini is one of the most prolific plants on the planet.

Plant these in raised mounds of soil enriched with a bit of compost. Give them about three feet of space in every direction because the leaves get massive. The main challenge with zucchini is powdery mildew later in the summer, but by the time the leaves start looking ragged around August, you will already have harvested more squash than you know what to do with. Slice them into rounds, dehydrate them, or shred them for freezer-friendly baking.

## 3. Garlic
Garlic is the ultimate low-maintenance pantry staple because it operates on a completely different timeline than almost everything else in your garden. You do not plant it in the spring; you plant it in the fall, right around the time you are putting your garden to bed for the winter.

Separate a bulb of grocery store organic garlic into individual cloves, keeping the papery skin intact. Push each clove about two inches into the dirt, pointy side up, spaced six inches apart. Then, walk away. Rain and snow will do the work for you. By next summer, you will harvest whole bulbs that taste infinitely sharper and more complex than anything you buy at the supermarket. Braindead simple, highly rewarding, and essential for the kitchen.

## 4. Potatoes
Growing potatoes feels a bit like magic. You take a scarred, sprouting potato from the back of your pantry, cut it into chunks, bury it in the dirt, and months later, you unearth a hidden treasure trove. 

You do not even need a massive plot of land for potatoes. You can grow a bountiful harvest in a simple five-gallon plastic bucket with drainage holes drilled into the bottom. Start with a few inches of soil, drop in your seed potato chunks, and as the green stalks grow upward, keep covering them with more soil or straw. When the foliage turns yellow and dies back in the late summer, dump the bucket out onto a tarp. You will harvest pounds of dense, storable spuds that keep for months in a cool, dark cupboard.

## 5. Onion Sets
Growing onions from tiny seeds can test the patience of a saint. They start out looking like blades of grass and take forever to size up. Instead, buy a bag of onion sets—which are essentially miniature, dormant onion bulbs—from your local nursery in early spring.

Push the sets directly into loose soil just deep enough that the tips peek out. They require very little attention beyond weeding and regular watering. As they grow, you can pull a few early ones as green scallions. Leave the rest until the tops fall over in mid-summer, then pull them up, let them dry in the sun for a few days, and store them in mesh bags. You will have your own cooking onions for months.

When you are ready to transition from planning to planting, taking a little time to map out your space and soil preparation will make all the difference for your harvest day success.
