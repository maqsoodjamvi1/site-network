When I first got serious about growing my own food, I made the classic mistake of planting a massive patch of annual vegetables. By mid-July, I was spending every weekend weeding, staking tomatoes, and replanting crops that bolted in the heat. By October, I was exhausted. 

That experience taught me a valuable shortcut: perennial vegetables. Unlike annuals that die off every winter and force you to start from scratch, perennials establish a root system and come back year after year with very little help. If you want a productive garden without turning your life into a farming apprenticeship, these five crops are the best place to start.

## Asparagus

Asparagus is the ultimate delayed-gratification crop, but it is surprisingly easy to manage once you understand its rhythm. You do not grow asparagus from tiny seeds unless you have immense patience. Instead, you buy one-year-old crowns—which look like dried-out, giant spiders—and plant them in a trench about eight inches deep in early spring. 

The trick to asparagus is restraint. For the first two years after planting, you must let the spears grow into tall, ferny bushes. This allows the plant to feed its roots. By the third spring, you can finally harvest the tender spears for about four to six weeks. After that, you stop harvesting and let the ferns grow again. Plant them in a sunny spot with well-drained soil, keep the weeds down while they are young, and you will get a delicious spring harvest for up to fifteen years without replanting.

## Rhubarb

If you live in a region with cold winters, rhubarb is nearly foolproof. It thrives on neglect, provided it gets full sun and rich, moist soil. I planted a single crown in a neglected corner of my yard, and within two years, it was producing massive, glossy leaves and thick, tart stalks.

Harvesting rhubarb is simple: you never cut the stalks. Instead, you grasp the base of the stalk, pull firmly, and give it a slight twist, and it will snap cleanly away from the crown. Always leave at least a few stalks on the plant so it can keep growing. Keep in mind that while the stalks make amazing crumbles and jams, the leaves contain high levels of oxalic acid and belong in the compost pile, not the dinner plate. 

## Jerusalem Artichokes (Sunchokes)

Do not let the name fool you—these plants are not artichokes, and they did not come from Jerusalem. They are actually a type of native North American sunflower that produces knobby, tuberous roots beneath the soil. Sunchokes are so tough that they practically grow themselves. 

Plant the tubers in the spring, and by late summer, you will have towering yellow flowers that reach eight feet tall, acting as a great natural privacy screen. In the late fall, after the frost kills the stalks back, you dig up the tubers just like potatoes. They have a nutty, crunchy texture similar to water chestnuts. Make sure you plant them in a spot where you do not mind them staying permanently, because any tiny piece of tuber left in the ground will sprout into a new plant next spring.

## Horseradish

If you want a vegetable that can outlast almost any pest, weather event, or gardening mistake, plant horseradish. I put a single root cutting into a corner of my raised bed, and it has reliably returned every single year with zero maintenance. 

Horseradish requires deep, loose soil if you want straight roots, but it will grow almost anywhere. The real work with horseradish happens at harvest time in the late autumn. You dig up the thick taproot, brush off the dirt, and peel it. Grating horseradish releases powerful vapors that will clear your sinuses instantly, so I always recommend grating it outdoors or near an open window. Save a small side root, replant it in the dirt, and your patch will be renewed for the following season.

## Bunching Onions (Perennial Scallions)

Standard bulb onions require a lot of attention regarding soil moisture, day length, and curing. Perennial bunching onions, sometimes called Welsh onions or walking onions, bypass all of that hassle. They form tight clumps of green shoots that taste just like scallions and stay green for most of the year.

Unlike regular onions, these plants multiply underground, creating a wider clump each season. You can snip the green tops all summer long to use in salads and stir-fries, and the plant simply grows more. Every couple of years, you can dig up the clump, divide the individual bulbs, and spread them to other parts of your garden. They take up very little space and tolerate poor soil better than almost any other garden vegetable I have grown.

Adding perennial vegetables to your yard shifts your relationship with gardening from a frantic annual chore to a relaxed, long-term routine. To get started this season, pick just one or two of these crops that match your local climate and culinary tastes, prepare a small dedicated patch of soil, and let nature do the heavy lifting for years to come.
