When I first started growing my own food, I made the classic rookie mistake of buying twenty different seed packets, building elaborate raised beds, and spending every weekend hauling hoses. By August, half my crops had bolted, pests had wiped out my squash, and I felt thoroughly defeated. 

The turning point for my garden came when I discovered self-sowing annuals and hardy perennials. These are the plants that drop their seeds at the end of the season, sleep through the winter, and pop right back up in the spring with almost zero help from me. If you want the satisfaction of harvesting homegrown food without chaining yourself to a watering can all summer, you need to start with crops that practically grow themselves. 

Here are five of the easiest, most reliable self-sowing and perennial vegetables that will return to your yard year after year.

## 1. Jerusalem Artichokes (Sunchokes)

Do not let the name fool you—these are neither from Jerusalem nor are they artichokes. They are actually a species of native North American sunflower that produces knobby, crisp tubers underground. The flavor is nutty and similar to a water chestnut. 

Sunchokes are aggressively hardy. Plant the tubers about four inches deep in a sunny spot in early spring. Once they are established, they will grow into towering, yellow-blossomed stalks up to ten feet tall by late summer. In the fall, after the frost kills the stalks, you can dig up the roots. 

The secret to their return is simple: you will inevitably miss a few small tubers while digging. Those overlooked bits underground will sprout next spring, giving you a perpetual harvest. Just make sure to plant them in a corner of the yard where you do not mind a tall, permanent screen of foliage.

## 2. Arugula

If you buy bagged arugula at the grocery store, you already know it turns to mush in a few days and costs a small fortune. Growing your own is dead simple, and once you plant it once, you essentially have it for life.

Sow arugula seeds directly in the dirt as soon as the ground can be worked in early spring. It grows at lightning speed, letting you snip baby greens in about three weeks. If you let a few plants in the corner of your bed go to flower, they will shoot up tall stems with delicate white blossoms, get tough, and eventually drop thousands of tiny pepper-flavored seeds into the soil. 

By mid-summer, the parent plants will die off, but as soon as the late summer rains cool things down, you will see a dense carpet of tiny new arugula seedlings. They shrug off light frosts, meaning you can often harvest fresh greens well into November in many US growing zones.

## 3. Walking onions (Egyptian Walking Onions)

Standard bulb onions can be finicky for beginners, but walking onions are practically indestructible. Instead of growing traditional seeds, these perennial onions grow small bulbils (called topsets) right at the top of their tall green stalks. 

As the topsets get heavier by mid-summer, the stalk literally bends over and drops the baby bulbs onto the soil a few inches away from the mother plant. This is why they are called "walking"—they slowly march across your garden bed over the years. 

You can eat the green shoots like scallions in the spring, use the base bulbs like standard onions, or harvest the little topsets to pickle or replant. Stick a few bulbs in the ground this weekend, walk away, and let them multiply on their own schedule.

## 4. Sorrel

If you want a leafy green that does not bolt the second the temperature hits eighty degrees, French sorrel is your best friend. It is a perennial herb with lemony, tangy leaves that add a serious punch to salads and soups.

Buy a potted sorrel plant from a local nursery, or start it from seed in the spring. Plant it in a spot with partial shade or full sun. Once its roots dig in, sorrel comes back every single year, pushing up fresh green leaves weeks before anything else in the garden wakes up. 

To keep it productive, you just need to shear the flower stalks off in mid-summer so the plant focuses its energy on making leaves rather than seeds. It handles winter freezes down to USDA Zone 4 without blinking an eye.

## 5. Bush Beans (Left to Dry)

Most gardeners treat green beans as a one-and-done summer crop, pulling up the spent plants in September. But if you leave a few pods on the vine until they turn brittle and brown, you unlock the magic of dry beans.

* Let a half-dozen bean pods dry completely on the plant until the seeds rattle inside.
* Shell the dry seeds into a glass jar and store them in your pantry over the winter.
* Plant those exact seeds back into the garden next spring after the last frost date.

By saving your own seeds from the plants that survived your specific backyard climate and pest pressures, you are inadvertently breeding a strain of vegetables uniquely adapted to your exact patch of dirt. 

To turn these low-maintenance ideas into a working plan for your yard this season, look at your available sunny spots and pick just two of these crops to start with. Laying down a simple base of perennials and self-sowers now sets you up for a much easier, more abundant harvest next spring.
