If you have ever noticed a strange, crusty white or yellowish powder forming on the top of your houseplant’s soil, or clinging to the outside of a terracotta pot, you are looking at mineral salt buildup. When I first started collecting houseplants, I thought this was some kind of harmless mold. It turns out, it is actually a chemical residue left behind by tap water and liquid fertilizers. 

Over time, these salts accumulate in the potting mix. Eventually, they create a toxic environment that burns roots and blocks your plants from absorbing vital moisture. When this happens, you have a choice to make: should you flush the soil with water, or is it time to completely repot the plant? Here is how I figure out which approach to take, depending on how severe the salt damage has gotten.

## What Causes Mineral Salt Buildup?

Every time you water your houseplants, you introduce tiny amounts of minerals like calcium, magnesium, and sodium. If you use standard tap water, these minerals stay behind in the soil as the moisture evaporates. 

Liquid fertilizers make this happen even faster. Most commercial fertilizers are essentially mineral salts dissolved in water. When your plant drinks the water, it leaves the salts behind. In nature, heavy rainfalls naturally wash these excess minerals deep down and out of the soil. But inside a potted plant, those salts have nowhere to go. They accumulate trip after trip, cycle after cycle, until the soil chemistry turns hostile.

You will usually spot the warning signs before the plant outright dies. The leaves might develop brown, crispy tips even though you are watering on a regular schedule. Growth might stall completely during the spring and summer. Sometimes, you will see white crust scaling the rim of the pot or crusting over the topsoil.

## When to Choose Leaching (Flushing the Soil)

Flushing—sometimes called leaching—is my go-to method when the plant is otherwise healthy, the soil is relatively fresh, and the salt crust is mostly cosmetic or mild. You do not need to disturb the roots; you just need to dissolve and wash away the excess minerals.

To flush a plant properly, take it to the sink, bathroom, or a bucket outside. Slowly pour room-temperature water through the soil, letting it stream freely out of the drainage holes. I like to let the water run for a solid two or three minutes. This thoroughly dissolves the trapped salts and flushes them out the bottom. 

Let the pot drain completely before you put it back on its saucer. Never let a flushed plant sit in a puddle of drainage water, or the salts you just washed out will simply get sucked right back up into the roots. If your tap water is exceptionally hard, consider using filtered or rain water for this final rinse.

## When to Drop Everything and Repot

Sometimes, flushing simply is not enough. If the salt buildup has gone on too long, leaching the soil might actually make things worse by shocking an already stressed root system. 

You need to skip flushing and move straight to repotting in a few specific scenarios:
* **Severe root burn:** If you pull the plant out of its nursery pot and notice the roots are brown, brittle, or mushy near the surface where the salt crust is thickest.
* **Hydrophobic soil:** When old soil breaks down, it can harden into a brick-like texture. Water will simply channel down the sides of the pot and run right out without wetting the root ball.
* **Aged potting mix:** If the plant has been in the same soil for more than two years, the organic matter has decomposed, leaving behind a dense, highly saline environment that no amount of flushing can fix.

When I repot a salt-damaged plant, I gently brush away as much of the old, crusty soil from the root ball as possible. I also rinse the roots under lukewarm water to wash away lingering residue. From there, plant it in fresh, nutrient-rich potting mix using a clean container. 

## Preventing Future Buildup

Once you solve your current salt crisis, you can keep it from happening again with a few simple habits. Instead of giving your plants tiny sips of water every few days, water them thoroughly until it runs out the drainage hole every single time, and empty the catch saucers shortly after. This prevents minerals from concentrating near the surface. 

When fertilizing during the growing season, cut the recommended manufacturer dose in half. Houseplants generally need much less food than the label suggests. Finally, consider giving all your potted plants a thorough flush every three to four months as preventative maintenance, stopping salt accumulation long before it ever has a chance to damage your green collection.

To figure out your next steps, take a close look at your plant's soil surface and overall vigor today to decide whether a simple kitchen sink flush will do the trick or if it is time to grab a bag of fresh potting soil.
