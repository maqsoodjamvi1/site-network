When I first started growing my own food, I made the classic rookie mistake: I bought thirty different seed packets, spent an entire weekend meticulously measuring rows, and waited for my backyard paradise to sprout. By August, half of my plants had succumbed to pests, and the other half required more maintenance than my day job. 

I almost gave up until an older neighbor handed me a handful of ugly, wrinkly bean pods and told me to throw them in a neglected corner of my yard and ignore them. 

That experience changed how I garden. If you want the satisfaction of harvesting your own food without spending your entire weekend weeding, watering, and replanting every single spring, you need to look at plants that do the heavy lifting for you. Specifically, you want crops that not only thrive on neglect but drop their own seeds at the end of the season to return all over again next year. 

Here are five of the easiest self-seeding vegetables you can plant once and enjoy for years.

## 1. Arugula (Rocket)
Most leafy greens are delicate divas that bolt, turn bitter, and die the second a heatwave hits. Arugula takes a different approach. It grows aggressively in cool spring weather, shoots up tall flower stalks as it warms up, and produces thousands of tiny, peppery seeds inside delicate pods. 

Let a few plants go to seed in the corner of your raised bed instead of ripping them out when they start tasting spicy. By mid-summer, those pods will dry out and pop, scattering seeds across your soil. When the autumn rains arrive—or early next spring—you will wake up to a dense carpet of baby arugula volunteers. Thin them out to a few inches apart, and you have a free salad bar with zero extra effort.

## 2. Walking onions (Egyptian onions)
Standard bulb onions require a lot of attention, but walking onions are practically bulletproof perennials. Instead of growing a single bulb underground that you have to harvest and replant, these plants form clusters of small bulbs right at the top of their green stalks. 

As the top-heavy stalks grow taller through the summer, they eventually become too heavy, bend over, and touch the ground. The little top-sets root themselves into the soil a few feet away from the mother plant, "walking" across your garden bed over the seasons. Once you establish a small patch of walking onions, you can snip the green chive-like tops in the spring and harvest the small underground bulbs in the fall, all without ever buying another onion set.

## 3. Bush beans (Left to dry)
While most people pick green beans while they are tender and juicy, you can trick a standard bush bean patch into perennial self-sufficiency by changing your harvesting strategy. Pick your fill of green beans all summer, but leave the last dozen pods on the vine until the entire plant turns brown, brittle, and dead in the autumn. 

The pods will dry out into rattles, packed with hard, colorful seeds. When you clear out your garden beds for winter, intentionally shake those dry pods over the soil, or simply let the pods drop naturally. When the soil warms up after the last frost, those dropped seeds will sprout into a brand-new crop of bush beans. Just make sure to rotate where they grow each year to keep the soil healthy.

## 4. Orach (Mountain spinach)
If you live in a region with scorching summers, traditional spinach turns to mush in June. Orach, an ancient leafy green also known as mountain spinach, looks and tastes remarkably similar to spinach, but it thrives in the face of intense heat and poor soil. 

Orach grows into a striking, bushy plant that can reach up to six feet tall if left unchecked, with leaves ranging in color from bright green to deep magenta. Because it produces massive amounts of flat, papery seeds, it is arguably the most prolific self-seeder in my backyard. I planted a single packet of red orach seeds four years ago, and I have not had to buy or plant a single seed since; they simply return every April like clockwork.

## 5. Tomatillos
If you want a fruit-bearing crop that requires almost no intervention, skip fussy slicing tomatoes and plant a pair of tomatillos. Tomatillos are exceptionally tough, naturally resistant to many of the pests that plague standard tomatoes, and loaded with seeds inside their papery husks. 

Drop a few fallen fruits into the dirt at the end of the season and leave them there. The fruit will rot away over the winter, pressing the seeds directly into the earth. In late spring, you will likely find dozens of tiny tomatillo sprouts emerging from that exact spot. You will need to pull out the weaker seedlings and leave only the strongest three or four to grow, but you will instantly save yourself the trouble of starting seeds indoors under grow lights.

Getting started with these self-seeding crops is mostly a matter of changing your definition of a "messy" garden. Letting a few plants go to flower, drop their leaves, and look a little unkempt at the end of the season is the secret to a low-maintenance harvest. Take a look at your available growing space this weekend, pick just one or two of these resilient crops to try out, and let nature handle the replanting for you next year.
