Sunday evenings used to fill me with a very specific kind of dread. Around 6 PM, right as the sun started going down, my stomach would tighten. I would start running through emails in my head, worrying about Monday morning meetings, and feeling like the weekend had vanished without giving me a chance to actually rest. 

If you live in the US and work a standard 48-hour or 40-hour week, you probably know this script by heart. We spend Saturday running errands and Sunday bracing for impact. 

A few years ago, I realized that waiting until I crashed on Friday night to practice self-care was entirely backwards. By then, the damage of the workweek was already done. True energy protection has to happen at the seam between the weekend and the workweek. 

Over time, I built a reliable system of micro-steps to shield my bandwidth before Monday morning hits. Here is the exact checklist I use every Sunday to reclaim my peace.

## 1. Do a 10-Minute "Brain Dump" and Triage

When your brain is carrying tomorrow's to-do list alongside tonight's relaxation, your nervous system can't tell the difference between actual danger and a pending spreadsheet. 

Instead of trying to keep everything in my head, I sit down on Sunday afternoon with a blank piece of paper and a pen. I set a timer for 10 minutes and write down every single task, worry, obligation, and random thought floating around in my brain. I don't organize it at first; I just evacuate my skull onto the page.

Once the timer goes off, I look at the list with a ruthless filter. I ask myself two questions: *What actually has to happen on Monday?* and *What can wait until Wednesday, or be dropped entirely?* I pick three—and only three—priorities for Monday. Everything else goes into a "parking lot" notebook. 

By getting the chaos out of my head and onto paper, my body immediately stops bracing for a catastrophe that hasn't happened yet.

## 2. Execute a "Friction Audit" of Your Morning Routine

Monday morning failure is almost never a motivation problem; it is a friction problem. If you have to make ten small decisions before you leave the house—what to wear, what to pack for lunch, where your keys are—you burn through your daily supply of willpower before 8 AM.

I use Sunday afternoon to remove every possible point of friction between my bed and my desk. 
* **The Outfit Rule:** I pick out my clothes down to my socks and hang them on the bathroom door. 
* **The Kitchen Sweep:** I run the dishwasher on Sunday afternoon so the sink is clear for Monday's coffee mug.
* **The Bag Check:** My laptop charger, ID badge, and headphones go directly into my work bag before I sit down for dinner.

When Monday morning arrives, I don't have to think. I just execute. That smoothness is a massive gift to your nervous system.

## 3. Set a Hard Boundary on Sunday Digital Consumption

At around 7 PM on Sunday, I implement a strict digital sundown. For me, this means no Slack notifications on my phone, no professional email checks, and no scrolling through news feeds that spike my cortisol.

Many of us check our work email on Sunday night under the guise of "getting ahead." In reality, you aren't getting ahead; you are just starting your workweek twelve hours early, for zero extra pay and zero actual productivity. An email read at 8 PM on the couch cannot be answered until Monday morning anyway, but reading it guarantees your brain spends the rest of the evening digesting a problem.

If you must check your schedule, do it with an intentional time limit. Better yet, turn your phone on "Do Not Disturb," put it in a drawer, and replace the scroll with a low-stakes analog activity like reading a physical book, stretching on the floor, or making a complicated tea.

## 4. Design an Anchor Meal for Sunday Night

Dinner on Sunday often falls into one of two bad categories: chaotic takeout because you forgot to buy groceries, or heavy, complicated cooking that leaves you with a mountain of dishes right before bed.

Instead, I rely on what I call an "anchor meal"—something deeply comforting, easy to prepare, and incapable of going wrong. It acts as a physical marker that the weekend is winding down in a gentle way. 

For me, this is usually a simple sheet-pan dinner or a pot of soup that requires minimal active monitoring. The goal isn't culinary ambition; the goal is warmth, predictability, and giving yourself something nice to look forward to while you wind down.

Protecting your energy before the workweek doesn't require a weekend spa retreat or an elaborate four-hour routine. It simply requires a few deliberate boundaries to keep the chaos of Monday from bleeding into your rest.
