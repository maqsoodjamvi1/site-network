When a relationship ends, the emotional fallout usually gets all the attention. We focus on the awkwardness of dividing up furniture, the heavy quiet of an empty side of the bed, and the exhaustion of changing your relationship status. But there is a very physical, highly practical corner of a breakup that almost everyone overlooks until they desperately need it: the shared medicine cabinet and bathroom drawers. 

Going through a bathroom stash you built together is surprisingly jarring. One minute you are looking for a stray bobby pin, and the next you are holding a bottle of prescription allergy medication with your ex's name printed on the label, or a bulk pack of ibuprofen you bought together at Costco during a weekend grocery run. Handling these items requires a mix of safety precautions and emotional self-preservation. Here is how to tackle the drawer cleanse without spiraling or putting your health at risk.

## Stage One: The Rules of Triage

Before you pull a single bottle out from under the sink, set some ground rules. Do not try to do this while you are already emotionally frayed, running late for work, or drinking wine alone at 11:00 PM. Pick a neutral time, put on a podcast or some upbeat music, and treat it like a simple household chore rather than a sentimental archeological dig.

Separate your inventory into three distinct categories: strictly personal items, shared over-the-counter supplies, and prescription drugs. Your approach to each of these needs to be completely different. Never casually toss a prescription bottle into the household trash, and never mail your ex a half-used tube of medicated cream just because you feel guilty about the breakup. Practicality has to override sentimentality here.

## Stage Two: Handling Prescriptions and Controlled Substances

Prescription medications are the trickiest part of the bathroom cleanup. If your ex left behind active prescriptions, do not keep them in your medicine cabinet, and do not flush them down the toilet. Flushing medications contaminates local water supplies, and holding onto someone else's prescription—especially if it is a controlled substance like an anxiety medication, sleep aid, or painkiller—is a legal and safety liability you do not need in your life.

If you are on good enough terms with your ex to coordinate a clean break, text them a simple, emotion-free logistical message: *"Hey, I'm clearing out the bathroom cabinets and found your prescription for [Medication Name]. Let me know if you want to pick it up from my porch by Thursday, or if I should safely dispose of it."* 

If you are no contact, or if they ignore the message, look up local drug take-back options. Many local pharmacies, police stations, and fire departments have permanent drop-off boxes. If those aren't accessible, you can mix most pills with an unappealing substance like used coffee grounds or cat litter in a sealed plastic bag and throw that bag into your household trash. 

## Stage Three: Over-the-Counter Essentials and First-Aid Stash

The bulk of a shared bathroom drawer usually consists of generic, over-the-counter mainstays: a giant bottle of ibuprofen, boxes of band-aids, half-empty bottles of aloe vera, and antacids. You do not need to throw away perfectly good medical supplies just because your ex touched them once, but you do need to audit them for safety and expiration dates.

*   **Check the expiration dates:** Cold medicine, pain relievers, and eye drops lose potency over time. If that box of allergy pills expired two years ago, throw it out regardless of who bought it.
*   **Assess hygiene:** Toss open tubs of topical ointments or creams where fingers were regularly dipped inside. Keep sealed items like unopened boxes of bandages, gauze, or fresh toothbrushes (if you bought a multipack).
*   **Split or keep:** If there is a brand-new, family-sized bottle of pain reliever and you are the one who actually uses it for migraines, keep it. If you want a completely fresh start and the sight of that specific brand triggers a memory of a specific Sunday morning hangover, pitch it and buy your own small bottle next time you are at the store.

## Stage Four: Resetting Your Personal Space

Once the sorting is done, take everything that belongs to your ex and put it in a single paper bag out of sight. Then, clean the physical space. Wipe down the shelves, throw away the ancient hair ties, and restock the cabinet with items you actually bought for yourself. 

Creating a medical and first-aid stash that is entirely yours might seem like a small detail, but it acts as a quiet, physical reclaiming of your daily routine. When you reach for a headache pill next Tuesday, you won't have to navigate someone else's leftover medical history. 

As you finish clearing out the physical clutter of your shared past, it is completely normal to notice other areas of your shared life that still need untangling, from digital subscriptions to living arrangements, which can feel overwhelming to manage on your own.
