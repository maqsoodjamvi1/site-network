Asking for a pay increase is already one of the most nerve-wracking parts of having a job. Add an extended medical leave for your manager into the mix, and the process feels almost impossible. You might find yourself wondering whether you should wait months for them to return, or if pausing your financial progress is unfair to you. 

When your direct supervisor is out of the office on medical leave—whether it is for six weeks or six months—your career momentum does not have to stall. Here is how I navigated this exact situation, and the practical steps you can take to advocate for your pay when your boss is away.

## Figure Out Who Holds the Decision-Making Power

The biggest mistake you can make is waiting around for your absent manager to process a compensation review. If they are completely out of contact due to medical reasons, they cannot advocate for your budget, sign off on paperwork, or sit in on approval meetings with HR. 

You need to identify who has stepped into their shoes. Look at the temporary reporting structure:
* **The Interim Manager:** Often, a peer of your manager or a director from another department takes over day-to-day oversight. 
* **The Skip-Level Manager:** Your manager’s boss might be handling approvals directly while the leave is ongoing.
* **Human Resources:** HR usually manages the operational workflow for out-of-cycle compensation adjustments during extended leaves.

Check your company directory or organizational chart to confirm who your official temporary point of contact is. Schedule a one-on-one meeting with that person to introduce yourself and establish a working relationship before you bring up money.

## Build a Standalone Case Based on Extra Work

If your manager’s absence means you have absorbed extra responsibilities, you have a strong, factual basis for a raise. However, you cannot just say, "I am doing more work." You need to quantify it.

When my own manager went on a four-month medical leave, I took over two of their recurring team syncs and became the primary escalation point for client emergencies. Instead of venting to my coworkers about the extra stress, I kept a running log of everything outside my normal job description that crossed my desk. 

When you sit down with the interim decision-maker, present a clear breakdown of your expanded scope. Use concrete examples:
* "Since Sarah went on leave in October, I have taken over weekly reporting for the Southeast region, which adds about four hours of data analysis to my plate each week."
* "I am now the primary trainer for the two new hires who started last month, a task that previously fell to my supervisor."

This shifts the conversation from subjective feelings about your workload to an objective business case for why your current pay no longer matches your current job description.

## Timing Matters More Than Ever

Timing a raise request is tricky under normal circumstances, but it is even more sensitive when dealing with an executive medical leave. You want to avoid asking during the chaotic first two weeks of their departure, when the department is still figuring out basic operations. You also want to avoid springing it on them during company-wide budget freezes at the end of the fiscal quarter.

Instead, look for a sweet spot. Aim for a few weeks after the interim management structure has stabilized, or tie your request to a natural checkpoint, such as the completion of a major project your team successfully delivered without your boss. 

Send a calendar invite to the covering manager or skip-level leader with a transparent subject line, such as "Compensation and Role Review." Do not ambush them in the hallway or bring it up casually at the end of a status update meeting. Give them a few days to mentally prepare for the conversation.

## Prepare for the Inevitability of Consultations

Do not be surprised if your interim manager tells you, "I agree you deserve this, but we need to wait for Sarah to return so she can weigh in." 

This is a common stall tactic, but it is also a legitimate logistical hurdle. If you hit this roadblock, do not just accept a flat "no." Propose a compromise that respects your manager's privacy while protecting your timeline:
* Ask if the interim manager can submit a provisional recommendation to HR now, pending a final sign-off.
* Ask for a hard calendar date to revisit the conversation, tied to your absent manager's expected return date, rather than a vague "let's check back later."
* Get clarity on whether the company's annual review cycle is your better path forward, especially if the return date is only a few weeks away.

Navigating a pay raise while your direct boss is out requires extra diplomacy, but it is entirely doable if you focus on the business impact of your work and communicate clearly with the people temporarily holding the keys to the budget.
