When my direct manager announced she was leaving for a competitor last year, my first thought wasn't about the transition plan. It was about my upcoming performance review and the merit raise I had been planning to ask for. The ground had just shifted beneath my feet. If you are in the same boat, you might feel like your window of opportunity just slammed shut. After all, who is going to advocate for your pay bump if the person who knows your daily output is walking out the door?

Through trial, error, and a fair bit of awkward conversation, I learned that a manager's departure isn't a roadblock to a raise. In many ways, it can actually create a unique window of opportunity—if you handle the timing and the handoff correctly. Here is how I navigated it, and the exact steps you can take to get your pay increased during a leadership transition.

## Document Your Impact Before They Pack Their Desk

Your departing manager is your primary witness. Before they log out for the last time, you need to capture their institutional memory regarding your performance. Do not wait until their final week, when they are buried under offboarding paperwork and exit interviews. 

Schedule a dedicated thirty-minute chat specifically to discuss your contributions. Walk into that meeting with a printed or shared list of what you have accomplished over the last six to twelve months, focusing on metrics rather than tasks. 

Instead of saying, "I managed the Q3 marketing campaigns," say, "During Q3, I took over the product launch workflow, which reduced our campaign rollout time by two weeks and brought in 15% more leads than our Q2 baseline." Ask your manager directly: "As you transition out, I want to make sure my contributions are clearly represented to leadership. Do these data points accurately reflect the value you've seen me add?" 

Once they agree, ask them to write a quick note to their boss—your prospective interim manager or department head—highlighting your recent wins, or at least give you permission to quote them in your formal raise proposal.

## Figure Out Who Holds the Purse Strings Now

When a manager leaves, authority usually shifts temporarily. In some companies, human resources freezes all compensation changes until a permanent replacement is found. In others, the departing manager's boss steps in and has full budget discretion. You need to map out the new hierarchy immediately.

Reach out to HR or the departing manager's supervisor and ask for clarity on your reporting structure during the interim period. This isn't just about day-to-day tasks; it is about finding the right decision-maker. 

* Identify who is conducting your manager's exit interview and absorbing their responsibilities.
* Determine if your department has a discretionary budget for retention, or if raises must wait for the annual compensation cycle.
* Find out if the company plans to post the role internally, which might open up a different path to a promotion rather than a standard merit increase.

Knowing who has the final sign-off prevents you from wasting your breath on an interim manager who legally or logistically cannot approve a pay bump.

## Frame Your Raise Around Increased Responsibility

If your manager's departure means their work is trickling down to you, you have immediate leverage. Companies hate losing institutional knowledge, and when a team is short-handed, remaining staff absorb extra pressure. This is your strongest argument for a salary increase.

When you sit down with the decision-maker, connect your request directly to the upcoming operational gaps. Avoid sounding transactional or opportunistic. Instead, frame your proposal around business continuity. 

For example, I told my department head, "With Sarah leaving, I am stepping up to manage the vendor relationships and run the weekly reporting pipeline until we backfill her role. Because this expands my core duties by about 25%, I would like to discuss adjusting my base salary to reflect this expanded scope of work." 

This approach shifts the conversation away from "I want more money" to "I am keeping this department stable while we scale the mountain, and my compensation should match that output."

## Secure It in Writing Before the Dust Settles

If leadership agrees to your raise, do not rely on a verbal handshake, especially if the person agreeing to it is also transitioning out or taking on temporary duties. Interim managers and chaotic transition periods are notorious for dropped handoffs and forgotten promises.

As soon as you receive a verbal "yes," follow up with an email summarizing the discussion. Outline your new responsibilities, the agreed-upon salary adjustment, and the effective date. Ask HR to generate an updated compensation letter for your personnel file. 

Navigating a manager's exit while trying to advance your own career requires patience and precise timing. By locking down your achievements, identifying the true decision-maker, and leveraging your expanded workload, you can turn a moment of company disruption into a profitable career step.
