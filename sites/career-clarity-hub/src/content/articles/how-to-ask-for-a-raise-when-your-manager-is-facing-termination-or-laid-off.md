Finding out your manager is on the chopping block is stressful enough when you are just trying to keep your own job secure. But if you were already gearing up to ask for a raise, the timing feels downright disastrous. When leadership is unstable, your first instinct is usually to freeze, keep your head down, and wait out the storm. 

However, office restructuring does not automatically mean your financial progress has to grind to a halt. In fact, if you handle the situation strategically, a manager's departure can sometimes create a window of opportunity to reset your compensation with whoever steps into the power vacuum. Here is how I navigate asking for a raise when the person in charge of approving it is halfway out the door.

## Pause and Assess the Reality of the Situation

Before you schedule any conversations, you need to figure out exactly what kind of exit your manager is facing. There is a massive difference between a manager who is quietly resigning for a better opportunity elsewhere and one who has just been handed a pink slip due to corporate layoffs or poor performance metrics. 

If your boss is being terminated for performance issues, management is likely already auditing that department closely. Asking for more money right then can make you look out of touch with the team's immediate struggles. On the other hand, if your manager is leaving on good terms or taking a voluntary buyout, they may actually have the goodwill and institutional memory left to advocate for you one last time before they pack up their desk.

## Figure Out Who Holds the Purse Strings Right Now

If your manager is actively walking out the door, they probably no longer have the authority to approve a salary increase, even if they agree you deserve one. Approaching them directly for a raise at this stage is usually a waste of political capital.

Instead, look two steps up the ladder or sideways toward HR. You need to identify who will be stepping in to cover your manager's duties in the interim. 
* Find out if an interim or acting manager is being appointed from within your team.
* Determine if your skip-level manager (your boss's boss) is absorbing your team temporarily.
* Check with HR to see if compensation review cycles are frozen due to the restructuring.

If an interim boss is appointed, give them a couple of weeks to settle in before you bring up money. Ambushing a newly promoted interim manager on their third day with a compensation request will immediately put them on the defensive.

## Leverage Your Extra Responsibilities

When a manager leaves, work rarely leaves with them. Usually, their daily tasks, administrative duties, and fires get dumped onto the remaining team members. This transition period is actually your strongest leverage point for a pay bump.

If you are absorbing extra work to keep the department afloat while leadership is in limbo, document every single task you take on. When you finally sit down with the decision-maker, your argument should not just be about what you used to do; it should center on the expanded scope of your role during the transition. 

For example, instead of saying "I have been here two years and deserve a cost-of-living adjustment," frame it around operational reality: "Since Sarah's departure, I have taken over the vendor management and weekly reporting duties, which has increased my weekly hours by about 20 percent. I want to discuss adjusting my title and compensation to reflect this broader operational scope."

## Pivot to the Skip-Level Manager

If your direct boss is gone and no replacement has been named, your best bet is taking your case directly to your skip-level manager. This can feel intimidating, but senior leaders respect employees who take ownership of their careers, especially during chaotic times.

When you request this meeting, keep the focus entirely on business continuity. Do not complain about the recent departure or gossip about why your manager left. Walk into the meeting with a clear, one-page document outlining your recent accomplishments, the extra responsibilities you are currently handling, and market data supporting your salary request. Senior leaders appreciate brevity and facts, particularly when they are stretched thin covering for vacant positions.

## Know When to Hit the Pause Button

Sometimes, the smartest move is to wait. If the company is undergoing mass layoffs, restructuring entire departments, or freezing all spending, pushing for a raise can accidentally paint a target on your back. 

If you suspect your own job security is shaky because of the broader company climate, prioritize stabilizing your position and proving your worth to the incoming leadership team first. Once the dust settles and the new organizational chart is set in stone, you can schedule a formal performance and compensation review with your permanent supervisor.
