Asking for more money right after your company cuts staff feels awkward. When HR announces layoffs or a hiring freeze, the standard advice tells you to keep your head down and be grateful you still have a job. 

I’ve been in that exact spot. Two members of my four-person marketing team were laid off last year, and suddenly I was absorbing their daily campaign schedules, client emails, and weekly reporting. I knew the department budget was tight, but I also knew I was doing the work of two extra people for my old salary. 

If your team's headcount was just reduced and your workload doubled, you have a strong, numbers-backed case for a compensation review. Here is how I approached the conversation, and how you can do it too.

## Separate the Layoff from Your Value

The biggest mistake people make in this situation is framing the raise request around personal financial stress or complaining about being overworked. Bosses react poorly to complaints framed as "I am overwhelmed." 

Instead, frame the conversation around scope and market value. A layoff changes your job description. If you absorbed specific responsibilities from a departed colleague, you are no longer performing the job you were originally hired to do. 

Look at your weekly calendar and list the exact tasks you inherited. If you used to write two blog posts a week and now you also manage paid ad campaigns and answer customer support tickets, you have fundamentally changed your role. You are asking to be paid for the job you are doing right now, not the job you started with.

## Build a Before-and-After Document

Managers and directors are often buried in their own fires, especially right after a team reduction. They might genuinely lose track of just how much operational weight has landed on your desk. 

Before you schedule a meeting, put together a simple one-page document. Do not bring a novel; bring a clear breakdown:

*   **Your Previous Scope:** 3-4 bullet points outlining your core responsibilities prior to the headcount reduction.
*   **Your Current Scope:** The added duties you have taken on since the layoffs, including the specific software, processes, or clients you now manage.
*   **Business Impact:** How taking on these duties protects company revenue or prevents project delays. For example: "By absorbing Sarah's client accounts, we retained three key enterprise contracts worth $150,000 annually."

This document shifts the meeting from an emotional negotiation to an objective review of your updated job description.

## Timing It Right in the Wake of Cuts

You should not walk into your manager's office the day after layoff notices go out. That looks tone-deaf and creates friction. 

Wait roughly two to four weeks. Let the initial dust settle, allow the team to establish a temporary routine, and ensure you have successfully executed at least one major deliverable or project in your expanded capacity. This proves you can actually handle the new workload before you ask to be compensated for it.

When you request the meeting with your manager, keep the calendar invite neutral. Label it "Compensation and Role Review" or "Career Development Discussion." This gives your boss a heads-up so they aren't ambushed, allowing them time to consult with HR or look at department financials beforehand.

## The Script: What to Actually Say

When you sit down for the conversation, skip the preamble about how stressed you are. Start with gratitude for the stability of your role, then pivot immediately to the operational reality. 

I used a script very close to this: 

"Thanks for meeting with me. Over the last month since the restructuring, I’ve taken ownership of [Project A] and [Task B] to keep our department running smoothly. Looking at my current day-to-day responsibilities, my role has expanded significantly to cover the gaps left by the recent headcount reduction. I’d like to discuss adjusting my compensation to reflect this broader scope of work."

Then, stop talking. Let your manager respond. They will likely say one of two things: they agree and need to check with finance, or they will point to budget constraints.

## Handling the "No Budget" Response

If your manager tells you that company finances are frozen due to the layoffs, do not accept a dead end immediately. Companies almost always have discretionary funds for retention, especially when losing another person—you—would cause catastrophic project failure.

If cash increases are genuinely impossible right now, negotiate for alternative value. You can ask for a signed agreement to revisit your salary in six months with a guaranteed percentage increase if revenue targets are met. You can also ask for extra paid time off, a one-time retention bonus, or a clearly defined promotion timeline once the hiring freeze thaws. 

Preparing for this conversation takes courage, especially when the economic climate feels unstable. But taking on extra work without extra pay sets a dangerous precedent for your career. Reviewing your options and mapping out your next steps will help you approach this critical discussion with absolute confidence.
