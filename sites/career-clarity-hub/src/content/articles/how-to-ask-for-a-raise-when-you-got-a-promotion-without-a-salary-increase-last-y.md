Getting a promotion feels great until you check your bank account and realize your pay stayed exactly the same. Last year, I went through this exact situation. I was handed a new title, a heftier workload, and direct reports, but my boss told me the company budget was frozen and my salary bump would have to wait. 

If you are sitting in that same boat right now, waiting for the one-year mark to finally bring up your compensation, you are probably feeling a mix of anxiety and frustration. You already proved you can handle the job, but waiting an entire year to get paid for it is exhausting. Here is the exact playbook I used to successfully turn that unpaid promotion into the salary I deserved.

## Build the paper trail of your impact

When you ask for a raise after a delayed-pay promotion, memory is your enemy. Bosses often remember the awkward transition period when you were learning the ropes, rather than the high-performing machine you have become nine months later. 

You need to gather concrete evidence of what your new role actually involves. Pull up the job description you were given when you got promoted. Next to each bullet point, write down a specific project, metric, or task you successfully handled over the past year. 

For instance, if your title changed from Marketing Specialist to Marketing Manager, do not just say "I managed campaigns." Instead, write down: "I managed a $50,000 quarterly ad spend, reduced our cost-per-lead by 14 percent, and onboarded two junior coordinators." This shifts the conversation from how you feel to the financial and operational reality of what you are delivering to the business.

## Time the conversation around business cycles

Timing can make or break your request. Do not ambush your manager during their busiest week of the quarter or right after the company announced a round of layoffs. Instead, look at your company's financial calendar.

The best time to bring this up is usually four to six weeks before annual performance reviews or budget planning cycles begin. This gives your manager time to advocate for you with finance and human resources before the final payroll numbers are locked in. If your company does not have a strict annual cycle, aim for a time when you have just wrapped up a major win—like successfully launching a product, retaining a massive client, or guiding the team through a busy season. 

## Schedule a dedicated meeting

Never spring a conversation about money on your boss at the end of a regular one-on-one status update. You need dedicated time where compensation is the primary agenda item. 

Send a calendar invite with a clear, neutral subject line like "Career and Compensation Discussion." When your manager inevitably asks what the meeting is about in advance, keep it straightforward. You can say: "I want to look back at my first year in the [New Title] role, review my impact on the team, and discuss aligning my compensation with these new responsibilities." 

This removes the element of surprise and gives your boss time to mentally prepare rather than getting defensive on the spot.

## Deliver your pitch with a market-based anchor

When you sit down for the meeting, keep your tone collaborative rather than adversarial. Frame the conversation around the unfinished business of last year's promotion. 

You can use a script structure like this:
*   "Thank you for meeting with me. Reflecting on this past year as [New Title], I am really proud of what our team has accomplished, especially [mention one major win]."
*   "When I stepped into this role last year, we agreed to postpone the salary adjustment due to the budget freeze. Now that I have a full year of performance data in this position, I want to discuss bringing my compensation up to market rate for this title."
*   "Based on my research for this role in our geographic area and industry, the standard range is between X and Y. Given my performance, I am asking for an adjustment to [Specific Number]."

Notice how this approach avoids complaining. It treats the delayed raise as a temporary logistics issue from last year that simply needs to be corrected now.

## Know your walk-away point

Sometimes, despite a glowing performance review, the answer is still no or not right now. If your manager says the budget still does not allow for a raise, you need to be prepared with a follow-up question. 

Ask specifically what milestones or timeline need to be met for a salary review to happen, and get that commitment in writing via a follow-up email. However, if you get continuous excuses with no clear path forward, remember that your newly minted title makes you a very attractive candidate on the external job market. Taking your updated resume and your year of senior-level experience elsewhere might be the fastest way to get the pay you deserve.

As you plan your next moves and prepare to sit down with your manager, having a structured framework for your numbers and talking points will keep you grounded and confident.
