When the company next door—or even just down the hall—gets dismantled, the ripple effects hit fast. Suddenly, your manager drops the news that your department is absorbing the remaining projects, systems, and clients of a disbanded team. For most of us, the immediate emotional reaction is a mix of dread and exhaustion. You are already at capacity, and now you are inheriting a whole new mountain of work. 

If you are staring down a heavier workload without any clear sign of extra pay, you might be wondering if it is the right time to ask for a raise. The short answer is yes. In fact, absorbing another team gives you some of the strongest leverage you will ever have in your career, provided you frame the conversation around measurable business value rather than just feeling overworked. 

Here is a practical, step-by-step way to navigate this tricky conversation and turn a departmental merger into a financial upgrade.

## Wait for the Dust to Settle (Just a Little Bit)

The day the layoff or restructuring announcement happens is not the day to walk into your boss's office with a compensation request. In the immediate aftermath, leadership is often dealing with PR fallout, HR paperwork, and panicked emails. 

Give it about two to four weeks. During this window, focus on getting your bearings. Figure out what systems the incoming team used, which projects are actually active, and where the major bottlenecks are. When you finally schedule the meeting, you want to be able to speak specifics about the exact volume of work you are now handling, rather than just complaining that things are chaotic.

## Audit Your New Job Description

When a team dissolves, work rarely transfers in a neat, organized package. You usually inherit a messy bundle of ongoing tasks, ad-hoc requests, and legacy problems. Before you talk money, write down your original job description on the left side of a page, and your current daily reality on the right. 

Be granular. For example, don’t just write "handling marketing operations." Write down:
*   Onboarded 14 new enterprise client accounts previously managed by the disbanded West Coast team.
*   Took over administration of the Salesforce database migration project.
*   Assumed responsibility for weekly cross-functional reporting that used to be handled by two departed senior associates.

This document becomes your shield and your sword. It proves objectively that your role has expanded beyond your current pay grade.

## Shift Your Pitch From "Workload" to "Value"

The biggest mistake people make when asking for a raise after a merger is leading with how tired or stressed they are. While your manager should care about your well-being, the company budget is ultimately tied to business outcomes. 

Instead of saying, "I have too much on my plate," frame it around risk mitigation and increased output. You are protecting the company's revenue by keeping these inherited clients happy, or you are saving the company thousands of dollars in hiring costs by absorbing these duties internally instead of opening new positions. 

Position your request not as a demand for more money for doing the same job, but as an adjustment for a promotion-level expansion of duties.

## Time the Request Around Your Wins

Timing can make or break this conversation. Do not ask for a raise during the exact week your department is missing deadlines due to the transition. 

Wait for a small win. Maybe you successfully closed the first major project handed over by the disbanded team, or you ironed out a workflow conflict between the two merging groups. Once you have a tangible success under your belt, set up a one-on-one meeting with your manager specifically dedicated to role alignment and career growth. 

## What to Do If the Answer Is "Not Right Now"

Budgets can be tight, especially in companies that are actively restructuring or laying off staff. If your manager agrees that you are doing more work but claims there is simply no money in the budget for a raise right now, do not just accept a dead end. 

Instead, negotiate for alternative compensation or career capital:
*   **A structured timeline:** Ask for a written agreement to revisit your salary in six months, tied to specific performance milestones.
*   **One-time bonuses:** If permanent budget increases are frozen, ask for a retention or transition bonus for keeping the merged operations afloat.
*   **Title upgrade:** Sometimes a title change (like moving from Specialist to Senior Specialist) costs the company zero dollars today, but makes you much more marketable if things don't improve.

Navigating a corporate merger or department absorption is rarely easy, but it presents a rare inflection point where your value to the company spikes overnight. By tracking your new responsibilities, focusing your pitch on business impact, and picking the right moment to speak up, you can turn organizational chaos into a well-deserved pay bump.
