Asking for a raise is already stressful enough when you can walk down the hall and knock on your manager's office door. When your boss lives in a completely different country and operates in a time zone six hours ahead of yours, the logistics alone can feel paralyzing. You might worry about your message getting lost in a Slack thread, or that out-of-sight-out-of-mind dynamics will work against you. 

I’ve navigated this exact situation myself. Working from a Chicago apartment while my direct supervisor managed the team from a laptop in London taught me that remote compensation requests require a completely different playbook. You cannot rely on casual watercooler chats or catching your boss in a good mood after a successful presentation. You have to be deliberate, highly organized, and proactive about visibility.

Here is the exact framework I used to secure a 12% pay bump across an ocean, and how you can do the same.

## Map the Time Zone and Communication Gap

The biggest mistake people make when communicating with an international manager is treating them like a local colleague who reads messages all day. If your boss logs off at noon your time, firing off a complex compensation proposal at 3:00 PM means it sits unread at the bottom of their queue until the next morning. 

Before you even draft your request, map out your overlapping hours. Aim for a synchronous video chat during your manager's morning, which is usually your early morning or late afternoon depending on the longitude. Never try to tackle a raise conversation asynchronously over email or a quick chat message. You need face-to-face time, even if it is through a webcam, to read facial expressions and nuances that text simply cannot convey.

## Build Your "Invisible Work" Dossier

When your manager can't physically see you logging long hours or stepping up in meetings, your daily output can easily become invisible. International managers rely heavily on written metrics and documented project wins because they miss the ambient office chatter. 

A few weeks before you schedule the conversation, assemble a concrete impact log. Go through your past six months of work and pull out specific examples of how you made money, saved time, or solved critical problems. 

Instead of saying, "I've been working really hard," your preparation should sound like this:
*   Redesigned the client onboarding workflow, which reduced setup time by 20% across our regional accounts.
*   Stepped in to manage the Berlin product launch while the regional lead was on parental leave, keeping the timeline on track.
*   Assumed responsibility for training our three newest junior hires without any drop in my primary output.

Put these points into a clean, one-page document. Send it to your manager 24 hours before your scheduled video call so they have time to review the tangible data before you talk numbers.

## Factor in Local Market Realities and Currency

One hurdle of having a cross-border manager is that they might not understand the local economic landscape where you sit. A manager based in a lower-cost country or region might look at your current salary and think it is generous, completely missing the reality of inflation or rising cost of living in your specific US city.

Do your homework on regional salary bands. Look at aggregator sites, industry salary reports, and recent job postings in your city for your exact title. When you make your case, frame it around your value and local market rates. You might say: "Based on current market data for senior account managers in the Midwest, and given my expanded scope over the last year taking on international projects, I am looking to adjust my compensation to reflect these responsibilities."

## Schedule the Conversation Intentionally

Never blindside an international manager with a compensation request at the end of a routine weekly sync. If they have a hard stop in ten minutes for a board meeting in another time zone, your request will feel rushed and stressful.

Instead, send a calendar invite with a clear, neutral subject line like "Career Development and Compensation Review." Give them at least a week's notice so they can mentally prepare and, if necessary, consult with HR or finance about regional budget approvals. International companies often have complex payroll structures, and your manager might need time to check what internal approvals are required to raise a salary across borders.

## Follow Up and Document Everything

Once you have the meeting, treat the aftermath with the same cross-border discipline you used to set it up. International HR processes can move slowly, and requests can easily get stuck in a bureaucratic loop between your local payroll provider and your manager's home office.

Within 24 hours of your meeting, send a follow-up email summarizing what was discussed, the specific number or range you agreed upon, and the expected timeline for a decision. This creates a paper trail and holds your manager accountable, ensuring your request doesn't evaporate across the Atlantic. 

Navigating your career remotely takes extra effort, but taking control of the process shows leadership that transcends geography.
