Have you ever taken a promotion and immediately felt like you were handed a house fire instead of an office? 

A few years ago, I watched a colleague step into a newly created VP role at a mid-sized tech firm. The company had just lost its largest client, faced a federal compliance audit, and its software platform was actively bleeding users. Management threw a celebratory pizza party, handed her the keys, and told her she was the savior they needed. Eighteen months later, after absorbing the blame for a sinking ship she didn’t build, she was pushed out. 

She wasn't just handed a hard job. She was dropped onto the glass cliff. 

Coined in the early 2000s, the "glass cliff" describes a phenomenon where people—historically women and minorities, though it can happen to anyone—are appointed to leadership positions during times of crisis or extreme company downturn. Because the odds of failure are astronomically high, the risk of a reputational crash is severe. 

If you suspect you were brought in to take the fall for a disaster you didn’t create, look for these five warning signs.

## 1. The Scope Changed Dramatically After You Signed
In your interview, you were told you were leading a standard turnaround. Maybe you were hired to manage a marketing team or oversee supply chain operations. But the day you badge in, the goalposts shift. 

Suddenly, your job isn't just fixing the department; it's absorbing two failing teams, cutting a bloated budget by forty percent, and navigating a union dispute, all without additional resources. If the job description you agreed to bears no resemblance to the daily reality of your calendar, you are looking at a manufactured crisis.

## 2. You Have Zero Runway and Zero Authority
A legitimate turnaround requires time, data access, and the executive backing to make unpopular changes. A glass cliff setup does the opposite. 

You are handed an impossible deadline—such as turning around a five-year negative revenue trend in two quarters—while upper management blocks every structural change you propose. If you are held fully accountable for the metrics, but stripped of the budget, hiring power, or organizational authority needed to move them, you are being used as a human shield for executive failure.

## 3. Your Predecessor Fled a Sinking Ship
Pay close attention to *why* the last person in your seat left. If they retired quietly after a long tenure, that's normal turnover. But if your predecessor abruptly resigned on a Friday, was ousted via an emergency board meeting, or quietly transferred to a low-impact backwater division three weeks before you arrived, dig deeper. 

Ask former peers what the real climate was like. If the previous leader saw the writing on the wall and ran, you should probably check the exit routes yourself.

## 4. The Executive Team Suddenly Goes Quiet
When things were going well, your boss and the C-suite were deeply involved. They wanted weekly updates, brainstormed with you, and chimed in on Slack. 

The moment you inherit the troubled project, however, the silence is deafening. Your calendar invites to strategic planning sessions are ignored, emails about risk mitigation go unanswered, and executive leadership distances themselves from your decisions. This is intentional. They are creating deniability so that when the project craters, they can point the finger directly at your desk.

## 5. You Feel an Immediate, Unnatural Need to Defend Your Competence
Good jobs challenge you; setups make you feel like you are losing your mind. If you find yourself spending seventy percent of your workweek drafting defensive memos, creating paper trails to prove you warned management about impending disaster, and second-guessing your baseline professional judgment, your nervous system is sounding an alarm. 

When your primary energy goes toward self-preservation rather than actual problem-solving, the environment is toxic to your career health.

## How to Protect Your Career and Walk Away
Recognizing a glass cliff is terrifying, but it also gives you immense power. Once you realize the deck is stacked against you, you can stop wasting energy trying to win a rigged game. 

Start by documenting everything in writing. Send recap emails after verbal conversations with superiors to confirm your lack of resources or blocked proposals. At the same time, quietly update your resume, dust off your professional network, and start taking calls from recruiters who value your actual skill set. 

You do not owe your professional reputation to a sinking company that set you up as collateral damage. Walking away isn't quitting; it's refusing to be the scapegoat for someone else's mess. 

If you are currently wrestling with a toxic promotion, weighing your exit strategy, or trying to decide if you should stick it out or jump ship, evaluating your next career move requires a clear-eyed look at your options.
