Navigating a compensation conversation is tricky under normal circumstances. Add in a management restructure where your direct boss pivots to an outside contractor, and the dynamic gets completely muddy. I lived through this exact scenario last year: my manager of two years suddenly became a 1099 vendor providing fractional consulting to our mid-sized marketing firm. 

Suddenly, I had no direct internal advocate, a hazy reporting line, and a burning need to update my salary to match my expanding duties. If you are sitting in this same awkward transitional phase, wondering who actually approves a pay bump when your boss no longer sits on the internal org chart, here is the exact playbook I used to secure a 12% raise.

## Map the New Power Structure Immediately

When a manager transitions to a contractor role, companies usually handle the reporting lines in one of two ways. Either you temporarily report to a higher-up (like a director or VP) who is spread too thin, or you fall into a strange interim limbo where day-to-day tasks are handled by the ex-boss, but administrative authority belongs to someone else entirely.

Before you ask for a penny, you need to find out who holds the purse strings. Look at your HR portal or ask HR directly: "With [Former Manager's Name] moving to a vendor agreement, who is officially signing off on my performance reviews, headcount budget, and compensation changes?" 

Do not ask your former manager for a raise. Even if they still direct your daily workload, they are legally an outside vendor now. They cannot authorize internal payroll increases. You need to identify your new internal decision-maker—usually the person your former boss now reports to in their contractor capacity.

## Separate Your Ex-Boss From Your Value Pitch

One major trap in this scenario is leaning too heavily on your old manager to fight for your raise. While your ex-boss might love you and want to help, they now lack internal political capital. Their incentive structure has changed; they are focused on billing hours and delivering specific project milestones, not fighting human resources for internal salary bands.

Instead, build a standalone case centered entirely on your independent value. When I met with my department's VP (who inherited my oversight), I didn't mention my old boss at all during the pitch. Instead, I highlighted three specific things:
* The operational gaps left behind by the restructure that I personally absorbed.
* Quantifiable projects I had successfully driven to completion without hands-on supervision.
* Current market rate data for my specific title and expanded responsibilities in our geographic region.

By framing your raise around what the company needs right now—stability and independent execution—you make it easy for the new decision-maker to say yes.

## Time the Request Around the Budgetary Reset

Corporate restructures usually trigger a re-allocation of funds. When my company converted my manager to a contractor, they actually freed up a chunk of internal budget because they weren't paying his full-time salary, benefits, payroll taxes, and PTO anymore. 

Pay attention to timing. Do not ask for a raise during the chaotic first two weeks of the transition while everyone is figuring out new logins and workflows. Wait about 30 to 45 days. By then, the leadership team will feel the operational friction of the change and will deeply appreciate that you kept things running smoothly. This window—right after the dust settles but before the next fiscal quarter locks in—is the sweet spot for pitching a compensation review.

## Schedule a Direct Conversation With Your Interim Leader

Set up a 30-minute one-on-one meeting with your new internal point of contact. Keep the calendar invite simple, such as "Role and Project Alignment." 

During the meeting, run through a structured agenda. Start by reaffirming your commitment to the team during the transition. Then, pivot directly to your expanded scope. Say something practical like: "Over the last month operating under the new contractor structure, I’ve officially taken on [Task A] and [Task B], which previously fell outside my job description. Given that my day-to-day responsibilities have grown and I am operating with a high degree of autonomy, I would like to review my base compensation to align with these current duties."

Be prepared for them to ask for time to consult HR or look at the department budget. That is a normal response, not an outright rejection. Follow up in writing 48 hours later with a brief summary of your talking points and your market data attached for their reference.

Handling a compensation review during a corporate restructure requires patience and a clear understanding of who actually controls the budget. If you take charge of your own narrative and present yourself as a stable, self-sufficient asset during a time of flux, you put yourself in a strong position to win financial recognition.
