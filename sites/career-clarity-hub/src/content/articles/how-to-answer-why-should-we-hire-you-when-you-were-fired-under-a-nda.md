Getting fired under a nondisclosure agreement is a uniquely stressful professional hurdle. When you are legally barred from explaining the exact circumstances of your departure, answering "Why should we hire you?" feels like walking through a minefield. You want to pitch your value, but you are terrified of accidentally violating a legal contract or raising red flags about your past. 

I’ve been in that exact seat, staring across a desk at a hiring manager while biting my tongue about a messy severance and an NDA. What I learned is that you do not need to overshare to win the job. You just need to shift the conversation away from the exit and lock it firmly onto your execution.

Here is how to answer that dreaded question honestly, legally, and persuasively.

## Understand the Boundaries of Your NDA

Before you even step into an interview room, you need to read your separation agreement and NDA with a highlighter. Most people panic and assume an NDA means they cannot talk about the company at all. Usually, that is not true. 

NDAs typically protect proprietary data, trade secrets, client lists, and specific internal disputes. They rarely prevent you from stating that you worked somewhere, outlining your general job duties, or sharing high-level achievements. 

If you are bound by a strict separation agreement that dictates a neutral reference—meaning your old employer will only confirm your dates of employment and title—your story needs to match theirs. Never badmouth the previous employer. Frame the departure neutrally, such as, "My tenure there ended as part of a restructuring," or "Leadership and I parted ways over differing strategic visions, and we signed an agreement to keep the details private." 

## Pivot Immediately to Value

The hiring manager’s question—"Why should we hire you?"—is not actually about your past. It is about their future. They want to know what problems you are going to solve for them starting on day one. 

When you were fired, your brain naturally fixes on the injustice or the anxiety of the exit. The interviewer does not care about that drama. They care if you can write code, manage a budget, or close enterprise sales. Use the bridge technique: acknowledge the prompt briefly, then pivot hard to your core competencies.

*   Acknowledge the boundary: "While I'm bound by a confidentiality agreement regarding the specifics of my departure from my last role..."
*   Pivot to the present: "...what I can talk about is the exact skill set I brought to that role, and how I plan to apply it here."
*   Deliver the pitch: Focus on a specific metric, project, or workflow you mastered that directly benefits the company sitting across from you.

## Prepare a Neutral, Rehearsed Exit Statement

You need a one-sentence explanation for your departure that is so boring and non-dramatic that the interviewer immediately loses interest in digging deeper. Interviewers ask follow-up questions when they sense evasion or panic. If you deliver your neutral statement with absolute calm and confidence, they will usually nod and move on.

For example, practice saying: "My time at Company X concluded under a mutual separation agreement, and out of respect for those terms, I focus my energy on the work I delivered there." 

Deliver it once, look them in the eye, and transition immediately into asking a question about their current team goals. Confident brevity shuts down awkward pauses and signals professionalism.

## Spotlight Your Transferable Wins

Since you cannot lean heavily on the narrative of your last job, you need to mine your broader career history for undeniable proof of your capabilities. Pull out specific examples from earlier in your career, freelance projects, or side ventures that prove your competence. 

Bring concrete artifacts if the industry allows—like a portfolio, a redacted case study, or a dashboard showing a 20% increase in lead generation. When you can physically or digitally point to results, the mystery of your last departure fades into the background. Hiring managers hire proof, not pleasantries.

## Practice Out Loud Until It Feels Natural

The biggest mistake candidates make is scripting their answer in their head and trying to wing it out loud. The first time you say your NDA pivot out loud, it will probably sound stiff and paranoid. 

Sit down with a friend or record yourself on your phone. Practice moving from the uncomfortable topic of your departure straight into your biggest professional strengths without missing a beat. The goal is to make your answer to "Why should we hire you?" sound entirely centered on your enthusiasm for this new role, leaving the ghost of your old job firmly in the past.

Navigating a job search after a termination under an NDA takes practice, but it is entirely possible to land a great role without violating legal bounds or losing your confidence.
