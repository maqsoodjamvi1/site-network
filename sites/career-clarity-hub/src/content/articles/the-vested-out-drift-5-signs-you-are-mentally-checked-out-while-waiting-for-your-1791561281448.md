You have logged into your internal equity portal for the fourth time today, not to check your net worth, but to stare at the exact date of your next vest. If your calendar says you have three months, six months, or a year until a major block of Restricted Stock Units (RSUs) hits your account, you might be experiencing what I call the vested-out drift. 

This is the strange professional limbo where your body is at your desk, but your mind cleared out weeks ago. You are officially pacing yourself, hoarding your remaining energy, and treating your current job like a layover at an airport terminal. 

Staying until your vest date makes financial sense, but surviving the psychological drag requires recognizing what is happening. Here are five signs you are mentally checked out while waiting out your equity, and how to manage the drift without torpedoing your reputation on the way out.

## 1. You Have Mastered the Art of Strategic Minimalism
When you care about a job, you lean into ambiguity. You raise your hand for messy, undefined projects because you want to build things and grow. When you are mentally vested-out, you do the exact opposite. 

You execute your core responsibilities—just enough to avoid a performance improvement plan—and nothing more. If someone drops an open-ended problem in Slack, you let someone else grab it. You have optimized your workflow down to the bare minimum, clearing your calendar of any meeting that does not directly require your signature or attendance. 

While pacing yourself is a rational survival tactic, the danger is crossing the line into active avoidance. When you start dodging standard responsibilities that take minimal effort just out of spite or boredom, you risk drawing unwanted attention from your manager.

## 2. Your Internal Roadmap Replaces the Company Roadmap
Normally, your short-term goals align with the company's quarterly objectives. During the RSU waiting period, your internal roadmap has only one milestone: the vesting date. 

Every work week is mentally converted into a countdown metric. You measure time not in sprints, product launches, or fiscal quarters, but in pay periods and vest tranches. When a stakeholder asks about a project slated for next year, your immediate internal reaction is mild panic because you mentally checked out of next year months ago. 

This tunnel vision makes current tasks feel excruciatingly tedious. Because you know you will not be around to see the long-term results of your labor, writing code, planning campaigns, or managing client accounts starts to feel like doing homework for a class you have already dropped.

## 3. Your Tolerance for Corporate Friction Drops to Zero
In a normal operating state, you might roll your eyes at a bureaucratic process, schedule a sync to fix it, and move on. But when you are mentally checked out, minor company annoyances feel like personal insults.

A long-winded email from leadership, a clunky internal software migration, or a pointless status meeting will ruin your entire morning. Because you have already emotionally detached from the culture, you no longer have the patience to play the corporate game. Your internal filter frays. You find yourself biting your tongue in meetings to avoid saying what you actually think about top-down decisions that make no operational sense. 

## 4. You Quietly Begin Your Next-Chapter Research
You aren't just waiting; you are preparing. Your browser history tells the real story: salary benchmarking sites, passive job boards, portfolio refreshes, and articles about career transitions. 

You might not have sent out a single resume yet, but your mental energy has shifted entirely to what comes next. You spend lunch breaks sketching out ideas for your next move, updating your LinkedIn profile with subtle tweaks, or reaching out to former colleagues under the guise of "catching up." 

This is actually a healthy impulse, provided you keep it strictly offline and off-network. The risk is letting your preparation bleed into company time, or accidentally signaling your impending departure to a coworker who might mention it to your manager before your shares clear.

## 5. You Have Adopted the Observer Mindset
Perhaps the clearest sign of the vested-out drift is a shift in how you experience office conflict. When bad news drops—layoff rumors, reorganization announcements, or strategy pivots—your colleagues panic or scramble to adapt. You, on the other hand, feel an eerie sense of detachment.

You watch company drama unfold with the detached curiosity of someone watching a movie on a flight. If a reorg happens, your first thought is no longer "How will this affect my team?" but rather "Will this accelerate or delay my vest date?" You have emotionally resigned, leaving only your physical shell behind to collect the payout.

***

Navigating the final stretch of an RSU vesting schedule without burning bridges requires balancing your financial goals with your daily sanity. As you map out your timeline for departure, consider what steps you need to take next to prepare for your eventual exit cleanly and professionally.
