Asking for a raise is already one of the most stressful parts of a job. Throw a temporary manager into the mix—someone who might be filling in for three months while on secondment or parental leave—and the process feels almost impossible. Your first instinct is likely to hit pause and wait out the interim period. 

Waiting usually costs you money. Interim leaders often stay longer than planned, and even if they don't, your performance reviews and compensation cycles keep moving. When an interim boss sits in the chair, you need a different playbook. Here is how I approached asking for a pay bump under a temporary supervisor, and how you can do it too.

## Understand What Your Interim Manager Can (and Cannot) Do

Before you set up a meeting, you need to understand the structural limits of an interim role. A stand-in manager usually falls into one of two camps: an internal peer who stepped up temporarily, or an external consultant brought in to keep the lights on. 

Neither typically has the authority to issue a permanent 15% salary bump on a whim. Their mandate is usually operational continuity, not long-term structural changes to the team's compensation. 

However, they *do* have influence. An interim manager writes the notes that go into your permanent file, validates your day-to-day output, and holds the ear of upper leadership or HR. If you frame your request not as "give me more money" but as "help me document my value so leadership can adjust my compensation," an interim boss can become your strongest advocate.

## Timing It Right

Do not bring up money during your interim manager's first two weeks. They are still figuring out where the virtual bathroom is, and your compensation is the last thing on their radar. Conversely, do not wait until their final week, either, when they are mentally checked out and handing back the keys.

The sweet spot is right in the middle of their tenure, typically between week six and week twelve. By this point, they have seen your work quality firsthand, realized how much you actually handle, and developed enough organizational capital to speak on your behalf to higher-ups.

## Build a Standalone Evidence Packet

When your permanent boss is gone, corporate memory takes a hit. Your interim manager does not know what you delivered twelve months ago, what major project you rescued in Q1, or how your scope expanded six months before they arrived. 

You cannot rely on them "just knowing" what you do. You have to hand them a tightly organized packet of facts. Keep it to one page and include:

*   **Current baseline vs. actual output:** A bulleted list of tasks you currently handle that sit outside your original job description.
*   **Measurable impact:** Concrete metrics (revenue generated, hours saved, or processes streamlined) rather than vague adjectives like "hardworking" or "dedicated."
*   **Market data:** Two or three salary data points from sites like Glassdoor or Salary.com for your exact title in your specific US metro area.

Handing someone a neatly packaged argument makes it easy for them to say yes, or at least easy for them to forward your email directly to HR with a note saying, "This checks out."

## Have the Conversation

Keep the conversation collaborative rather than adversarial. Frame the interim manager as a partner in getting your compensation aligned with your actual responsibilities. 

Sit down for your one-on-one and say something concrete: "I know you're in this role temporarily, but my annual compensation review is coming up, and my responsibilities have grown significantly over the past year. I want to make sure I have your support in bringing a market adjustment request to HR. Here is a summary of what I've taken on."

Notice the phrasing. You aren't asking *them* to fund the raise out of their temporary budget. You are asking for their endorsement of the facts.

## Secure a Paper Trail for the Permanent Handover

Even if your interim manager agrees that you deserve a raise, they might not have the sign-off power before their term ends. If that happens, you need a paper trail before they pack up their desk.

Ask your interim boss to send a quick email summarizing your conversation and their endorsement, or ask them to include their notes in your official performance file. When the permanent manager finally arrives, you can forward that thread and say, "Before [Interim Name] left, we reviewed my current scope and agreed that a compensation review was warranted."
