When your company decides to shutter another department and dump its remaining workload onto your team, your first instinct is probably survival. You are suddenly expected to triage a flood of new projects, learn unfamiliar software, and answer frantic emails from stakeholders you have never worked with before—all while keeping your original job running smoothly. 

If you are absorbing a disbanded team, your job description just fundamentally changed. You are no longer doing the job you were hired for. You are doing that job, plus managing a massive operational transition. This is one of the clearest, most justifiable moments in a career to ask for a raise, but timing and framing matter entirely. 

Here is how I approach asking for a pay bump when corporate restructuring lands extra work on my desk.

## Document the Scope Creep Immediately

Managers have notoriously short memories when it comes to extra work, especially during chaotic restructuring. By week three of the merger, your new baseline will simply be "the way things are." 

Before you even request a meeting with your boss, you need hard evidence of how your day-to-day has shifted. Do not just say, "I am busier." Bring a concrete breakdown to the table:

*   **List the absorbed tasks:** Write down the specific projects, clients, or recurring reports that belonged to the disbanded team and now live on your plate.
*   **Track the time sink:** Keep a rough log for one week showing how many hours you spend managing the new workflows versus your core responsibilities.
*   **Note the skill gap:** Highlight any specialized training, new software, or complex processes you had to learn on the fly to keep the absorbed work from failing.

When you sit down to talk numbers, you want to show that you aren't just complaining about a busy week. You are presenting a quantifiable increase in operational output and responsibility.

## Separate "Helping Out" From "A Permanent Role Expansion"

When a team dissolves, leadership will often frame the absorbed work as a temporary fix. They might say, "We just need everyone to pitch in while we figure out the long-term strategy." 

If you accept this framing, you will be doing two jobs for the price of one indefinitely, with no compensation adjustment. You need to pin down a timeline. 

In my experience, temporary help lasts about thirty days. Anything beyond that is a structural change to your position. When you schedule your compensation conversation, frame it around this reality. Acknowledge that you stepped up to keep operations stable during the transition, but point out that the volume of work indicates a permanent absorption rather than a short-term favor. If the work is here to stay, your compensation needs to match the new scope of the role.

## Frame the Request Around Value and Market Rate

Never base a raise request on personal financial need, like rising rent or inflation. Management does not factor your personal expenses into payroll budgets. Instead, tie your request directly to the financial value you are saving the company.

By absorbing a disbanded team instead of backfilling those roles, your company is saving thousands of dollars a month in salary, benefits, and recruiting costs. You are essentially acting as a lean, two-person department wrapped into one employee. 

When you make your pitch, use language like this: "Given that our team has absorbed the entire workload of the former analytics division, my daily responsibilities now mirror a senior-level position. Based on current market rates for managing both portfolios, I would like to discuss adjusting my salary to reflect this expanded scope." 

## Choose the Right Moment in the Chaos

Timing a raise request during a corporate shake-up requires reading the room. If the company is actively laying people off to avoid bankruptcy, asking for more money might put a target on your back. However, if the restructuring was driven by department consolidation, shifting priorities, or strategic growth, budgets are often flexible—especially for employees holding the operation together.

Aim for a window where the dust has settled slightly. This is usually about four to six weeks after the other team has officially disbanded. By this point, your boss has likely realized how catastrophic it would be if *you* walked out the door, too. 

Schedule a dedicated one-on-one meeting specifically for this discussion. Do not ambush your manager at the end of a status update or drop the question in a Slack message. Put it on the calendar with a clear subject line so they have time to mentally prepare and check internal compensation bands.

Navigating a workplace merger or team shutdown is stressful, but it also creates rare leverage for your career. If you take the time to map out your new responsibilities and present them clearly to leadership, you can turn a chaotic company restructuring into a well-earned pay raise.
