A few years ago, I looked at my resume and realized my career had quietly stalled out. I was working forty to fifty hours a week, handling a mountain of internal logistics, fixing broken spreadsheets, and putting out daily fires for my team. On paper, I was busy. But when I sat down to update my LinkedIn profile, I stared at a blank page. None of the actual work I spent my days doing translated into skills that a hiring manager outside my company would care about. 

I call this the phantom-duty trap. It happens when your job morphs over time into a collection of administrative tasks, operational glue-work, and internal housekeeping. You are fully employed, but your resume is starving. 

Here are five warning signs that your current job is shrinking your market value while keeping you too exhausted to notice.

## 1. Your daily wins only make sense to people who share your office email domain
If you spend your week resolving internal ticketing systems, chasing down approvals from specific department heads, or formatting reports in a proprietary software tool that nobody outside your company uses, you are doing phantom work. 

When you apply for a new job, a recruiter wants to see transferable achievements. They want to know if you grew revenue, managed external client relationships, shipped a product, or optimized a public-facing process. If your primary accomplishment this quarter was successfully migrating the company shared drive from one obscure folder structure to another, you are accumulating company-specific lore rather than industry-standard skills.

## 2. You are the permanent 'fixer' for routine operational friction
Every organization has gaps—processes that were never quite built right or software tools that don't talk to each other. In a healthy company, these gaps get automated or assigned to dedicated operations teams. In a shrinking role, you become the human patch cord. 

Ask yourself: Are you spending hours every week manually copying data from a PDF into an Excel sheet because nobody bothered to set up an integration? Are you regularly acting as a human mediator because two departments refuse to talk to each other directly? This labor feels helpful and earns you internal praise, but it is invisible to the broader job market. You are being paid for your labor, but you are not being compensated in resume currency.

## 3. Your title has seniority, but your scope has contracted
A dangerous trap is getting a nice-sounding title upgrade—like "Senior Coordinator" or "Operations Lead"—while your actual responsibilities pivot downward into execution rather than strategy. 

I once watched a colleague get promoted to manager, only to find herself spending 80 percent of her time scheduling meetings for her boss, booking travel, and formatting PowerPoint decks. She had the title of a leader, but the daily output of an entry-level assistant. When she eventually went on the job market, interviewers asked about her leadership experience, and she had zero examples of managing people, setting budgets, or owning a strategic project. The title was a decoy that masked a shrinking professional scope.

## 4. You haven't touched a new tool or methodology in over a year
Technology and industry practices move fast. If you look back over the last twelve months and realize you haven't learned a new platform, adopted a modern workflow, or earned a credential relevant to your field, your skills are actively depreciating. 

The phantom-duty trap relies on comfort. The tasks you know how to do best are often the ones that require the least cognitive friction, making them easy to repeat endlessly. But if your daily routine relies entirely on skills you mastered three years ago, you are falling behind peers at other companies who are already working with newer tech stacks and modern methodologies.

## 5. You are too tired at 6:00 PM to work on your actual career
The ultimate symptom of the trap is exhaustion without output. Administrative chaos, endless status meetings, and putting out low-level fires drain your cognitive battery just as effectively as high-stakes strategic work. 

When 6:00 PM rolls around, you don't have the energy to study for a certification, update your portfolio, or network with people in your industry. You log off feeling wrung out, which tricks your brain into thinking you had a productive day. In reality, you spent eight hours running on a treadmill, burning calories but going nowhere.

## Breaking Free From the Trap

Recognizing these signs is the first step toward reclaiming your career trajectory. You don't necessarily need to quit your job tomorrow, but you do need to audit where your time and mental energy are actually going. Start by tracking your tasks for a single week and categorizing them into career-building work and invisible administrative glue. Once you see the imbalance clearly, you can begin the process of restructuring your daily routine, pushing back on low-value requests, and redirecting your energy toward skills that travel with you wherever you go.
