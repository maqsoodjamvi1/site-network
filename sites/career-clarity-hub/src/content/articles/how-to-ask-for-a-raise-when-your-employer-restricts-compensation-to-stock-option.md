Hearing that your company cannot increase your cash salary because "compensation bands are strictly tied to stock options" is a frustrating pivot during a performance review. You wanted cash to pay your mortgage or student loans, but instead, HR is handing you a spreadsheet of projected share values. 

Navigating this conversation requires moving past the frustration and figuring out how to turn those equity grants into something that actually serves your financial goals. Here is how I approach this exact conversation with employers and how you can prepare for your next compensation review.

## Understand the Real Constraint

When a company tells you that compensation is restricted to stock options, they are usually dealing with a cash-flow limit or a strict internal leveling framework. Startups and mid-growth companies often use equity to preserve cash for operations, while public companies may have rigid pools allocated by stock administration teams. 

Before you walk into the meeting, you need to know which type of equity you are discussing. Options (ISOs or NQSOs) require you to spend your own cash to exercise them, whereas RSUs (Restricted Stock Units) are handed to you as actual shares when they vest. If your employer is offering options instead of cash, you are taking on market risk. Frame your response around that reality: equity is an investment, but salary is payment for services rendered.

## Shift the Negotiation from Salary to Total Value

If management will not budge on base pay, you need to expand what is on the table. Cash salary is only one component of your total compensation package. If they want to pay you in stock, you need to negotiate terms that make that stock significantly more valuable or less risky.

Here are a few specific levers you can pull during the counter-offer stage:

*   **Ask for a larger equity multiplier:** Since stock is cheaper for the company to give away than cash, ask for a higher volume. If they initially offered 5,000 options, counter with 10,000 to offset the lack of guaranteed cash.
*   **Negotiate an accelerated vesting schedule:** Standard vesting is often four years with a one-year cliff. Ask to shorten the cliff or accelerate vesting based on specific company milestones you control.
*   **Inquire about an equity refresh cycle:** Do not accept a one-time grant as a replacement for an annual raise. Ask for a commitment to review and grant additional equity every twelve months.
*   **Trade equity for guaranteed cash bonuses:** If base salary is off the table, ask for a performance-based cash bonus tied to quarterly metrics. Bonuses are often budgeted differently than permanent salary increases.

## Do the Math Before the Meeting

Never negotiate blind. If your employer offers you 10,000 stock options with a strike price of $5, you need to run scenarios on what happens if the stock stays flat, drops, or triples. 

Factor in your personal tax situation, too. Exercising options triggers different tax events depending on whether they are Incentive Stock Options or Non-Qualified Stock Options. If you have to pay out-of-pocket to exercise those options, you might actually be losing liquid cash in the short term just to hold company stock. Make sure you clearly communicate to your manager that taking on this financial vehicle carries real costs for you.

## Script Your Talking Points

Walking in with an emotional plea about your rent going up rarely works with finance departments. Instead, anchor your request to your market value and your recent performance data. 

Try using a script like this: *"I appreciate that our current compensation framework relies on stock options rather than base salary adjustments. However, my primary financial obligations require liquid cash. If we must use equity to bridge this gap, I need to look at a larger grant size and a shorter vesting schedule to make this package competitive with current market rates for my role."*

This approach acknowledges their policy while firmly holding your ground on total value. It forces them to look at the math of your retention rather than hiding behind a corporate policy handbook.

Ultimately, if a company refuses to pay you in cash and their stock prospects look stagnant or risky, you have to decide if the resume title is worth the discounted pay. Evaluating your next steps starts with looking at your broader career trajectory and deciding whether it is time to test the open market for a cash-paying role.
