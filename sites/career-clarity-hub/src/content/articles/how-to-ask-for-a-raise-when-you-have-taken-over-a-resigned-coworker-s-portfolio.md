When a coworker resigns and their work gets dropped onto your desk, management usually calls it "pitching in" or "keeping the lights on." But after a few weeks, reality sets in. You aren't just helping out; you are doing two jobs for the price of one. 

If you are currently drowning in extra accounts, tracking double the projects, or managing a workload that clearly belongs to two people, it is time to talk about a pay increase. Here is how I approached this exact situation, and how you can do it without sounding ungrateful.

## Calculate the Exact Scope Creep

Before you schedule a meeting with your boss, you need hard numbers. Vague complaints like "I'm really busy lately" won't result in a higher paycheck. You need to prove that the job description you signed up for no longer matches your daily reality.

Write down your original core responsibilities on one side of a page, and list the newly absorbed portfolio duties on the other. Include metrics where possible. For example, if you took over three major client accounts, write down their annual revenue value. If your daily email volume doubled or you inherited management of a weekly reporting pipeline, quantify that time. 

When I did this, I realized I had absorbed about 15 hours of weekly work from a departing colleague. Presenting that metric shifted the conversation from a subjective feeling of stress to an objective business reality: the company was getting 25% more output from me for the same flat rate.

## Build Your Value Case

Management rarely looks at payroll and thinks, "We should pay that person more just because." They look at payroll through the lens of risk and ROI. Your pitch shouldn't focus on how tired you are; it needs to focus on what the company would lose if you dropped the ball on this new portfolio.

Gather evidence of your success with the transition. Did you onboard your former coworker's clients without a single complaint? Did you catch a missed deadline before it became a crisis? Make a short list of wins from the past month. 

*   Zero drop in client satisfaction scores across the newly acquired accounts.
*   Successful delivery of the Q3 deliverables that were orphaned during the departure.
*   Cross-training two junior staff members on tasks from the absorbed workload to keep things moving.

By framing your request around the stability and value you provided during a staffing disruption, you position yourself as a crucial asset rather than a complainer.

## Time the Request Correctly

Timing matters heavily when asking for a pay bump outside of the standard annual review cycle. Do not bring this up during a chaotic week when your boss is dealing with a major crisis or a budget freeze. 

Instead, look for a natural inflection point. Good moments to schedule this conversation include:
*   Right after you successfully close out the first major project or month-end cycle utilizing the combined workload.
*   When management officially confirms that they are not planning to backfill your former coworker's position anytime soon.
*   During your regular one-on-one meeting, after giving your boss 24 hours' notice that you want to discuss workload alignment and compensation.

## Script the Conversation

Walking into your manager's office to talk about money can feel intimidating. Keep the tone professional, calm, and collaborative. Treat it like a business negotiation, not an emotional grievance.

Here is a practical script you can adapt:

> "Thanks for meeting with me. Since [Coworker's Name] left six weeks ago, I’ve absorbed their portfolio consisting of [mention 2-3 specific major duties]. I’ve managed to keep all those deliverables on track and maintain our client relationships, but looking ahead at the long-term structure, I am essentially handling two distinct roles. I want to discuss adjusting my compensation to reflect this expanded scope of work."

Then, stop talking. Let your manager respond. They might push back by saying it's a temporary assignment, in which case you should ask for a specific timeline on when that temporary coverage ends or when a backfill will start.

If they agree that the workload is permanent, you have successfully set the stage for a salary adjustment. Deciding your next steps after this conversation is crucial to ensuring your career and compensation continue moving in the right direction.
