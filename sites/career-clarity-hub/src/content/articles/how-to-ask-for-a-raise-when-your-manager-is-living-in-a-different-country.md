Asking for a raise is always high-stakes, but the difficulty spikes when your boss lives on a completely different continent. For the past year, my manager has been based in London while I work out of Chicago. When it came time to negotiate my compensation, I quickly realized I couldn't rely on the old playbook of catching someone after a Tuesday morning meeting or knocking on an office door. 

Working across time zones means your manager rarely sees your day-to-day hustle in real-time. If you want a pay bump, you have to bridge the visibility gap intentionally. Here is the exact approach I used to successfully secure a 12% raise without ever sitting across a physical desk from my boss.

## Build a Paper Trail of Your Impact

When your manager is seven hours ahead, they are missing the informal cues of your hard work. They don't see you staying late to fix a broken pipeline, and they don't hear you skillfully de-escalating an angry client on the phone. You have to translate your labor into hard documentation.

Start keeping a weekly impact log at least two months before you plan to ask for a raise. Do not just list your tasks; list your outcomes. Instead of writing "managed Q3 email campaigns," write "restructured Q3 email cadence, resulting in a 14% lift in click-through rates and $22,000 in direct attributed revenue." When you send your Friday wrap-up email, highlight one major win. This ensures that when your manager opens your review file, your recent accomplishments are front and center, not buried in ancient history.

## Master Asynchronous Communication

Timing matters immensely in negotiations. If you drop a detailed compensation request into an asynchronous message late on a Friday afternoon in your manager's time zone, it will sit and fester over the weekend, breeding anxiety for both of you.

Instead, request a dedicated video call specifically for a career and compensation check-in at least a week in advance. Do not ambush them during a routine one-on-one. Send an email with a clear subject line like "Upcoming Compensation Review Discussion," and attach your impact log. This gives your manager time to review your metrics and, crucially, time to consult with their own leadership or HR counterparts before you meet face-to-face on screen.

## Account for Currency and Cost of Living Realities

Global companies often look at compensation through a regional lens. If your company is headquartered in a country with a different currency or a lower cost of living, your manager might genuinely not understand the local economic pressures you are facing in the US. 

Do your homework before the call:
* Pull local salary data for your exact job title and seniority level using sites like Levels.fyi, Salary.com, or Built In.
* Benchmark your current pay against the current local market rate, factoring in inflation and rising local expenses.
* Frame your request around market value and expanded responsibilities, rather than personal financial need. 

Saying "my rent went up" rarely works. Saying "market rate for this expanded portfolio in the US is $95,000, and I am currently at $80,000" gives your manager a factual basis to take to their finance department.

## Treat the Video Call Like an In-Person Meeting

Virtual fatigue is real, but you cannot phone in a raise conversation. Set yourself up for success by treating the video call with the same gravity as an in-person boardroom meeting. 

Log on five minutes early, test your microphone, and ensure your lighting is professional. When you make your pitch, be direct and state your number clearly. Avoid rambling or preemptively apologizing for asking. 

* State your appreciation for the role and the team.
* Present the summary of your expanded responsibilities and documented wins.
* Deliver your specific target number or percentage.
* Stop talking and let them respond. 

Distance can make awkward silences feel even longer over a Zoom connection. Resist the urge to fill the dead air immediately after you state your case; let your manager digest the data and reply.

## Plan for the Approval Chain Delay

When your manager is remote, your raise request likely has to travel through layers of bureaucracy you cannot see. Your direct boss might be entirely on board, but they still have to pitch it to an HR business partner or a finance director who is sitting in yet another time zone. 

Do not panic if you do not get a "yes" on the spot. Ask your manager what their internal approval process looks like and when you can expect a follow-up date. Set a calendar reminder to ping them gently if that date passes without an update.

Navigating a remote negotiation takes extra prep work, but it is entirely manageable once you treat your visibility as something you actively manage rather than leave to chance. Take a look at your calendar, pick a date a few weeks out to start logging your wins, and map out your next steps toward earning what you are worth.
