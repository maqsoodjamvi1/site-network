Being the star player on a struggling team is an exhausting balancing act. On one hand, you are carrying the department, fixing broken processes, and likely covering for coworkers who aren’t pulling their weight. On the other hand, when annual review season rolls around, you worry that your manager will lump you into a generalized pool of "underperformance" and deny you a pay bump. 

I’ve lived this exact scenario. A few years ago, I was the sole analyst on a marketing team that missed nearly every quarterly KPI for twelve straight months. Morale was low, leadership was breathing down our necks, but my individual metrics were the highest in the department. When I decided to ask for a 15% raise, I knew I couldn't just walk into my boss's office and complain about my teammates. I had to build a case that completely unlinked my personal value from the team's overall scorecard. 

Here is the exact framework I used to secure that raise, and how you can do the same without throwing your colleagues under the bus.

## Separate Your Metrics from the Team's Baseline

The biggest trap you can fall into is letting leadership grade you on a curve dictated by your peers. If your team misses a revenue target by 20%, your manager’s first instinct will be to protect the company budget by freezing raises across the board. 

You need to rewrite the narrative before the conversation even starts. Pull your own granular data from the past six to twelve months. Do not talk about team goals; talk about your individual output, revenue generated, time saved, or projects rescued. 

For example, instead of saying, "We hit our department goals," frame it like this: "While the wider team faced headwinds meeting quota this year, my individual pipeline generated $350,000 in new business, which represents 40% of our total regional revenue, despite us being short-staffed by two people for six months." By anchoring the conversation to hard numbers you directly controlled, you make it mathematically difficult for a manager to say no based on team-wide struggles.

## Quantify Your "Invisible Labor"

When a team is underperforming, the highest performer inevitably absorbs the fallout. You are likely fixing mistakes, answering questions for peers, and stepping in to manage client escalations that technically fall outside your job description. 

In a standard performance review, this invisible labor goes unnoticed. You need to drag it into the light. Make a list of every task you take on that is outside your core job description. 

*   Onboarding and training struggling peers on software or workflows.
*   Taking over high-risk accounts or projects that were failing under someone else's watch.
*   Stepping in to lead meetings or coordinate logistics when leadership is absent.
*   Creating templates, SOPs, or automation tools that the whole team now relies on.

When you present this list to your manager, frame it as risk mitigation. Remind them gently that if you were to leave, the team’s current output wouldn't just drop—it would likely flatline completely.

## Schedule the Meeting Without Mentioning the Raise

Never ambush your manager with a compensation request during a standard one-on-one or a rushed Friday afternoon catch-up. You want their undivided attention, and you want them to come prepared.

Send a calendar invite specifically titled "Career Growth and Scope Review." In the description, give them a heads-up on what you want to discuss so they aren't caught off guard. 

*   "Hi [Manager's Name], I'd like to schedule 30 minutes next Tuesday to review my current project scope, my contributions over the last two quarters, and align on my compensation relative to my expanded responsibilities."

This signals professionalism and tells your boss that you are treating this as a business negotiation, not an emotional plea driven by frustration.

## Practice Delivering the Pitch Without Complaining

The hardest part of this conversation is advocating for yourself without sounding bitter about your coworkers. It is vital that you do not disparage your team. If you spend ten minutes complaining about how lazy or incompetent your peers are, your manager will get defensive, and the meeting will derail into team management instead of your pay.

Keep the focus entirely on your value and market rate. Here is a script template you can adapt:

"I’ve really enjoyed stepping up this year to help stabilize our department. As you know, we've faced some operational hurdles, but I’ve focused heavily on keeping my individual output high—closing X deals, managing Y accounts, and stepping in to build those new training processes for the group. Looking at my current market value and the expanded scope of my day-to-day work, I am looking to bring my salary up to [Target Dollar Amount/Percentage]. Based on my performance and the results I've driven, I'd love to know what we need to do to make this adjustment happen."

Notice what this script does: it states your value, acknowledges the reality of the department without blaming anyone, and directly asks for a specific number. 

If your manager tries to deflect by saying, "We just don't have the budget right now because the team missed targets," your counter should be immediate and calm. Remind them of your retention risk. Say something like, "I understand the broader budget constraints, but given that my retention is critical to keeping these specific accounts afloat, I want to make sure my compensation reflects my impact rather than the department's baseline."

Preparing for this conversation takes courage, especially when you are already carrying the weight of a struggling department. However, companies will rarely volunteer to pay you more simply because you are doing a good job in the background. Taking control of the narrative, bringing your own numbers, and treating the discussion as a business proposition is the most reliable way to get paid what you are actually worth.
