Counting down the days to your next Restricted Stock Unit vest date is a unique kind of professional purgatory. You look at your employee stock portal like a prisoner watching tally marks on a cell wall. The math is simple: if you quit today, you forfeit thousands of dollars. If you stay until the 15th of next month, that block of shares moves from "unvested" to your personal brokerage account. 

So, you stay. But your brain leaves the building about six weeks early. 

In tech, finance, and corporate environments where equity makes up a massive chunk of compensation, this is a well-known phenomenon. I call it the "vested-out drift." It is that strange period where you are physically employed and collecting a salary, but psychologically, you mentally quit weeks ago. 

How do you know if you are experiencing normal career fatigue or if you have officially slipped into the vested-out drift? Here are five signs that your mind has already clocked out while your body is still logging into Slack.

## 1. Your Calendar Is Full of "Focus Time" That Yields Zero Focus
When you are engaged in your work, calendar blocks are used to draft architecture docs, prepare client pitches, or clear out a dense queue of tickets. When you are checked out waiting for a vest date, those two-hour "Focus Time" blocks serve a completely different purpose. 

You use them to scroll through LinkedIn job openings, research housing markets in a city you want to move to, or calculate how many shares you need to sell to cover a kitchen remodel. You aren't building anything new. You are just managing your exit strategy in plain sight, hoping no one pings you with a sudden urgent request while you are deep in a Zillow rabbit hole.

## 2. You Have Lowered Your Standards for What Constitutes a "Good Enough" Meeting
Normally, you might prep a slide deck, anticipate pushback from engineering, and make sure your metrics are airtight before presenting to a director. Now? Your preparation consists of opening an existing deck, changing the date on the title slide, and speaking entirely in corporate platitudes. 

"Let's take this offline," and "We need to align on deliverables" become your entire vocabulary. You aren't trying to win arguments anymore; you are just trying to survive meetings without committing to any new long-term projects that might extend your timeline or require an awkward handoff later.

## 3. You Stop Translating Your Achievements for Your Next Review
Performance review season rolls around, and your manager asks for your self-evaluation. A year ago, you would have written a mini-thesis detailing your cross-functional leadership, revenue impact, and efficiency gains. 

During the vested-out drift, your self-review looks like a grocery list. You list the bare minimum operational tasks you completed just to keep the lights on. You stop lobbying for the promotion you wanted six months ago because you already know you are leaving the second that stock clears. Why invest emotional capital in a ladder you are about to step off?

## 4. You No Longer Feel Friction When Things Go Wrong
This is the most telling symptom. When a project hits a wall or a client throws a tantrum, an engaged employee feels a spike of adrenaline and anxiety. They want to fix it. 

When you are mentally checked out, your internal reaction changes entirely. A production bug happens? You shrug and type, "Let's page the on-call engineer." A cross-functional partner drops the ball? You gently nod and think, *Not my circus, not my shares.* That complete absence of friction or panic is a dead giveaway that your emotional investment in the company has hit absolute zero. You aren't angry; you are just a spectator.

## 5. You Refuse to Look Past the Vest Date
Ask yourself this question: What are you working on for the quarter *after* your vest date? If the answer is an absolute blank because your mental roadmap stops dead on the exact calendar day your shares settle, you are deep in the drift. 

You have effectively frozen your professional timeline. You are treating the vest date like a cliff, past which time ceases to exist. While this protects your mental energy, it can create a frantic scramble once the shares actually land and you realize you haven't laid any groundwork for what comes next.

Recognizing these signs isn't a reason to beat yourself up. Acknowledging that you are fried and just marking time for your equity is a normal reaction to golden handcuffs. The key is to manage this transition cleanly so you don't burn bridges you might need later, while preparing for the practical steps you need to take once those shares finally hit your account.
