When my husband’s parents installed a brand-new video doorbell right before the holidays, we thought it was a standard home-security upgrade. That changed on Thanksgiving morning when my mother-in-law texted us, "Loved your matching aprons—too bad you didn't answer when we knocked five minutes later." 

Her camera wasn't angled at *her* porch. Because our properties sit close together on a suburban corner lot, the wide-angle lens of their new device captured our entire front walkway, our porch swing, and every single person walking through our front door. 

If you are dealing with in-laws whose security setup feels less like neighborhood watch and more like a 24/7 surveillance feed of your private life, you know how quickly it turns into an uncomfortable boundary issue. Here is how I learned to handle the situation practically, calmly, and without starting a full-scale family feud.

## Check Your Local Laws First

Before you say a single word to your partner or your in-laws, take ten minutes to look up local privacy laws regarding video surveillance in your state or municipality. While property owners generally have the right to secure their own homes, many jurisdictions draw a firm legal line when cameras capture the interior living spaces, bedrooms, or private entryways of neighboring properties without consent. 

You do not need to threaten legal action right away—in fact, doing so will likely blow the situation up. However, knowing whether their camera setup violates local ordinances gives you a quiet sense of confidence. It moves the conversation from "I'm annoyed" to "This actually crosses a legal boundary regarding our household privacy."

## Let Your Partner Take the Lead

When family boundaries are crossed, the golden rule is that you deal with your own relatives. If my parents overstepped, it was my job to correct them; when my husband’s parents did it, it was squarely on his shoulders. 

Trying to confront your in-laws yourself about a security camera often backfires. They may view you as overly sensitive, dramatic, or controlling. When your partner approaches their own parents, it frames the issue as a joint family decision rather than an attack from an in-law. 

Sit down with your partner away from the home and get completely aligned. Agree on what bothers you—is it the constant monitoring of your coming and going, or is it the passive-aggressive comments based on what the camera sees? Once you agree, let your partner make the initial phone call or visit.

## Blame the Technology, Not the Intentions

When your partner brings up the camera placement with your in-laws, keep the tone collaborative rather than accusatory. People immediately get defensive when you imply they are being nosy. 

Instead, frame the issue around the quirks of wide-angle technology. You can use an approach like this: 
* "Hey Mom and Dad, we noticed the new doorbell camera has an ultra-wide lens. Because of the angle, it’s actually recording our entire front porch and walkway whenever anyone comes to our house. We're a bit uncomfortable having our comings and goings recorded continuously right at our front door, so we wanted to figure out how to adjust the zone."

This phrasing assumes the best of them—that they just bought a gadget without thinking through the field of view—while clearly establishing that the current setup is unacceptable.

## Offer Concrete Solutions

Sometimes, people genuinely do not know how to adjust their smart home devices. If your in-laws are older or less tech-savvy, they might have simply screwed the device into the siding and walked away, unaware that they can fix the problem digitally or physically.

Come to the conversation with practical fixes ready to go:
* **Privacy Zones:** Most modern video doorbells allow users to draw digital blackout boxes over neighboring properties so those areas are automatically masked and never recorded.
* **Angle Mounts:** A cheap wedge mount can tilt a camera five or fifteen degrees downward or sideways, keeping their view strictly on their own welcome mat.
* **Shorter Detection Ranges:** Adjusting the motion sensitivity keeps the camera from triggering every time your dog goes out to use the bathroom.

If they claim they "don't know how" to fix these settings, offer to log into their app and adjust the privacy zones for them the next time you visit. 

## Create Physical Barriers If They Refuse to Adjust

If your in-laws get defensive, claim they have a right to secure their property, or simply refuse to change the camera angle, you have every right to take matters into your own hands on your property line. 

You cannot legally touch or disable their hardware, but you can block its line of sight. Depending on your home's layout, you can install a tall potted plant, a privacy screen, an overhead patio awning, or outdoor blinds that obstruct the camera's view of your front door. If they complain that they can no longer see your porch, your partner can calmly remind them: "That's the point. We needed our privacy back."

Navigating boundary issues with extended family requires patience, but persistent privacy invasions like unauthorized video surveillance need an immediate, direct response. Taking care of this issue early protects your household peace and sets a healthy precedent for future boundaries.
