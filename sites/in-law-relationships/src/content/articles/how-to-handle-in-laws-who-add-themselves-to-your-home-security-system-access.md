When my mother-in-law asked for the app login to our newly installed Ring camera system "just in case of emergencies," I handed it over without thinking. It felt like a minor peace-of-mind gesture. Two weeks later, my husband and I got a push notification while sitting on our own living room couch: *Front door motion detected.* A minute later, my phone buzzed with a text message from her asking why the living room lights were still on at 11:00 PM. 

That was the moment I realized boundary-setting around smart home technology is an entirely new frontier in family relationships. If you are dealing with in-laws who have added themselves—or demanded to be added—to your home security system access, you are likely feeling a mix of frustration, invasion of privacy, and guilt. Here is how I learned to take back control of our front door without starting an all-out family war.

## Assess the Actual Access Level

Before you have any conversations, open your security app and audit what your in-laws can actually see and do. Many popular systems like SimpliSafe, Ring, ADT, or Google Nest allow for different permission tiers, but users often hand out master admin credentials by mistake.

Look for these specific access points in your settings:
*   **Live video feeds:** Can they watch your interior cameras, or just the exterior doorbell?
*   **Arming and disarming:** Can they shut off your alarm system remotely?
*   **Geofencing and location data:** Does the system share your phone's location to automatically arm when you leave?
*   **Activity logs:** Can they see exact timestamps of when you come and go?

If they have admin rights, they can change settings, delete video history, or even lock you out of your own hardware. If they are logged into a shared account rather than a designated "guest" profile, you lose all ability to monitor who is watching what.

## Have the Conversation Without Blaming

The hardest part of this dilemma is the delivery. If you accuse your in-laws of spying, they will likely get defensive and play the victim card, claiming they were "just trying to help protect the house." 

Instead, frame the change around your own household routines and digital privacy habits. You want to shift the focus away from *them* and onto *your* family's need for autonomy. 

I sat down with my husband and we agreed on a script. When we spoke to his mother, we said: "We are streamlining our smart home setup and realizing that having multiple households on one primary login is messing with our geofencing and notification settings. We are revoking all secondary app access so we can manage the house notifications ourselves." 

Notice what this does: it blames the technology, not her behavior. It is a technical reset, not a personal rejection.

## Revoke Access and Reconfigure the App

Once you have had the talk, do the digital cleanup immediately. Do not wait for them to "get around to logging out." 

Change your master account password right away. If your system uses two-factor authentication—which it should—make sure the verification code comes to your phone or your partner's phone only. 

If you still want to offer a compromised middle ground for genuine emergencies (like watering plants while you are away on vacation), set up a restricted guest profile instead of sharing your main login. Most modern apps let you give someone temporary access to a specific camera for a set number of days. Once the trip is over, the access expires automatically without requiring a confrontation.

## Establish Permanent Digital Boundaries

In-laws who overstep with home security often overstep in other physical ways, too. Once you lock down the app, expect a grace period of mild pushback. They might ask why a camera is offline or mention that they tried to check the weather camera and couldn't get in.

Hold your ground calmly. Repeat a simple, neutral phrase: "We decided to keep our home system private to our immediate household." Do not J.A.D.E. (Justify, Argue, Defend, or Explain). You do not need to prove *why* you want privacy in your own home.

Managing family dynamics when modern technology makes everyone feel entitled to constant updates is exhausting, but your home has to remain your safe space.
