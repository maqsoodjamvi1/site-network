Getting married or committing to a long-term partner is supposed to mean joining a new family. But when your mother-in-law or father-in-law repeatedly uses the wrong name—whether they refuse to switch to your married last name, shorten your first name into a nickname you despise, or cling to an ex-partner's name—it feels like a deliberate rejection. 

When it happened to me, I spent months agonizing over whether I was making a big deal out of nothing. I kept telling myself to let it go to keep the peace. But every time I heard that wrong name across the Thanksgiving table, my stomach dropped. 

If you are trying to decide how to handle in-laws who refuse to use your preferred name, you need a strategy that protects your self-respect without blowing up your marriage. Here is how I navigated it, and the practical steps you can take starting today.

## Assess the Intent Behind the Mistake

Before you draft a battle plan, you need to figure out if this is a genuine slip of the tongue or passive-aggressive hostility. Human brains are creatures of habit. If your in-laws have known you by one name for a decade, or if they spent forty years calling their son’s previous girlfriend a certain name, genuine cognitive inertia is real. 

Look for patterns. Do they correct themselves immediately when they mess up, or do they look you dead in the eye and keep talking? Do they make the mistake in private, or only when extended family is watching? 

If it is an honest mistake, grace and time usually fix it. If it is a power play designed to establish dominance and signal that you don't truly belong in the family, you need a different approach.

## Let Your Partner Take the Lead

The golden rule of in-law conflict is that you manage your family, and your partner manages theirs. When my in-laws kept using the wrong name, I initially tried to correct them myself. It backfired. They played the victim, accused me of being overly sensitive, and painted me as the bad guy to the rest of the family.

Sit down with your partner and get on the exact same page. Explain how it affects you—not just that it's annoying, but that it feels deeply disrespectful to your identity and your union. 

Ask your partner to have a private, calm conversation with their parents. Your partner needs to say something concrete, such as: "Mom, Dad, when you call her by her maiden name instead of her legal last name, it dismisses our marriage. We need you to use her correct name moving forward." 

When the correction comes from their own child, it removes you as the target and makes it a boundary set by the family unit.

## The Strategy of the Polite Ignore

If your partner has already spoken to them and the behavior continues, stop rewarding the disrespect with your attention. When we react with anger, dramatic sighs, or visible frustration, we sometimes feed the exact dynamic the in-laws are looking for.

Instead, try the deliberate pause. The next time you are sitting at dinner and you are addressed by the wrong name, simply stop talking. Count to three in your head. Do not answer. Look at them neutrally, waiting for them to realize they are talking to thin air. 

When they pause, calmly say, "Are you talking to me? My name is [Preferred Name]." Then immediately pivot the conversation to a completely different topic. Do not argue, do not roll your eyes, and do not storm out. Treat it like dealing with a toddler testing a boundary: firm, boring, and utterly consistent.

## Decide on Your Personal Boundaries

At a certain point, ongoing disrespect requires consequences. You have to decide what you are willing to tolerate and what your personal line in the sand looks like. 

*   **Limit exposure:** If visiting your in-laws always results in intentional misnaming and emotional exhaustion, cut the visits short. Leave early, or stay in a hotel rather than sleeping at their house.
*   **Decline hosting duties:** You are under no obligation to open your home to people who refuse to respect your basic identity under your own roof.
*   **Drop the rope:** Stop buying their birthday gifts, stop calling to check in on them, and let your partner handle 100 percent of the labor required to maintain that relationship.

Deciding to pull back isn't petty; it is a healthy preservation of your mental health.

Handling in-laws who disrespect your name is a test of endurance and partnership. It forces you and your spouse to decide how you will handle boundary violations as a team moving forward. As you weigh your next steps and figure out how much access these family members will have to your time and energy, it helps to look at the broader picture of your relationship dynamics.
