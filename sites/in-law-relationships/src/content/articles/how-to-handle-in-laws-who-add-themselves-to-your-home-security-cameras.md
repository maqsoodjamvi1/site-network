When my mother-in-law asked for the login credentials to our front porch camera last summer, I thought it was about package theft. We lived in a busy suburban neighborhood, and she had a habit of dropping off homemade soup on our porch. Handing over the app access felt like a harmless convenience. 

That convenience vanished the moment my phone buzzed at 9:15 PM on a Tuesday with a motion alert, followed immediately by a text message from her: "You left the side gate unlatched, and the recycling bin is still out at the curb. I can come over tomorrow morning and put it away for you." 

That was the day I realized our smart home setup had accidentally invited a third person into our marriage. If you are dealing with well-meaning in-laws who treat your home security feeds like a public broadcast network, you need a game plan that stops the overstepping without turning Thanksgiving into a hostage negotiation. Here is how my spouse and I navigated this exact mess, step by concrete step.

## Assess the Current Digital Footprint

Before you have any awkward conversations, you need to know the exact scope of access you have granted. Log into your camera app—whether it is Ring, Arlo, Eufy, or Google Nest—and check the user management settings. 

You might be surprised by what you find. In our case, I thought my mother-in-law had a standard "guest" view limited to one camera. Instead, because of how I set up the initial share link months prior, she had full administrative privileges. She could theoretically adjust motion zones, speak through the two-way audio, and view historical clips from our backyard camera, too. 

Take screenshots of the permission settings so you have a clear baseline. Decide beforehand which cameras, if any, you are actually comfortable sharing. Most families find that a doorbell camera facing the public street is the absolute maximum boundary for extended family. Backyard, side yard, and interior cameras should be strictly off-limits to everyone outside your immediate household.

## Blame the Technology, Not the Relative

Nobody reacts well to being told, "You are being too nosy." When you initiate this conversation, the easiest way to prevent defensiveness is to lean heavily on privacy boundaries as a general rule, rather than a direct accusation of bad behavior. 

Instead of saying, "Stop watching our house," try framing the change around how your own household uses the technology. Say something like: "We are actually revamping all our smart home permissions this weekend. We realized having outside users logged into the app is messing with our motion alerts and privacy zones, so we are taking everyone off the shared list except for the two of us."

This shifts the blame to the software, the router, or your own need for digital minimalism. It depersonalizes the restriction, making it much harder for an overbearing relative to take it as a personal slight. 

## Revoke Access and Reconfigure the App Settings

Do not announce that you are changing permissions and then wait for permission. Just do it. Open your app, go to the shared users or household members section, and remove their account entirely. 

Once they are removed, take a few minutes to clean up your notification settings so you do not get sucked back into old habits. Turn off any shared alert features, and make sure your login requires two-factor authentication sent only to your personal phone. 

If your in-laws live nearby and genuinely do need to check if you are home before dropping off groceries, establish a low-tech alternative. A simple text message asking, "Are you around today?" restores basic human courtesy and keeps the digital boundary intact.

## Hold the Line When the Notifications Stop

The real test happens about forty-eight hours after you revoke access. When your mother-in-law realizes she can no longer see if your car is in the driveway, she is going to ask questions. 

When you get the inevitable text message asking why the app is not working or why the camera feed went black, stick to your script. Do not over-explain, and do not apologize. A simple, polite repetition works best: "Oh, we had to reset the whole system for privacy reasons, so we aren't sharing the feed anymore." 

If they push further—insisting they only wanted to look out for packages—validate their good intentions while firmly closing the door. You can say, "We really appreciate you looking out for us, but we decided we prefer keeping our camera feeds completely private to just the two of us." Then, change the subject to something entirely unrelated, like the weather or weekend plans.

Managing family boundaries in the digital age requires the same intentionality as locking your front door. If your smart home setup is currently serving as an open window for extended family members, taking back control of your digital space is a necessary step for your peace of mind. As you look at ways to streamline your home's privacy and security settings, exploring how different apps handle user permissions can help you build a system that works for your household alone.
