When I first started time blocking my calendar, I thought I had solved productivity forever. I neatly carved up my days into colorful 60-minute rectangles. Write blog post: 9:00 to 10:00 AM. Answer emails: 10:00 to 11:00 AM. Review quarterly goals: 11:00 AM to 12:00 PM. 

It looked stunning on my screen. But by week three, I noticed a frustrating pattern. Simple administrative tasks that used to take me twenty minutes were suddenly stretching out to fill a full hour. I found myself agonizing over font choices, reorganizing folders I didn't need to touch, and slowly rereading emails just to pad the time until my calendar alarm went off. 

I had fallen straight into the Time Blocking Parkinson's Law Trap. 

Parkinson’s Law states that work expands to fill the time available for its completion. When you assign a rigid one-hour block to a task that actually requires thirty minutes of actual focus, your brain obligingly stretches the work out. You aren't being more thorough; you are just moving slower to fit the box you built.

## Why Your Calendar Blocks Are Backfiring

The core appeal of time blocking is that it stops you from wandering aimlessly through your workday. By giving every hour a job, you eliminate decision fatigue. However, beginners almost always make the same mistake: they match their schedule blocks to standard clock increments rather than the actual friction and duration of the work.

If you allocate 60 minutes to write a routine status update, your brain subconsciously registers that you have an entire hour to burn. You give yourself permission to check your phone, stare out the window, and over-edit every sentence. The task bloats to consume the real estate you gave it. 

Worse yet, this trap breeds a subtle form of daily exhaustion. You finish your workday feeling completely drained, but when you look back at your neatly colored calendar, you realize you didn't actually produce that much. You just spent eight hours slowly dragging your feet across oversized schedules.

## The Shrinking Block Method to Defeat Bloat

To break out of this trap, you need to change how you size your calendar appointments. Instead of defaulting to 30-minute or 60-minute chunks, use what I call the "shrinking block" method. 

Start by timing yourself on a routine task without any constraints, just to establish a baseline. Let us say you discover it takes you 20 minutes to process your daily inbox. Instead of scheduling a comfortable 30 or 60 minutes for "Email Processing," force yourself to schedule a tight 15-minute block. 

By deliberately under-allocating time, you trigger a healthy sense of urgency. You stop opening every single marketing newsletter and start rapidly archiving, deleting, and replying. You bypass perfectionism because the clock is actively working against you. 

## Actionable Rules for Tighter Time Blocks

If you want to keep the structure of time blocking without falling victim to task expansion, you have to change your scheduling habits. Here are the exact rules I use to keep my calendar honest:

*   **Cap routine tasks at 25 minutes:** Never give open-ended administrative tasks an hour. Force them into 15 to 25-minute sprints using a physical timer on your desk.
*   **Leave 15-minute buffers between blocks:** If you finish a task in 35 minutes instead of the scheduled 60, use that remaining 25 minutes to step away from your desk, stretch, or grab water. Do not just start the next block early, or you reward yourself with more work.
*   **Split deep work from shallow work:** Do not mix creative writing or coding with low-friction tasks like scheduling meetings. Creative work genuinely requires larger blocks, while administrative tasks require strict, aggressive caps.
*   **Track your actuals once a week:** Pick one day to compare what you *planned* to do versus how long it actually took. If you consistently finish tasks with minutes to spare, shrink those blocks on next week's calendar.

## Moving Forward With a Leaner Schedule

Time blocking is still one of the best tools for taking control of your day, but only if you treat your schedule as a constraint rather than a cushion. When you stop giving your tasks room to stretch out, you will often find that you can accomplish your daily to-do list in half the time, leaving your afternoons wide open for actual rest.
