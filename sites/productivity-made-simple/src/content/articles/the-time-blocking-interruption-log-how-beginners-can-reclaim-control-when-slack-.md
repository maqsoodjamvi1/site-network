If you have ever tried time blocking, you probably know the exact moment the system falls apart. 

You sit down at 9:00 AM, eager to dive into a ninety-minute block of deep work. You have your color-coded calendar looking pristine. Ten minutes in, a ping sounds in Slack. Someone needs a quick clarification on a project. You answer it. Then an email notification pops up. You check it—just to be safe. By 9:30 AM, you are deep in a rabbit hole of administrative tasks, and your time block is officially wrecked. 

Most productivity advice tells you to just close your tabs and turn off notifications. But if you work in a modern office, going completely dark for four hours is rarely realistic. You need a way to handle interruptions without letting them hijack your entire day. 

That is where a simple, analog tool called the Interruption Log comes in. 

## What Is an Interruption Log?

An Interruption Log is a physical piece of paper or a blank digital document you keep next to your keyboard while time blocking. Its sole purpose is to capture the urge to get distracted and the actual interruptions that land on your desk. 

When you start a time block, your brain is wired to resist deep focus. It craves novelty. Every time you feel the urge to check your inbox, or when a colleague drops a new request in your lap, you do not immediately act on it. Instead, you write it down in the log and return to your scheduled task. 

It sounds almost too simple to work, but it targets the psychological root of why time blocking fails for beginners: it bridges the gap between your rigid schedule and the messy reality of your workplace.

## How to Set Up Your First Log Today

You do not need an expensive app or a complex spreadsheet to make this work. Grab a legal pad, a sticky note, or open a blank text document. Draw three columns with the following headers:

*   **Time:** When did the interruption happen?
*   **Source and Content:** Who or what pulled you away (e.g., Slack from Sarah, or an internal thought like "buy groceries")?
*   **Urgency Score:** Rate it from 1 to 3, where 1 means it can wait until the end of the day, 2 means it needs attention this afternoon, and 3 means the building is metaphorically on fire right now.

Keep this log sitting right on your desk, physically visible, so it acts as a speed bump between your impulses and your keyboard.

## Putting It Into Practice: A Real Scenario

Imagine you are in a 10:00 AM to 11:30 AM block dedicated to writing a quarterly report. At 10:15 AM, a manager sends a message asking for last month's metrics. 

In the past, you would switch tabs, pull up the metrics, draft a reply, and lose twenty minutes of focus time. 

With the Interruption Log, your process changes:
1. You look at the Slack message. 
2. You quickly write down in your log: "10:15 - Slack from Mark re: monthly metrics - Score: 2."
3. You type a quick template response if necessary—or better yet, nothing at all if it's not a 3—and you go right back to your report.
4. You process that log entry during your scheduled email and chat buffer block at 11:30 AM.

Most things that feel urgent at 10:15 AM turn out to be completely manageable by 11:30 AM. By logging the interruption instead of reacting to it, you keep the steering wheel in your own hands.

## Building the Habit Without Burnout

Do not expect perfection on day one. You will still catch yourself clicking over to your email out of pure muscle memory. When that happens, do not beat yourself up or abandon your time blocks. Just log the slip-up, close the tab, and reset your timer. 

Over the course of a week, looking back at your Interruption Log gives you hard data on what is actually derailing your days. You might discover that certain colleagues always ping you with low-priority questions in the morning, giving you the leverage you need to politely request they batch their questions for later.
