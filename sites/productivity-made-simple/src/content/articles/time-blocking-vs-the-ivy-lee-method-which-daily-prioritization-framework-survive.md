If your work day usually gets hijacked by sudden Slack pings, unexpected client fires, and back-to-back meetings that leave you wondering what you actually accomplished, you have probably looked into daily prioritization frameworks. Two of the most popular systems you will find are Time Blocking and the Ivy Lee Method. 

I spent a month testing both methods in a real-world, highly interruptible work environment—managing freelance writing deadlines while handling unpredictable client communications. Here is how they actually hold up when your carefully laid plans collide with pure chaos.

## The Time Blocking Approach: Rigid Structure vs. Reality

Time blocking requires you to divide your day into distinct chunks of time on your calendar, assigning a specific task or category of tasks to each block. Instead of looking at a sprawling to-do list, you look at your calendar and see that from 9:00 AM to 10:30 AM, you are writing a draft; from 10:30 AM to 11:00 AM, you are processing email.

The theory is brilliant. It forces you to acknowledge that time is a finite resource and stops you from endlessly multi-tasking. 

However, the moment a client calls with an urgent request at 9:15 AM, the entire system starts to crack. On paper, time blocking is supposed to be flexible—you just drag the block to a later slot. In practice, when your schedule has three or four moving parts, moving one block creates a domino effect that ruins the rest of your afternoon. By Thursday, my calendar looked like a crime scene of crossed-out time slots and color-coded panic. 

Time blocking works wonderfully if you control your schedule. If you work in an environment where other people constantly dictate your priorities, it can become an exhausting exercise in constant rescheduling.

## The Ivy Lee Method: Simplicity on the Brink of Collapse

Originating from a 1921 efficiency consultation with industrialist Charles Schwab, the Ivy Lee Method is violently simple. Every evening, you write down the six most important things you need to achieve tomorrow. You cannot write seven. You rank them in strict order of importance. The next day, you start on task number one and work until it is finished before moving to number two. Whatever is left over at the end of the day gets moved to a new list of six for the following day.

When my schedule is absolute chaos, this method behaves completely differently than time blocking. Because it relies on a simple list rather than rigid calendar slots, it absorbs interruptions gracefully. 

If an emergency hits at 10:00 AM, I deal with it. When the dust settles, I don't have to recalculate my entire calendar. I just look down at my notepad, see that item number one is still unfinished, put my head back down, and get to work on it. The mental load of figuring out *what* to do next vanishes because the hierarchy was already decided the night before.

The downside? It requires brutal honesty during the planning phase. If you put "overhaul the entire website" as task number one, you will stall out immediately. 

## How They Handle Real-World Interruptions

To see which system actually survives a chaotic week, look at how they handle the inevitable Tuesday afternoon meltdown. 

* **Time Blocking in Chaos:** You miss your 1:00 PM deep-work block due to an impromptu review meeting. You now feel behind. You skip your planned break to catch up, leading to mental fatigue by 4:00 PM, which makes you slower and more prone to errors.
* **The Ivy Lee Method in Chaos:** Your 1:00 PM slot gets eaten by the same review meeting. You return to your desk at 2:30 PM, look at your scratchpad, and resume item number two. You might not finish all six items that day, but the rule is clear: you focus solely on the sequence, not the clock.

If you are trying to decide which framework to adopt, consider the nature of your daily friction. 

* Choose time blocking if you need to protect your hours from yourself—specifically, if you struggle with doomscrolling, getting lost in low-value emails, or letting small tasks expand to fill your entire day.
* Choose the Ivy Lee Method if your biggest enemy is decision fatigue, constant external interruptions, or a schedule where other people hold the remote control.

Ultimately, neither system will magically make more hours appear in your day. But matching the right framework to your actual work environment stops you from abandoning productivity habits entirely when things get messy. Take a look at your calendar and your typical day next week, and choose the tool that fits the level of control you actually have.
