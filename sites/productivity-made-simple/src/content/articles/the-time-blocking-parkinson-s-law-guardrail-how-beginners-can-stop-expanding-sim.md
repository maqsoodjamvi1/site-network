If you have ever cleared a two-hour block on your calendar to write a quick email newsletter, only to find yourself still tweaking the font and rewriting the intro right as the timer goes off, you have met Parkinson’s Law. 

Parkinson’s Law states that work expands to fill the time you allot for it. Give yourself a whole afternoon to update your budget spreadsheet, and it will somehow consume the entire afternoon. Give yourself twenty minutes before a client call, and you will suddenly discover a ruthless, laser-focused efficiency you didn't know you possessed. 

Time blocking is supposed to cure this. You look at your blank Monday schedule, divide it into neat little boxes, and assign a task to each one. But for beginners, time blocking often backfires. Instead of protecting your schedule, you end up handing over generous, bloated blocks of time to minor tasks. You write down "9:00 AM to 11:00 AM: Research." Because you gave yourself two hours, you wander down internet rabbit holes, read ten irrelevant articles, and organize your browser tabs. The time gets filled, but the actual productivity doesn't match the hours spent.

To make time blocking work, you need a guardrail against your own tendency to stretch simple chores. Here is how to stop letting tasks expand and start using time blocks to reclaim your day.

## Stop Booking Infinity Blocks

The most common beginner mistake is matching time blocks to the natural boundaries of the calendar rather than the size of the task. We default to 30-minute or 60-minute blocks because our digital calendars snap neatly to those increments. 

When you assign a clean, round hour to a task that realistically takes twenty minutes, you are inviting Parkinson’s Law in for a cup of coffee. Your brain recognizes the vast expanse of empty time and instinctively slows down to match it. You start typing slower, checking your phone between sentences, and overthinking minor decisions.

Instead of defaulting to standard calendar increments, audit your actual speed. If answering routine customer service emails takes you fifteen minutes on a brisk day, do not block out a full hour just to be safe. Block out twenty minutes, and set a loud physical timer on your desk. By shrinking the container, you force your brain to cut out the fluff and focus on execution.

## Build in Friction and Hard Stops

A time block without a clear endpoint is just a suggestion. To keep tasks from spilling over, you need to establish hard stops that trigger an immediate transition to whatever comes next. 

One practical way to do this is to schedule back-to-back tasks that require different types of physical or mental energy. If you give yourself a flexible schedule, a sluggish task will happily bleed into your break or your evening. But if your 10:00 AM task is "Draft project outline" and your 10:30 AM commitment is a hard stop to walk the dog or jump on a team check-in, you suddenly have a compelling reason to finish up. 

You can also use physical constraints as guardrails:
* **The battery trick:** Try doing a specific administrative task on your laptop while running on battery power with the charger in another room. When the low-battery warning pops up at 15%, use that as your artificial deadline to wrap up.
* **The tab rule:** Before you start a 25-minute writing block, close every browser tab except the single document you need. If you have to reopen your email, you have to break your focus, which acts as a natural speed bump against distraction.
* **The "good enough" threshold:** For routine tasks like expense reporting or cleaning out your inbox, define what "done" looks like before you start. Decide in advance that you are stopping as soon as the main objective is met, rather than hunting for perfection.

## Account for the Drag of Context Switching

When tasks expand to fill your time blocks, it usually ruins the rest of your schedule. If your morning tasks run thirty minutes over, that delay ripples through the afternoon, pushing everything back and leaving you frazzled by 5:00 PM. 

To prevent this, treat your time blocks like public transit schedules. If a train leaves at 9:00 AM, it doesn't wait for passengers who are leisurely strolling down the platform. If your time block for processing invoices ends at 10:15 AM, you must drop it—even if one invoice remains—and move on to your next scheduled item. You can always capture the leftover piece in a separate catch-all block later in the week, rather than letting one chore hijack your entire day.

Learning to master time blocks without falling into the Parkinson’s trap takes a bit of experimentation. As you start tightening your schedules and cutting down your block sizes, pay attention to where you actually finish early versus where you consistently hit a wall. Adjusting your containers based on real-world data will help you build a routine that moves fast and leaves room for the rest of your life.
