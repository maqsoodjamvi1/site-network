If your workday looks like a pinball machine—where Slack notifications bounce you into emergency emails, which bounce you into surprise meetings—you have probably tried a dozen different productivity systems. Two of the most talked-about options sit at opposite ends of the philosophy spectrum: Time Blocking and the Autofocus Method. 

I spent a month testing both systems back-to-back in my own chaotic, remote-work routine. Here is what actually happened when theory met reality, and how to figure out which one will keep your head above water.

## What Is Time Blocking (And Why It Fails When Chaos Hits)

Time blocking is the practice of dividing your calendar into distinct chunks of time, assigning a specific task to each block. If you use this method, your Tuesday morning might look like an architectural blueprint: 9:00 AM to 10:30 AM for writing a proposal, 10:30 AM to 11:00 AM for email triage, and 11:00 AM to 12:00 PM for deep work on a client deck. 

When things go according to plan, time blocking feels incredible. You look at your calendar and see a fortress of productivity. 

The problem? Real work is rarely an architectural blueprint. 

During my week of strict time blocking, the system imploded by Tuesday afternoon. A client called with an urgent website bug at 9:15 AM. That ate my proposal block. By noon, I was staring at a calendar that was entirely wrong, which triggered a familiar anxiety: the feeling that I was already "behind" by lunchtime. Rearranging the rest of the day took twenty minutes of administrative shuffling—time I didn't actually have. Time blocking demands a predictable environment, and if your job involves putting out fires, it can quickly turn into a source of stress rather than relief.

## What Is the Autofocus Method (And Why It Feels Uncomfortably Loose)

Invented by productivity writer Mark Forster, Autofocus is the exact opposite of time blocking. There are no calendars, no color-coded blocks, and no rigid schedules. Instead, you write down a running, master list of every single task you need to do—work projects, chores, admin tasks, and long-term ideas—all on a single legal pad or digital document.

To work through the list, you follow a counter-intuitive set of rules:
* Read through the list from top to bottom without acting on anything.
* Stop reading the moment a task catches your eye or feels appealing.
* Work on that task for as long as you want to do it.
* When you are finished (or bored), cross it off or rewrite it lower down on the list, and start reading from the top again.

When I first tried Autofocus, my brain rebelled. Without a deadline or a calendar invite staring me down, I felt like a college student procrastinating on a term paper. How could I trust myself to just pick *whatever* felt right? 

Yet, something fascinating happened by day three. Because I was only working on tasks that my brain had subconsciously flagged as interesting or urgent, my momentum skyrocketed. I flew through a tedious expense report in ten minutes simply because my eyes landed on it and I decided to get it out of the way. 

## Head-to-Head: How They Handle Real Workday Disruptions

To figure out which system deserves a permanent spot in your workflow, you have to look at how each one reacts when a coworker dumps an unexpected priority on your desk at 2:00 PM.

Under a **Time Blocking** regime, a disruption is a crisis. You have to actively break your own system. You must look at the calendar, decide which block to sacrifice, drag the boundaries, and mentally pivot. If you are prone to perfectionism, this friction often causes you to abandon the system entirely until next Monday.

Under the **Autofocus** regime, a disruption is a non-event. The new task simply gets scribbled at the bottom of your master list. When you finish your current task, you scan the page. If the new fire is the most compelling thing staring back at you, you tackle it next. If it isn't, you let it sit lower down until you have the bandwidth. The list absorbs the chaos without breaking.

## Which System Should You Choose?

Neither of these methods is universally superior, but they serve entirely different work styles and job descriptions. 

Choose Time Blocking if your calendar is largely under your own control. If you are an independent contributor, a researcher, or someone who can turn off their chat applications for hours at a time, time blocking gives you the strict boundaries needed to produce deep, high-value work. It prevents Parkinson’s Law—the idea that work expands to fill the time available for its completion.

Choose the Autofocus Method if your workday is dictated by other people. If you work in customer support, management, or a fast-paced agency environment where reactive tasks dominate, trying to force your day into rigid time blocks is a recipe for burnout. Autofocus gives you the flexibility to move fast, drop what isn't working, and maintain a sense of agency even when your inbox is exploding.

If you are trying to decide your next steps for overhauling your daily routine, consider running a two-day experiment with each approach to see which one leaves you with more mental energy at five o'clock.
