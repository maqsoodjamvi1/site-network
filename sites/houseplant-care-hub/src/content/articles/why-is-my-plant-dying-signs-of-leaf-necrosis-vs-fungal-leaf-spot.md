Panic usually sets in the second you notice a brown, dying patch on your favorite houseplant. I have been there too many times, staring at a Monstera or a Pothos, wondering if I am underwatering it, overwatering it, or if some creeping plant disease has moved in overnight. 

When houseplant leaves start turning brown, black, or crisp, plant parents usually jump to one conclusion: root rot. But brown patches can mean a couple of very different things. 

To save your plant, you first need to diagnose the exact problem. You are likely looking at either non-living tissue damage—known as leaf necrosis—or a living pathogen called fungal leaf spot. They look similar at a glance, but treating the wrong one will kill your plant faster than the original issue.

## What Is Leaf Necrosis? (The Environmental Injury)

Leaf necrosis sounds terrifying, but it is just a botanical term for dead plant tissue. When a part of a leaf suffers from lack of water, too much direct sun, chemical burn from tap water, or physical damage, the cells in that area die. Once plant tissue undergoes necrosis, it will never turn green again. 

The most common cause of necrosis I see in indoor plants is inconsistent watering. When you let a Calathea dry out completely and then flood it, the sudden shift shocks the root system. The roots cannot push water up to the furthest edges of the leaves fast enough, so the tips and margins dry out and die. 

You will also see necrosis from fertilizer burn. If you dump liquid fertilizer into dry soil, the high salt concentration literally dehydrates the roots, causing sudden, crisp brown patches to form across the center of the leaves. 

## Spotting Fungal Leaf Spot (The Active Infection)

Fungal leaf spot is an entirely different beast. Instead of a purely physical reaction to your care routine, this is caused by microscopic fungal spores that land on wet leaves and begin to feed on the plant tissue. 

Unlike the neat, dry brown tips caused by dry air, fungal spots usually look a bit more sinister. 

*   **The Target Look:** They often form distinct, circular spots rather than sweeping brown edges.
*   **The Halo Effect:** Look closely at the brown or black spot. Is there a faint yellow ring—or halo—around it? That halo is a classic sign of a living infection spreading through the leaf cells.
*   **Rapid Spreading:** While necrosis stays put or slowly creeps over weeks, a fungal spot can multiply and merge with other spots in a matter of days if conditions are right.

Fungi thrive in stagnant, humid air and wet foliage. If you are in the habit of misting your houseplants every morning to boost humidity, you might accidentally be running a breeding ground for fungal spores. 

## The Easy At-Home Test: Location and Texture

When you are standing over your struggling plant, use these practical steps to tell necrosis and fungus apart:

1.  **Check the location on the leaf.** Necrosis almost always starts at the very tips or outer edges of the leaves because those are the hardest places for the plant to push moisture. Fungal spots can appear anywhere—right in the middle of the leaf blade where water droplets tend to pool.
2.  **Feel the texture.** Necrotic tissue is usually very dry, papery, and crisp. Fungal spots can sometimes feel soggy, water-soaked, or gummy in the early stages before they dry out.
3.  **Look at your watering and misting habits.** Did you forget to water for three weeks? It is necrosis. Do you keep your plants crowded together in a low-airflow corner and mist them daily? It leans heavily toward fungus.

## How to Handle Leaf Necrosis

If your diagnosis points to necrosis, your plant is telling you to adjust your environment. You cannot fix the brown patches, so you can use sharp, sterilized scissors to snip off the dead tips for aesthetic reasons, or leave them be. 

To stop new necrosis from forming, adjust your routine. Switch to filtered water if your tap water is heavy in minerals like fluoride and chlorine, which build up in the soil and cause tip burn. Invest in a moisture meter so you stop guessing when your plants actually need a drink. 

## How to Stop Fungal Leaf Spot

If you have a fungal issue, you need to act quickly to keep it from jumping to your other houseplants. 

First, isolate the infected plant immediately. Move it away from your indoor jungle. Next, prune off the heavily infected leaves entirely—do not just cut the spots off, remove the whole leaf and throw it in the trash, not the compost bin. 

Fix the environment that caused the fungus in the first place. Stop misting your plants; use a tabletop humidifier instead so the moisture goes into the air, not onto the leaves. Turn on a small oscillating fan in your plant room to keep the air moving, which dries up stray moisture before spores can germinate. If the infection is widespread, treat the plant with a copper-based fungicide or a DIY neem oil spray according to the bottle instructions.

Distinguishing between a plant throwing a tantrum over dry air and a plant battling an actual disease takes a little practice, but checking the spot location and texture will point you in the right direction. Evaluating your watering habits, airflow, and leaf wetness gives you a clear path forward to nursing your indoor garden back to full health.
