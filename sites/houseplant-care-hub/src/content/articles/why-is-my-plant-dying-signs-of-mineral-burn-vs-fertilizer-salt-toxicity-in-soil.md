Have you ever noticed a crisp, brown crust forming around the rim of your favorite terracotta pot, accompanied by leaf tips that look like they've been scorched by a match? You probably assumed you were either underwatering or overfertilizing. 

I made this exact mistake with my prized monstera last year. I kept dumping liquid fertilizer into the soil every couple of weeks, figuring more food meant faster growth. Instead, the plant started stalling, and ugly white and yellow crusts appeared on the soil surface. 

When houseplant leaves start turning brown and crispy, the culprit is usually chemical buildup in the potting mix. But there is a distinct difference between mineral burn from your tap water and fertilizer salt toxicity from plant food. Figuring out which one is plaguing your indoor garden is the key to saving your plants before the damage becomes fatal.

## What Is Mineral Burn and Where Does It Come From?

Mineral burn happens when dissolved solids—primarily calcium, magnesium, sodium, and chlorine—accumulate in your potting soil over time. These minerals come directly from your tap water. 

Every time you water your houseplants, the soil retains a small fraction of these elements. As the moisture evaporates, the minerals are left behind. In nature, heavy rain flushes these salts deep down past the root zone and out into the wider earth. In a potted plant, that water has nowhere to go unless it drains entirely out of the bottom hole. 

The most common signs of mineral burn include:
*   A chalky, crusty white or yellowish buildup forming on the top of the soil.
*   A similar crust creeping up the outside of unglazed terracotta pots as the water evaporates through the clay walls.
*   Lower leaves developing brown, crispy tips that slowly spread inward toward the center of the leaf.
*   Sudden dropping of older leaves while new growth looks surprisingly green and healthy.

If your municipal water supply is hard (meaning it has high mineral content), your houseplants are experiencing a slow buildup every single week. Over six months to a year, that concentration reaches toxic levels in the root zone, effectively burning the delicate root tips and stopping the plant from taking up water.

## How Fertilizer Salt Toxicity Differs

Fertilizer salt toxicity looks remarkably similar to mineral burn at first glance, but the root cause is entirely different. Commercial houseplant fertilizers are actually mineral salts. When you apply them, you are feeding your plant, but you are also adding a concentrated dose of salts.

If you fertilize too frequently, use too much strength, or forget to water your plant thoroughly before applying liquid plant food, those salts accumulate quickly. 

The classic sign of fertilizer salt toxicity is a fluffy, crystalline crust on the soil surface that looks almost like cotton candy or fine sea salt. Unlike the hard, chalky mineral rings left by tap water, heavy fertilizer salt can sometimes pull moisture *out* of the roots through osmosis. This is what botanists call "fertilizer burn." 

When this happens, your plant might look like it is severely drought-stressed even though the soil is soaking wet. The roots have been chemically burned and can no longer drink, leading to rapid wilting, sudden leaf drop, and black, mushy root tips if you pull the plant out of its pot to inspect it.

## Step-by-Step Guide to Flushing Your Soil

Fortunately, you do not need to repot your plants every time salt or mineral buildup occurs. Unless the potting mix has completely broken down into heavy muck, you can fix both mineral burn and fertilizer salt toxicity with a thorough soil flush. 

Take your plant to the kitchen sink, bathroom, or take it outside if the weather is warm. Pour a large volume of room-temperature water steadily over the soil—ideally two to three times the total volume of the pot. For example, if your plant is in a six-inch pot, slowly pour a gallon of water through the soil. 

Let the water drain completely out of the bottom holes. This process dissolves the accumulated mineral crusts and fertilizer salts, washing them right out of the drainage holes. Wait an hour, and repeat the process one more time to catch any residual buildup. 

## Preventing Future Buildup

Once you have cleared out the old buildup, change your routine slightly to keep it from returning. If your tap water is very hard, switch to using distilled, reverse-osmosis, or collected rainwater for your sensitive houseplants like calatheas, ferns, and marantas. 

When fertilizing, always dilute your plant food to half the recommended strength, and remember the golden rule: never fertilize dry soil. Always water your plant normally a day before you feed it so the roots are plump and protected from chemical shock.
