It happens almost overnight. You walk past your favorite houseplant, and suddenly, a leaf looks like it spent the afternoon under a magnifying glass. 

When your indoor greenery starts showing crispy, discolored patches, the first instinct is to blame the sun. But as I learned the hard way after frying my favorite fiddle leaf fig by a south-facing window, not all light damage is created equal. 

There is a distinct difference between sunburn and leaf scorch. Diagnosing the exact issue correctly is the only way to save the rest of your plant from meeting the same crispy fate. Here is how I learned to spot the difference, figure out the root cause, and stop the damage before it spreads.

## Sunburn: The Sudden Shock

Sunburn is the plant equivalent of falling asleep on the beach without sunscreen on the first hot day of summer. It happens quickly, usually within a few hours, and it catches you completely off guard.

This typically happens when you move a plant abruptly. For instance, if you take a low-light houseplant like a ZZ plant or a Calathea out of a dim corner and set it right on a blazing south-facing windowsill, it will burn almost immediately. The plant’s cellular structure simply cannot handle the sudden, intense spike in ultraviolet radiation.

**What sunburn looks like:**
*   Bleached, whitish, or pale yellow patches that appear almost translucent.
*   Damage concentrated entirely on the side of the plant facing the light source or windowpane.
*   Crispy, dry spots that show up on the flat surface of the leaves, not just the tips.
*   Damage that appears rapidly—sometimes overnight after a particularly sunny afternoon.

If you notice these pale, bleached spots, the plant is telling you it was rushed into the spotlight too fast. 

## Leaf Scorch: The Slow Burn

Leaf scorch, on the other hand, is a chronic condition. While sunburn is caused by too much light intensity all at once, scorch is usually the result of heat buildup, dry air, or root stress over a period of weeks. 

I see leaf scorch most often in winter when running indoor heating systems. The air gets bone-dry, and if a plant sits right next to a radiator, or directly touches a hot, double-paned window baking in the afternoon sun, the leaves literally cook. The plant loses water faster through its leaves than its roots can suck it up from the soil.

**What leaf scorch looks like:**
*   Dark brown or black crispy tips and margins (edges) that look burnt around the edges.
*   Curling leaves that feel brittle and papery to the touch.
*   Damage that creeps in slowly, starting at the very tip of the leaf and working its way down toward the stem.
*   Brown patches that can appear on plants that aren't even getting direct light, simply because the ambient air is too hot and dry near a heat vent.

## How to Tell Which One You’re Dealing With

When you are staring at a damaged houseplant in your living room, ask yourself two questions: *How fast did this happen,* and *where is the damage located?*

If the damage is a white, bleached-out patch right in the middle of a top leaf after you rearranged your plant shelf yesterday, you are looking at sunburn. The light was simply too harsh, too fast.

If the damage is a dark brown, crispy fringe creeping along the edges of every leaf after months of stable growth, you are looking at leaf scorch. This usually points to environmental stress, such as chronic underwatering, root rot choking off water supply, or a nearby heat vent blasting dry air.

## Simple Steps to Fix and Prevent Light Damage

Once a leaf is burned or scorched, it will never turn green again. Plants do not possess the cellular ability to heal dead, crispy tissue. However, you can stop the damage from claiming the rest of the plant.

First, use clean scissors to snip off severely damaged leaves, or carefully trim away just the dead brown tips, cutting at a natural angle to keep the plant looking tidy. 

Next, adjust the environment based on your diagnosis. If you caused a sunburn, move the plant back to its original spot immediately, and if you want it to live in a brighter location, introduce it to the new light gradually over the course of three to four weeks, moving it a foot closer to the window every few days. 

If you are dealing with leaf scorch, check your watering routine and move the plant away from drafty windows, air conditioners, and heating vents. Increasing the humidity around tropical houseplants using a pebble tray or a small room humidifier can also stop the edges from crisping further. 

Evaluating your plant's specific symptoms and making these simple environmental adjustments now will help your indoor garden adjust smoothly to changing seasons.
