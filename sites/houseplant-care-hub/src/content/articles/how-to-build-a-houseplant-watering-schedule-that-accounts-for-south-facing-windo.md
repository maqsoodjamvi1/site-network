If you have ever placed a gorgeous fiddle-leaf fig or a trailing pothos in front of a bright south-facing window, only to watch it crisp up and suffer by mid-July, you already know that summer window glare is no joke. Down here in the Northern Hemisphere, south-facing windows act like magnifying glasses for the sun, especially between June and August. 

When people ask me how often they should water plants in these prime sun-drenched spots, my answer always surprises them: I tell them to throw their calendar away. A rigid seven-day watering routine does not work when your living room transforms into a greenhouse every afternoon. Here is how I build a practical, adaptable watering schedule that actually keeps high-light indoor plants alive through the most intense months of the year.

## Why South-Facing Windows Demand a Different Strategy

Let’s look at the physics of what happens to a plant sitting two feet away from a southern exposure in the middle of summer. The light is direct, harsh, and lasts for hours. This triggers rapid photosynthesis, which in turn causes the plant to drink water faster through its roots and transpire it rapidly through its leaves. 

At the same time, that same intense heat bakes the potting mix. Soil in a plastic or ceramic pot sitting in a south-facing window can dry out completely in a matter of days—sometimes even hours—while a plant of the exact same species on a north-facing shelf stays damp for a week or more. Sticking to a fixed schedule means you are either drowning your plant during a cloudy week or dehydrating it during a heatwave. 

## Ditch the Calendar and Adopt the "Weight and See" Method

Instead of asking *what day of the week* it is, you need to start asking *what state the soil is in*. The absolute best tool for checking this is not a plastic moisture meter from the garden center; it is your own hands. 

For small to medium pots, pick the plant up. You will quickly learn the difference in weight between a pot filled with saturated, heavy soil and one that is parched and bone-dry. For larger floor plants that you cannot easily lift, use your finger. Push it a full two to three inches down into the soil. If it feels cool and damp at your knuckle, leave the watering can alone. If it feels completely dry and warm to the touch, it is time for a drink.

## Implement a Summer Soaking Routine

When plants in south-facing windows finally do need water, giving them a casual splash from a cup usually causes more harm than good. The top layer gets wet, but the rapid evaporation and intense heat mean the water never reaches the root ball sitting at the bottom of the pot.

During the peak of summer, I practice thorough, deep saturation. Take your plants to the sink or use a watering can indoors until water runs freely out of the drainage holes. This ensures the entire root system gets hydrated. 

*   **Drainage is non-negotiable:** Never let your pots sit in standing water in a saucer for more than fifteen minutes after a heavy summer watering. The combination of intense window heat and soggy bottom soil is a fast track to root rot.
*   **Time your watering wisely:** Water your sun-blasted plants early in the morning. This gives the roots time to absorb the moisture before the fierce afternoon sun hits the glass. If you water in the evening, moisture can linger too long in the warmth, inviting fungal issues.

## Protect the Foliage from Actual Burns

Intense summer glare does not just dry out soil; it can literally fry leaves. If your windows lack UV-filtering films or double-paned glass designed to block harsh rays, water droplets left sitting on leaves can act as miniature lenses, burning brown spots right into the foliage. 

If you notice your plants getting bleached, faded, or developing crisp brown patches on the side facing the glass, you have a few options. You can install sheer white curtains to diffuse the harshest afternoon rays without cutting off too much light, scoot the plants a few feet back from the sill, or simply turn the nursery pot a quarter-turn every time you water to ensure even exposure.

Adjusting your approach to match the intensity of the season takes a little bit of observation upfront, but once you start reading the soil rather than the calendar, your plants will reward you with steady, healthy growth all summer long.
