Drooping leaves, brown tips, and sudden leaf drop are the universal heartbreak of the indoor jungle. When a houseplant starts turning crispy and sad, most of us immediately reach for the watering can or run to the store for a bottle of liquid plant food. Ironically, that extra dose of fertilizer might actually be what pushed your green friend over the edge. 

In my years of caring for everything from dramatic fiddle-leaf figs to unkillable pothos, I have learned that "plant sickness" often boils down to a case of fertilizer trouble. But there is a big difference between dumping too much plant food into the pot all at once and the slow, crusty accumulation of minerals over time. Figuring out which one you are dealing with is the only way to save your plant before it gives up entirely.

## What Fertilizer Burn Actually Looks Like

Fertilizer burn happens fast. It is essentially chemical shock. When you pour a heavy dose of liquid fertilizer into dry soil, or use double the recommended strength because you want your monstera to grow faster, you create a high-salt environment. Because of how osmosis works, the concentrated salts in the soil actually suck moisture *out* of the plant's roots instead of letting water in. 

The signs of acute fertilizer burn usually show up within a few days to a week after feeding. You will notice crisp, dark brown tips and edges on the leaves that look scorched, almost as if someone touched them with a hot iron. Sometimes, you will see yellow halos separating the brown dead tissue from the still-green parts of the leaf. In severe cases, lower leaves will drop while they are still mostly green because the root system has been severely compromised. 

If your plant was thriving, you gave it a heavy feeding, and a week later it looks like it spent a day in the oven, you are dealing with classic fertilizer burn.

## Identifying Chemical Salt Buildup

Chemical salt buildup, on the other hand, is a slow burn. You won't see symptoms a week after feeding; instead, your plant might look fine for months, and then slowly stall out, lose its vigor, and develop strange leaf issues as the seasons change.

Tap water contains minerals, and almost all synthetic fertilizers are made of mineral salts. Every time you water your plant, some of those minerals get left behind. As the water evaporates, the salts move upward and accumulate in the potting mix and around the edges of the pot. 

Here is what chemical salt buildup looks like in the real world:
* A crusty, white or yellowish powdery ring forming around the rim of a terracotta pot.
* A crusty white layer sitting directly on the surface of the soil, looking almost like mold or fine sea salt.
* Lower leaves turning yellow and dropping off while new growth comes in tiny and stunted.
* Roots circling the bottom of the nursery pot turning brown, brittle, and hollow instead of firm and white.
* Water running straight through the pot instantly during watering, because the soil has become hydrophobic (repelling water due to mineral compaction).

While fertilizer burn is caused by a sudden spike in chemical concentration, salt buildup is the cumulative effect of months of regular watering and feeding without a proper flush.

## How to Fix Fertilizer Burn

If you suspect you have given your plant chemical burn, you need to act quickly to remove the excess fertilizer from the root zone. Stop all feeding immediately. Do not give that plant any more plant food for at least two to three months.

Next, you need to leach the soil. Take your plant to the sink, shower, or take it outside with a hose. Run lukewarm water gently through the potting mix for a solid three to five minutes. Let the water pour freely out of the drainage holes. This process dissolves the excess chemical salts and washes them out the bottom of the pot. 

If the burn is severe and the plant is continuing to decline, don't be afraid to repot it. Gently knock the old soil off the root ball, rinse the roots in clean water, trim away any mushy or completely dead roots with sterilized shears, and replant it in fresh, nutrient-free potting mix. 

## How to Prevent Future Damage

Managing nutrients is all about consistency and moderation. The golden rule I follow in my own indoor garden is simple: dilute and flush. 

Always dilute liquid fertilizers to half or even a quarter of the strength recommended on the bottle, especially during the darker winter months when plants are resting. It is much safer to feed a plant a weak solution every time you water than to hit it with a massive dose once a month. Additionally, make it a habit to thoroughly flush your pots every few months by running a heavy volume of plain water through the soil to wash away any lingering mineral deposits before they can turn into a crusty problem.

Taking a closer look at your potting soil and root health can save you from playing the guessing game with your indoor collection. Evaluating your watering habits and keeping an eye out for mineral crusts will help you keep your plants thriving all year round.
