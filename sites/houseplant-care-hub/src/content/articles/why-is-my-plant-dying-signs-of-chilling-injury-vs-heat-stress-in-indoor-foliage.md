Indoor plants can't talk, but when something in your home environment goes wrong, they find plenty of ways to complain. If you are staring at a drooping pothos or a calathea with crispy edges, it is easy to assume you are simply underwatering. However, temperature extremes often mimic the exact same symptoms as drought or root rot. 

Figuring out whether your foliage is suffering from chilling injury or heat stress can save you from making the problem worse with the wrong adjustments. When my own indoor jungle starts looking distressed, I always check the thermometer and the room's airflow before I ever reach for the watering can. Here is how to tell the difference between hot and cold damage, and what you can actually do to fix it.

## The Cold Truth: Recognizing Chilling Injury

Tropical houseplants are native to warm, humid environments, which means most of them hate temperatures below 55 degrees Fahrenheit. Chilling injury happens when a plant gets too cold—often without actually freezing. Unlike outright frost damage, which turns leaves into mush, chilling injury is sneakier. 

The most common culprit in US homes is a drafty window during winter, or carrying a newly purchased plant home from the nursery in the dead of winter without a proper plastic sleeve. 

Symptoms of chilling injury usually show up a day or two after the cold event:
*   **Water-soaked spots:** Leaves develop translucent patches that look bruised, eventually turning dark brown or black.
*   **Sudden drop:** Plants like ficus benjamina (weeping figs) will throw a dramatic temper tantrum and drop green leaves by the handful if a cold winter draft hits them.
*   **Stem collapse:** The main stems or petioles may suddenly go limp, even if the soil is adequately moist.

If you suspect cold damage, the best step you can take today is to move the plant away from exterior doors, drafty windows, and uninsulated mudrooms. Do not prune away the damaged leaves immediately; leave them on the plant until new growth emerges, as they still provide some energy to the stem.

## The Heat Wave: Identifying Heat Stress

On the flip side, indoor foliage can easily overheat. This is surprisingly common even in winter if your plants sit directly above a forced-air heating vent, right next to a radiator, or baked behind a south-facing window with no sheer curtain to diffuse the afternoon sun. 

Heat stress pushes a plant's metabolism into overdrive. It drinks water faster than its roots can pull it up from the soil, leading to a strange paradox: the plant looks dehydrated even when the dirt feels damp.

Typical signs of heat stress include:
*   **Crispy, bleached tips:** The edges of the leaves turn brown and dry, while the centers may look bleached out or faded compared to their usual deep green.
*   **Cupping and curling:** Leaves will curl inward like tacos to minimize their surface area and reduce moisture loss through evaporation.
*   **Rapid soil dry-down:** If you find yourself having to water a previously low-maintenance plant every single day, heat is almost certainly the culprit.

To reverse heat stress, relocate your plant away from direct heat sources and appliances. If moving it isn't an option because of lighting constraints, try placing a sheer curtain over the window to block the intense thermal radiation, or move the plant a few feet back into the room.

## The Diagnostic Checklist: Is It Hot or Cold?

Because both heat and cold cause leaves to drop, turn brown, or wilt, you need to look at the immediate environment to diagnose the issue correctly. Walk over to the struggling plant right now and ask yourself these three practical questions:

*   **Where is the air blowing?** If the plant sits directly in the path of an air conditioner vent, space heater, or ceiling fan, it is experiencing temperature stress. Move it out of the airflow zone immediately.
*   **What season is it near the glass?** Single-pane windows get freezing cold in winter and blistering hot in summer. If your plant is pressed right against the glass, it is experiencing extreme temperature swings that day-to-day room thermostats don't register.
*   **How does the soil feel?** If the soil is bone dry and the leaves are crispy, you are likely looking at heat stress combined with underwatering. If the soil is wet, yet the plant is dropping leaves or showing mushy spots, suspect a chilling injury or root shock from cold water.

Fixing these issues is rarely about buying more products or expensive plant food; it is simply about micro-climate management. Finding that sweet spot of steady, moderate room temperature will help your indoor foliage bounce back and put out fresh, healthy growth. 

As you evaluate your houseplants and start shifting them to safer spots away from drafts and heat vents, taking a closer look at your home’s overall humidity and seasonal micro-climates can make all the difference in keeping your indoor garden thriving year-round.
