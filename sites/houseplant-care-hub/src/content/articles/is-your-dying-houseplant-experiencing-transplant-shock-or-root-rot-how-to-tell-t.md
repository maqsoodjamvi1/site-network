There is nothing quite like the sinking feeling of watching a prized houseplant suddenly turn on you. One week it is putting out vibrant new leaves, and the next, it is dropping foliage by the dozen or looking permanently limp, no matter how much you fuss over it. 

When a plant hits this downward spiral, the panic sets in. Did I overwater it, or is it just reacting to that repotting job I did last weekend? 

Figuring out whether your plant is suffering from transplant shock or root rot is the key to saving it. Treat root rot like transplant shock, and your plant will turn to mush. Treat transplant shock with heavy watering, and you will drown a root system that is already struggling. Here is how I diagnose what is actually happening in the pot so you can take the right corrective action.

## The Timeline and Trigger Test

The easiest way to start narrowing down the culprit is to look at your calendar. Plants rarely develop advanced root rot overnight unless they have been sitting in a swamp for a month, but transplant shock operates on a very specific, immediate timeline.

If your plant started looking miserable within 24 to 72 hours of you messing with its roots—whether that meant potting it up into a larger container, dividing a root ball, or aggressively stripping away old soil—you are almost certainly dealing with transplant shock. The plant is throwing a tantrum because its root hairs were damaged, stressed, or exposed to dry air during the transition. 

On the other hand, if you haven’t touched the pot in six months, but the lower leaves are turning yellow, dropping off, or the stems are getting squishy near the soil line, root rot is the likely diagnosis. Rot is a creeping condition caused by anaerobic bacteria or fungi thriving in permanently soggy soil, usually brought on by a pot that lacks drainage holes or a watering habit that leaves the bottom of the soil profile wet indefinitely.

## Inspecting the Leaves and Stems

Take a close look at how the foliage is failing. Transplant shock and root rot look a bit different above the soil line once you know what to look for.

With transplant shock, the plant usually wilts dramatically, almost as if you forgot to water it for a month. You might also see sudden leaf drop, particularly on older leaves, or crisping edges on newly unfurled foliage. The plant simply cannot pull enough water up through its temporarily compromised root system to support its current mass of leaves and stems. However, the stems themselves usually remain firm and upright at the base.

Root rot tells a different story. The wilting from root rot often happens even when the soil is soaking wet, which confuses many beginners who water it more because they think it is thirsty. The stems of a root-rotting plant often develop a watery, translucent, or dark brown to black lesion right at the soil line. If you gently wiggle the base of the plant, a rotted plant will often wobble loosely in the pot like a loose tooth because the anchoring roots have completely disintegrated.

## Getting Down to the Roots

The ultimate truth serum requires getting your hands dirty. Gently tip the plant out of its nursery pot and brush away enough soil to inspect the underground system. You do not need to wash every grain of dirt away—just enough to see what is happening beneath the surface.

Healthy roots are firm, plump, and generally light in color (tan, white, or pale yellow, depending on the species). 

*   **If it's transplant shock:** The root system will still look mostly healthy and firm. You might notice some broken root tips from where they were handled during repotting, but the overall mass of the root ball is intact and free of foul odors.
*   **If it's root rot:** The roots will be dark brown, gray, or black. When you touch them, they will feel mushy, stringy, or hollow—the outer sheath will literally slide right off the inner core, leaving behind a thin, thread-like string. A classic, unmistakable indicator of rot is a sour, swampy, or decaying odor coming from the root ball.

## How to Respond and Recover

Once you have identified the issue, your recovery strategy needs to match the diagnosis. 

If you confirm transplant shock, stop fussing. Do not fertilize a shocked plant, as chemical salts will burn already stressed roots. Move the plant out of harsh, direct sunlight into bright, indirect light to reduce the plant's energy demands. Keep the soil consistently, lightly moist—not soggy—and leave it alone. Many growers swear by placing a clear plastic bag loosely over the canopy for a few days to create a mini-greenhouse that locks in humidity, helping the foliage stay hydrated while the roots regenerate.

If you find root rot, immediate surgery is required. Use a pair of clean, sterilized shears to ruthlessly snip away every single mushy, brown, or rotting root until you are left only with firm, healthy tissue. Do not try to save questionable roots; rot spreads rapidly if left behind. Wash the remaining healthy roots gently under lukewarm water to remove lingering fungus spores, rinse the pot thoroughly with soap and hot water (or grab a fresh pot), and repot the plant in a fresh, fast-draining soil mix amended heavily with perlite or orchid bark. 

Deciding how to proceed next depends on how much root system your plant has left after pruning away the damage, and whether you need specialized recovery supplies or a fresh start with a new cutting.
