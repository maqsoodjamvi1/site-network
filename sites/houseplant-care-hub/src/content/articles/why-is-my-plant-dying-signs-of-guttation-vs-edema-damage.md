Panic usually sets in the moment you notice something dripping from your favorite houseplant. You might wipe away the moisture, assume it is just sweating, and then wake up the next morning to find ugly, corky brown patches ruining the leaves. 

When my collection of indoor plants first started showing these mysterious wet spots and crusty bumps, I assumed it was a pest invasion or a fungal disease. I spent weeks treating for spider mites and swapping fungicides before I figured out what was actually happening. The culprit was almost always a watering routine that was out of step with my home's indoor humidity.

If your plant is leaking water or developing weird bumps, you are likely looking at one of two completely different physiological responses: guttation or edema. Learning how to tell them apart will save you from guessing at treatments and help you fix the root cause before real damage sets in.

## What is Guttation and Should You Panic?

Guttation looks alarming, but in most cases, it is completely normal. It is simply the process of a plant pushing out liquid water through specialized microscopic pores along the edges of its leaves, known as hydathodes. 

This usually happens at night. When the soil is damp and the humidity in your room is high, the roots keep absorbing water because root pressure builds up. However, the plant's stomata (the breathing pores) are closed at night, so the plant cannot release that moisture as vapor through transpiration. Instead, it forces the excess water out physically. 

You will typically spot guttation as tiny, crystal-clear droplets forming precisely along the tips and jagged edges of leaves on plants like Monsteras, Alocasias, and peace lilies. 

Here is a practical test to confirm it is guttation: touch the droplet with a clean tissue. If it feels like pure water, you are seeing guttation. Leave it alone. Do not wipe harsh chemical sprays on it. However, keep in mind that these droplets can sometimes contain minerals from your tap water. When the water evaporates, it can leave behind a white, powdery residue on the leaf margin that looks suspiciously like a pest issue, but it is just harmless mineral buildup.

## When Water Pressure Turns Destructive: Edema Damage

Edema is the ugly sibling of guttation. While guttation safely forces excess water out into the open air, edema happens when the plant absorbs water faster than it can transpire, and that pressure becomes trapped *inside* the plant tissue.

Instead of escaping as a droplet, the internal water pressure builds up so much that individual plant cells burst. As those cells rupture and die, they form water-soaked blisters. Over the next few days, those blisters dry out, cork over, and turn into permanent, unsightly brown, tan, or rust-colored scabs. 

Unlike the neat droplets of guttation, edema damage usually appears as:
* Raised, bumpy, cork-like patches on the undersides of leaves
* Ring-shaped scars or concentric circles
* Pitted, sunken areas that feel rough or papery to the touch

This frequently happens to thick-leaved houseplants like jade plants, peperomias, and string-of-pearls. If you overwater your jade plant while it is sitting in a cool, dimly lit room, the roots keep pumping water into the stems, but the dim light and high humidity mean the leaves cannot evaporate it fast enough. The internal cells literally pop from the pressure.

## How to Tell the Difference Quickly

When you are staring at a damp or damaged houseplant, run through this quick checklist to figure out what you are dealing with:

* **Location:** Guttation always happens right at the tips and margins of the leaves where the hydathodes live. Edema can pop up anywhere on the leaf surface, though it often starts on the underside.
* **Texture:** Guttation is wet liquid sitting on top of the leaf. Edema creates a permanent physical change in the leaf texture, turning smooth tissue into a bumpy, corky scab.
* **Timing:** Guttation is usually a temporary morning event after a heavy watering and a humid night. Edema damage is permanent and develops over a few days as ruptured cells die and dry out.

## Fixing Your Watering Habits to Stop the Damage

Neither guttation nor edema are diseases, meaning you cannot cure them with a spray bottle of fungicide or neem oil. They are purely mechanical symptoms of a mismatch between how much water your plant’s roots are taking in and how much moisture the surrounding air can absorb.

To get the situation under control, you need to adjust your care routine based on environmental triggers:

* **Let the soil dry deeper:** If you see either of these signs regularly, your soil is staying wet for too long. Stick your finger two inches into the potting mix before watering again. If it still feels cool or damp, wait another week.
* **Improve air circulation:** Stagnant air traps humidity right around the foliage, preventing transpiration. A small, low-speed oscillating room fan near your plant shelf can work wonders.
* **Adjust for the seasons:** Your houseplants need significantly less water in the dark winter months. If you keep watering on the same summer schedule when the days get shorter and cooler, edema is almost guaranteed to follow.

Once edema scars form, they will never heal or turn green again—the plant has permanently hardened that tissue off to protect itself. But identifying the root cause now will ensure your new leaves grow in healthy and smooth.
