When your teenager starts talking about getting their first part-time job, it is an exciting milestone. They are growing up, wanting a bit of independence, and figuring out the value of a dollar. But if you are navigating this as a separated or divorced parent, that innocent conversation about filling out an application at the local grocery store or fast-food joint can quickly trigger high anxiety. 

Suddenly, you are not just thinking about work permits and transport schedules; you are worrying about how to text your ex without reigniting a months-long feud over parenting boundaries. 

When my oldest kid decided to apply for a bagger position at our neighborhood supermarket, I stared at my blinking phone screen for twenty minutes trying to draft a three-sentence text. I wanted to avoid the usual traps: over-explaining, sounding too demanding, or leaving an opening for a sarcastic reply about my past scheduling mishaps. Through trial and error—and a few texts that definitely went sideways—I figured out a practical blueprint for sending this specific type of message.

## Keep the Focus Strictly on the Teen's Initiative

The biggest mistake I made early on was framing the job hunt around *our* logistics. A text reading, "Leo wants a job, so we need to figure out who is driving him on Tuesdays and Thursdays" immediately invites pushback. It makes the text about adult inconvenience rather than teenage growth.

Instead, shift the spotlight entirely onto your child's effort. Frame the text so that saying "no" or starting an argument makes your ex look unreasonable to the teenager. 

A better approach looks like this: "Hey Mark, Leo is applying for a part-time job at the grocery store down the street to save for a car. He has an interview scheduled for next Thursday at 4 PM, which falls during my time. However, it's right near your house. Would you be open to dropping him off so he can make it on time, or should we look at a rideshare?"

Notice what this does. It respects your ex’s time, provides a clear logistical solution, and centers the child’s ambition. It is very hard for a difficult co-parent to argue with a kid trying to earn their own money.

## Stick to the Facts and Leave Out the History

Old grievances love to sneak into co-parenting texts, especially when money or scheduling is involved. If your ex historically thinks teenagers shouldn't work during the school year, you might feel tempted to preemptively defend the decision. Don't. 

Leave out commentary like, "I know you think this is a bad idea, but..." or "Unlike last time, I've got this handled." 

*   State the company name and the exact role.
*   Note the date, time, and location of the interview.
*   Clearly state what input or action you need from them (if any).
*   Set a specific window for a response if scheduling requires it.

By stripping the message of emotional padding, you starve the potential argument of oxygen. If your ex responds with a tangent about how your teenager’s grades are going to slip, do not take the bait. Respond only to the logistical reality: "I hear your concern about grades, and we are keeping a close eye on study time. For now, the interview is set for Thursday. Let me know if you can handle the drop-off by Tuesday evening so we can confirm with the manager."

## Prepare for the Transportation Tug-of-War

Job interviews require punctuality, and in a two-household dynamic, that often means crossing state lines or neighborhood boundaries. This is where most co-parenting texts about jobs fall apart. 

If the interview lands on your ex's parenting day, do not demand that they cancel their plans to play chauffeur. Offer to handle the heavy lifting, or propose a neutral handoff. 

*   **If it's your day:** Ask if your ex can assist with the commute if their work schedule or location makes it significantly easier.
*   **If it's their day:** Ask if they are comfortable with your child using public transit, or offer to pick the teen up from the other parent's house to take them to the interview.
*   **If it falls during an exchange window:** Propose shifting the exchange time by an hour just for that specific week, rather than rewriting the entire custody schedule.

Keep your proposal binary when possible. Give them two reasonable choices rather than an open-ended question like "What should we do about this?" People who love to argue will drive a semi-truck right through an open-ended question.

## Knowing When to Step Back and Let Your Teen Handle the Rest

As teenagers step into the working world, they also need to step into a bit of adult communication. Once you have cleared the initial logistical hurdle with your ex—making sure everyone is on the same page and nobody is actively sabotaging the kid's ride—step back and let your teenager manage the details. 

If your ex starts texting *you* micro-questions about the job that the teenager should be answering, gently redirect them. "That's a great question for Maya—she's the one managing her work schedule now!" 

Setting up a first job interview is a major milestone, not just for your teenager learning how to navigate the professional world, but for you and your co-parent as you practice communicating about the future. By keeping your texts laser-focused on logistics, stripping out emotional history, and centering your child's hard work, you can get through this hurdle without starting a digital war.
