When your teenager is gearing up to take the SAT or ACT, the process can feel overwhelming enough. Add a co-parent you are no longer on great terms with into the mix, and simple logistics turn into a minefield. If your teen has a 504 plan or an Individualized Education Program (IEP) at school, securing test accommodations like extra time, a quiet room, or extended breaks requires a coordinated effort. 

The College Board and ACT, Inc. do not make this easy. Both organizations require documentation that often flows through the school, but parents have to sign off on consents, confirm deadlines, and sometimes split costs for private evaluations if the school’s paperwork falls short. If you and your ex are prone to arguing, a text about College Board SSD (Services for Students with Disabilities) codes can easily derail into a fight about past grievances or who is paying for what. 

Here is how I learned to draft a text message that gets the paperwork signed without reopening old wounds.

## Keep It Strictly Business and Future-Focused

When you text your ex about testing accommodations, treat them like a reluctant business partner. Drop all emotional language, pleasantries, and passive-aggressive jabs. Your goal is a yes, a signature, or a piece of information—nothing more. 

Avoid starting the text with "I hope you're doing well" if you both know you aren't. Instead, open with the specific task at hand. 

**Bad example:** "Hey, are you going to help me with Leo's accommodations for the SAT, or are you going to ignore this like you did his math tutor?"
**Good example:** "Hi [Ex's Name], Leo's counselor needs a parent signature on the ACT accommodations request form by Thursday so we don't miss the late registration deadline."

This approach anchors the conversation to a hard deadline and a neutral third party (the school counselor), which naturally discourages your ex from making the text about you.

## Use the "Information-First, Action-Last" Template

If your ex is defensive, they likely hate feeling ambushed by requests. To counter this, lay out the facts before you ask for anything. 

Break the text into three parts: the what, the why, and the exact action required. Here is a template you can adapt:

"Hi [Name], quick update on Leo’s SAT testing. His school submitted his IEP for 50% extended time, but the College Board requires a parent consent form uploaded to the SSD portal by [Date]. I downloaded the PDF and emailed it to you, or I can text you a screenshot of the signature page. Can you sign and text it back to me by Wednesday night so I can upload it?"

By telling them exactly what you already did (you tracked down the form, you know the deadline), you reduce the cognitive load for them. People are much less likely to start a fight when the heavy lifting is already done.

## Anticipate the Cost Conflict

Testing accommodations sometimes require fresh psychoeducational evaluations if the school’s documentation is more than three years old. If private testing is required, or if you are hiring an independent educational consultant to push the paperwork through, money will come up. 

If this text involves out-of-pocket expenses, do not negotiate prices over text. State the hard number, provide the invoice or school notice, and propose a split based on your custody agreement or past precedent. 

If your ex immediately texts back complaining about the cost, do not take the bait. Reply with: "I understand it’s an unexpected expense. If you want to review the school counselor's email stating why this evaluation is mandatory for his accommodations, I can forward it to you. Let's look at getting the consent form signed first since the deadline is in 48 hours." 

Keep pivoting back to the teenager's immediate timeline. It is hard for a co-parent to justify missing a College Board deadline out of spite when the school district is explicitly requiring it.

## Know When to Move to Email

Text messaging is great for quick check-ins and urgent deadlines, but it is terrible for multi-step administrative processes. If your ex starts asking a cascade of logistical questions about test centers, digital SAT formats, or documentation rules, use the text to transition platforms.

"It might be easier to look at all these requirements in one place. I'm going to email you the checklist from the school counselor with the links so we can both track what's submitted."

Moving to email creates a paper trail that feels less immediate and frantic than a buzzing phone, which often lowers the temperature on high-conflict dynamics. 

Getting your teen through high school testing is a marathon, and managing a co-parent while doing it requires strict boundaries. By keeping your texts brief, factual, and anchored to external deadlines, you protect your energy and keep the focus entirely where it belongs: on your teenager's success. 

As you navigate the next steps of registration, deadlines, and score reporting, having a reliable system to keep your co-parenting communications organized can make all the difference in keeping the process smooth for your teen.
