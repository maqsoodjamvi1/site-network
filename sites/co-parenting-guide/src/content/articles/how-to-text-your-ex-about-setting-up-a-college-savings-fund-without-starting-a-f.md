When my co-parent and I finally sat down to figure out how we were going to pay for our oldest kid’s future college tuition, we realized we had a massive problem. We didn’t agree on what kind of account to open, who should control the monthly deposits, or even whether we should prioritize college savings over our own retirement. Bringing it up over text felt like walking through a minefield. 

If you are at the stage where you need to text your ex about setting up a college fund, you already know how fast a simple logistical question can spiral into an argument about past spending, old resentments, or who makes more money. 

The secret to texting about high-stakes financial topics without starting a fight is keeping the message entirely forward-looking, removing emotional language, and giving your ex a clear, low-pressure way to respond. Here is how I learned to draft those texts, along with a few exact examples you can adapt for your own situation.

## Why Texting About Money Goes Wrong (And How to Fix It)

Text messaging is terrible for nuance. When you read a text from an ex, your brain automatically fills in the missing tone of voice, usually assuming the worst. If you send a text that reads, "We need to talk about putting money away for Leo's college," your ex might read it as an accusation that they aren't contributing enough.

Before you touch your keyboard, strip out any words that assign blame or demand immediate agreement. Shift your framing from "What are we going to do?" to "Here is a specific idea I looked into." People find it much easier to react to a concrete proposal than an open-ended problem. 

## Step 1: Do Your Homework First

Never text your ex to ask *if* you should save for college. That invites a debate you might not be ready for. Instead, do the legwork beforehand so you are texting them a solution, not a chore. 

Decide which type of account makes the most sense in your state—such as a 529 plan—and figure out the basic contribution minimums. When you text your ex with specific numbers already crunched, it shows you respect their time. It also prevents the conversation from devolving into a vague back-and-forth about how expensive everything is nowadays.

Here is a practical text template you can use for this initial outreach:

> "Hey [Ex's Name], I’ve been looking into setting up a 529 college savings plan for Maya. I found a couple of options with no monthly maintenance fees and a $25 starting minimum. Do you have 10 minutes this week to look over the details together, or would you prefer I send over the link for you to check out on your own?"

Notice what this text does. It offers two easy paths forward: a quick chat or independent review. It also avoids demanding an instant decision.

## Step 2: Address the Control and Ownership Questions

The underlying fear in most co-parenting financial discussions is loss of control. Your ex might worry that if you open the account, you will make unilateral decisions about how the money gets invested, or that they won't have visibility into the balance.

Anticipate this anxiety in your second text message. Make it clear from the beginning that transparency is the priority. 

* Propose that whoever opens the account sets up automated quarterly statements to be forwarded to the other parent.
* Clarify upfront whether you view this as a 50/50 split or an arrangement proportionate to your current incomes.
* Keep the math simple so it doesn't look like you are trying to trap them in a lopsided obligation.

If you need to discuss how the monthly deposits will work, try framing it around budget predictability rather than fairness:

> "To keep things simple for both of our budgets, I was thinking we could each set up an automatic monthly transfer of $50 into the account starting next month. That way neither of us has to remember to manually send money. Let me know if that amount works for your current monthly expenses."

## Step 3: What to Do When They Don’t Respond (or Push Back)

Silence is common when co-parents see a text about money. If your ex leaves you on read, do not follow up twelve hours later with a frustrated question mark. Give them at least three to four business days to process. 

If they respond with resistance—such as saying they cannot afford to contribute right now—resist the urge to point out their spending habits on vacations or new gadgets. Instead, pivot to a scaled-back plan that keeps them on board without breaking their bank. 

> "I totally get that things are tight right now. What if we start with a token amount, like $15 a month just to get the account active, and we can revisit increasing it next year when your work schedule stabilizes?"

By lowering the barrier to entry, you keep the door open for cooperation without triggering a defensive argument.

Setting up your child's financial future doesn't have to become another battleground in your co-parenting journey. Taking the time to craft a low-pressure, solution-oriented message sets a collaborative tone for decisions to come. As you weigh your options for structuring these accounts and mapping out long-term expenses, having the right financial tools and resources in place can make the process significantly smoother.
