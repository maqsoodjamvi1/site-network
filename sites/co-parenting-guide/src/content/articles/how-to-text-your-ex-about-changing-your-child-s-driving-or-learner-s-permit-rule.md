When my teenager turned fifteen and started asking about getting a learner's permit, my stomach dropped for two reasons. The first was the terrifying thought of them behind the wheel of a two-ton vehicle. The second was texting my ex-husband to figure out how we were going to handle it. 

Divorce tends to put every milestone under a microscope, but teaching a kid to drive is a whole different level of high-stakes anxiety. If you are sitting on your couch right now staring at your phone, trying to draft a text about driver's education, insurance, or curfew rules without triggering a three-day argument, I get it. Over the last year, through plenty of trial and error, I have figured out a few tactical ways to handle these texts so you can protect your peace and keep the focus where it belongs: on your teenager's safety.

## Strip Out the History and Focus on the DMV

The biggest mistake I made early on was letting old baggage bleed into logistical texts. If your ex has a tendency to be controlling, or if you have lingering resentment about past financial issues, a text about driver's ed can easily morph into a debate about past grievances. 

Before you hit send, read your draft and ask yourself if a family court judge would find your message entirely necessary. Cut out any adjectives that judge your ex's parenting style. 

Instead of writing: "Since you never take safety seriously, we need to talk about driving rules," try something stripped-down and administrative. Treat the text like you are messaging a business partner. 

* *Draft:* "Hi Mark, Sam is eligible for a learner's permit next month. I want to make sure we are on the same page about the 50 required practice hours and who is adding them to the auto insurance. Let me know when you have ten minutes to map this out."

## Propose a Pre-Game Plan Instead of Making Demands

Co-parenting arguments usually start when one person feels backed into a corner or ambushed by a rule the other parent set unilaterally. If you decide that your kid is not allowed to drive on the interstate yet, do not text your ex: "I told Sam no highway driving." That is an instant invitation for a fight about who gets to make those calls.

Instead, frame your safety rules as a starting proposal that invites their input, even if you already know what needs to happen. 

* **Share the burden of the rules:** Tell your ex that you are feeling nervous about the transition and want to present a united front to your teenager. 
* **Focus on external authorities:** Use the state driving handbook or insurance requirements as your backup. It is much harder for an ex to argue with the state of California's graduated driver licensing laws than it is to argue with your personal opinion.

## Use a Bulleted List for Complex Logistics

Text messages are terrible for nuance, but they are great for checklists. When you are discussing the nitty-gritty of permit rules—like who pays for driver's education classes, who handles the DMV paperwork, and how many practice hours happen in whose car—do not dump it all into a massive, run-on paragraph. 

Break it down so your ex can reply to specific points without feeling overwhelmed. Here is an exact text structure that worked for us when dividing up the initial logistics:

* **Class:** I found a local driver's ed course for Sam starting July 10th. It costs $350. Can we split this 50/50?
* **Insurance:** We need to call our respective insurance companies to see what adding a permit driver costs before they get the actual license. 
* **The Car:** Let's agree that whoever is in the passenger seat is responsible for the phone being locked away in the glove box.

This format prevents the conversation from getting derailed. If they ignore the insurance question and only answer the cost of the class, you can easily text back: "Got it on the class. What did your insurance agent say about the permit?"

## Establish a "No Texting While Driving" Rule for Yourself

This sounds obvious, but it is worth mentioning because timing matters. Do not text your ex about driving rules while you are actually in the car with your teenager practicing turns in a empty parking lot. You need 100 percent of your mental energy focused on teaching your kid how to check their blind spots, not on your phone vibrating in the cup holder with a passive-aggressive response from your ex.

Set a boundary for yourself: handle driving logistics during your work day or during your child-free evenings. If your ex fires back a heated text while you are actively in the middle of a stressful driving lesson, mute the notification. You do not need to respond in real-time. A delayed, calm response is always better than a reactive, defensive one.

Taking the friction out of co-parenting around major milestones takes practice, and driving is often the first real test of how well you and your ex can coordinate logistics as your kids get older. By keeping your messages brief, objective, and focused strictly on state requirements and safety, you can usually bypass the emotional landmines entirely.
