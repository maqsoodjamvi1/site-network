Deciding to start a college fund or a long-term savings account for your kid is a great milestone. But when you are co-parenting with someone you no longer live with, even a positive step like saving money can feel like a minefield. Send the wrong text, and suddenly you are arguing about who controls the funds, which bank is better, or whether you are trying to look like the "better" parent. 

When I first wanted to open a high-yield savings account for my daughter, I stared at my phone for twenty minutes trying to phrase a single text message. I didn't want to sound demanding, but I also didn't want to leave room for a flat-out refusal. 

If you are gearing up to send that text, here is a practical, battle-tested approach to bringing up a child savings account without triggering an argument.

## Lead with the long-term goal, not the money

The biggest mistake you can make in that initial text is making it sound like an administrative demand or a financial obligation. If your co-parent thinks you are just trying to hit them up for more cash each month, defenses will go up immediately. 

Instead, frame the conversation entirely around your child’s future. Keep the focus narrow. You aren't asking them to sign away their financial freedom; you are asking them to collaborate on a milestone for their kid. 

When I texted my ex, I didn't mention monthly contributions at all in the first message. I just brought up the idea of setting aside cash gifts from grandparents and holidays so they wouldn't get frittered away on plastic toys. That made the idea feel useful and low-stakes.

## Keep the initial text short and closed-ended

Do not write a paragraph. Long texts give anxious or high-conflict co-parents too many points to nitpick, argue with, or ignore. You want a message that requires a simple, low-effort response.

Here is an example of what you can send:

"Hey [Name], I’d like to set up a dedicated savings account for [Child's Name] to put away birthday money and small cash gifts. Do you have a preference for which bank we use, or are you open to me setting one up at [Bank Name] and sharing the statements with you?"

Notice what this text does. It removes the mystery. It tells them *where* the money is going, *what* the money is for, and offers transparency right out of the gate by mentioning shared statements. It also gives them a sense of control by asking for their bank preference.

## Anticipate their hidden fears

If your ex pushes back, it is rarely actually about the bank account. Usually, resistance stems from a few specific underlying worries:
* **Control issues:** They are worried you will withdraw the money for personal use.
* **Affordability guilt:** They worry you are setting up an expectation for monthly contributions they cannot afford.
* **Control freak dynamics:** They just don't want you doing something independent that involves the family name.

You can disarm these fears before they even turn into a fight. If they reply asking, "How much am I supposed to put in?", immediately pivot and lower the bar. Reply with: "Zero pressure on monthly deposits. This is just a holding spot for cash gifts from family. I'm funding the baseline; you don't have to contribute a dime unless you want to." 

By removing the financial pressure, you take away their ammunition.

## Decide on the account structure beforehand

Before you text, know what you are actually asking for. There are a few ways to set these up, and having a plan makes you look organized rather than reactive:

* **A custodial account (UGMA/UTMA):** Legally belongs to the child, managed by an adult until they turn 18 or 21. 
* **A joint savings account:** Opened in your name and the child's name, or both parents' names. 
* **A separate personal savings account:** An account solely in your name that you mentally earmark for the child (least messy if your co-parent refuses to cooperate, but lacks transparency).

If your co-parent is difficult, option three might be your sanity-saver. You don't always need their legal signature to save money for your kid, even if you want their buy-in. If they put up a massive wall about opening a joint account, you can always fund a separate account on your own and let your actions speak for themselves.

## Moving forward with your co-parenting plan

Getting through the savings account text is usually the hardest part, but establishing this financial boundary sets a great precedent for future big-picture decisions. Once you get that green light—or even a neutral "fine, do whatever you want"—you can move on to the actual paperwork.
