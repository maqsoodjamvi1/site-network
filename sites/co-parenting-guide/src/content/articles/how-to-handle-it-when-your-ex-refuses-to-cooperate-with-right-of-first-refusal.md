Right of First Refusal sounds great on paper. The clause in your parenting agreement states that if one parent needs a babysitter for more than a few hours during their scheduled time, they have to offer the other parent the chance to step in before hiring a teenager down the street or calling a grandparent. 

When you signed off on it, you probably pictured extra time with your kids and a seamless teamwork vibe. But reality often looks very different. If your ex routinely ignores the rule, hires overnight sitters without telling you, or weaponizes the clause just to disrupt your personal life, co-parenting feels impossible. 

Here is how I learned to handle a co-parent who flat-out refuses to cooperate with the Right of First Refusal, and how you can protect your sanity and your custody rights without dragging yourself into court every single week.

## Figure Out If It’s Worth the Fight

Before you send another angry text, take a step back and look at the actual patterns. Is your ex violating the agreement because they genuinely need childcare for an unexpected work meeting, or are they doing it out of spite? More importantly, is the alternative care actually harmful to your kids? 

If your ex drops the kids off with a loving grandmother for three hours while they run errands, fighting this in court will likely make you look petty to a judge. Family court judges are drowning in high-conflict cases, and they generally do not want to micromanage minor schedule adjustments. 

However, if your ex is leaving your eight-year-old alone for entire weekends with a barely-legal babysitter while they go out of town, that is a genuine safety issue. Separate the minor annoyances from the real violations so you can focus your energy where it actually matters.

## Document Everything Without Being Obnoxious

You cannot prove a pattern of non-cooperation with vague accusations. If you want to address this effectively—either with your ex, a mediator, or a judge—you need a factual paper trail. 

Keep a dedicated log that includes:
* The date and time of the violation.
* How you found out about it (a text from your child, a slip of the tongue by your ex, or photos).
* What alternative care was used instead of offering the time to you.
* Whether you were available and willing to take the kids during that window.

Stop relying on phone calls to discuss schedule changes. If you talk on the phone, your ex can twist your words or claim you said something you didn't. Move your communication strictly to a parenting app or text message. When an issue arises, send a calm, direct message: "I noticed the kids were with a sitter last Tuesday from 2 PM to 8 PM. Per section 4b of our agreement, I should have been given the option to watch them. Please make sure to follow this clause moving forward."

## Fix the Ambiguity in Your Current Order

Often, an ex gets away with ignoring the Right of First Refusal because the clause itself is poorly written. Vague agreements are almost impossible to enforce. If your current order just says "parents must offer first refusal," it leaves too much room for debate.

Take a close look at your paperwork. Does it specify a time threshold? "Four hours" is very different from "overnight." Does it state how the offer must be delivered (email, text, phone call)? Does it give a deadline for you to respond before the other parent can make alternative plans?

If your current agreement is a blank slate, your ex can easily claim, "I tried to text you, but you didn't answer fast enough, so I hired a sitter." You may need to file a motion to clarify or modify the specific terms of the agreement so there are strict timelines, specific methods of communication, and clear consequences for breaking the rule.

## Propose a Trial Run or a Hard Sunset Date

Sometimes, rigid clauses fail because life changes faster than court orders. If your ex refuses to cooperate because your current Right of First Refusal is genuinely impractical—such as requiring a 30-minute notice for a sudden work emergency—it might be time to negotiate a practical workaround outside of court.

Offer a sit-down conversation or mediation to rewrite the rule so it actually fits your current lifestyles. You might agree to drop the clause for weekday appointments under four hours, but keep it strictly enforced for overnights and weekends. Setting clear boundaries that both of you can actually live with reduces the temptation for your ex to blow off the rule entirely.

## Decide When It Is Time for Legal Action

If you have documented repeated, flagrant violations—such as your ex consistently hiding overnight trips and leaving your kids with unsafe or inappropriate caregivers—informal talks will not work. Your next step is filing a motion for contempt or a motion to enforce the parenting time agreement. 

Before you call your lawyer, make sure you have your documentation organized chronologically. Judges appreciate parents who present clear, objective facts rather than emotional grievances. Keep in mind that enforcement actions cost money and time, so weigh the financial and emotional toll against the severity of the violations. 

Navigating a broken parenting agreement is deeply frustrating, but reacting out of anger usually backfires. If you are trying to figure out your next moves, exploring structured legal resources and specialized co-parenting tools can help you take control of the situation.
