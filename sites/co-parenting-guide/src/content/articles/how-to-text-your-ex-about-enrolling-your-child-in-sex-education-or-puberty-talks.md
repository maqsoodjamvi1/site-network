When my oldest kid hit ten, I knew the puberty talk was coming. I also knew that if I brought it up wrong with my ex, we’d end up in a three-day text war about school curriculums, parental rights, and who gets to decide what. 

Co-parenting communication is already a minefield, but sex education and puberty discussions are uniquely high-stakes. They trigger personal values, anxieties about growing up, and the deep-seated fear of being cut out of important parenting milestones. If you are standing at this exact crossroads and wondering how to text your ex without setting off a bomb, you are not alone. 

Here is the exact framework I learned—mostly through trial and painful error—for texting your co-ex about sex ed and puberty talks so you can actually get things done.

## Strip Out the Emotion and State the Objective

When we feel defensive or expect a fight, our text messages tend to get defensive first. We over-explain, justify our choices, or preemptively argue against points they haven't even made yet. 

If you text, "I know you think kids are too young for this, but our child is asking questions and the school is offering a program," you have instantly put your ex on the defensive. You’ve boxed them into a corner where they feel they have to argue against you, rather than think about the child.

Instead, keep it strictly focused on the logistical reality and the child's needs. State the facts, name the event, and ask a direct, non-threatening question. 

**Example text to avoid:** "The school has a sex ed permission slip. I know you hate this stuff, but it's important and I signed it."
**Example text to use:** "Hi [Name], the school sent home the permission slip for the upcoming 5th-grade growth and development unit. It starts next month. Have you had a chance to look it over yet?"

## Frame It As an Invitation, Not a Declaration of War

If you have joint legal custody, making unilateral decisions about sensitive educational topics is a fast track to court or a severe breach of trust. Even if you have full decision-making authority, looping the other parent in smoothly prevents unnecessary blowback that your child will eventually absorb.

The trick is to ask for their input on something specific, rather than asking for permission to parent. People resist permission requests because it feels like a test of authority. They respond much better to collaboration.

*   Share the actual material or curriculum link if the school provided one.
*   Ask what they are planning to cover at their house, or offer to share the books/resources you are using.
*   Give them a reasonable window to respond so they don't feel rushed into a knee-jerk "no."

By treating them like a partner in the logistics—even if you can't stand being in the same room—you lower their guard.

## Anticipate Their Specific Trigger Points

Every co-parenting dynamic has its own distinct landmines. Before you hit send on that text, ask yourself what your ex is most likely to panic about. 

Is your ex worried about age-appropriateness? Are they coming from a strict religious background where these topics are handled privately? Are they just terrified that their kid is growing up too fast? 

You don't need to apologize for your child growing up, but acknowledging their underlying concern can neutralize it before it turns into an argument. For instance, if you know your ex is worried about the school curriculum being too explicit, your text can preempt that anxiety gently.

*   "Hey, regarding the puberty presentation next week—I reviewed the slides the school sent home. It’s mostly basic hygiene and biological changes, nothing heavy. Let me know if you want me to forward the PDF to you."

This removes the mystery. Mystery breeds suspicion in high-conflict co-parenting relationships. Transparency kills the argument before it starts.

## What to Do When They Don't Reply (Or Say No)

If your ex ignores the text or replies with a flat "No, I don't want them doing that," do not fire back with an insult or an ultimatum. That is your cue to move the conversation out of text message purgatory.

Text messaging is terrible for nuance. If they push back, your reply should pivot to a low-pressure phone call or an offer to review it together. 

"I hear your hesitation about this. Can we do a quick five-minute phone call tonight after the kids are in bed to make sure we're on the same page?" 

If they refuse to talk and a school deadline is looming, lean back on your parenting plan or court order to see what legal rights you have regarding educational decisions. But try the collaborative route first; children benefit immensely when they don't see their parents split down the middle on basic developmental milestones.
