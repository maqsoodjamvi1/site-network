When my co-parent and I first split up, simple logistics felt like minefields. But nothing made my heart rate spike quite like drafting the message about enrolling our seven-year-old in Sunday school. Religion is deeply personal, and when you are already navigating the fragile territory of a post-separation relationship, bringing up faith traditions can easily feel like an attack on your ex's values—or a fight over who gets to dictate the child's worldview.

If you are staring at a blank text message window right now, wondering how to hit send without starting a war, take a breath. You can send this text successfully, but it takes more than just crossing your fingers. Here is how I learned to approach the conversation practically, respectfully, and with the highest chance of getting a constructive response.

## Check Your Court Order or Parenting Plan First

Before you type a single word into your messaging app, open your legal documents. In many US custody agreements, major decisions regarding a child’s upbringing—including medical care, education, and religious instruction—require joint decision-making, even if you share physical custody 50/50 or one parent has primary residential custody. 

If your parenting plan explicitly grants you sole legal custody, you technically have the final say. However, texting your ex a demand that says, "I signed Leo up for catechism," is a fast track to a hostile custody battle. Even if you have the legal right to make the choice alone, giving a heads-up prevents resentment. If you have joint legal custody, unilateral enrollment is a breach of the agreement and will likely land you back in court. Know your baseline before you draft the text.

## Focus on Community and Routine, Not Dogma

When you text your co-parent, frame the request around benefits your child will actually experience day-to-day, rather than abstract theological concepts. Co-parents often get defensive when they feel their ex is trying to "save" the child or indoctrinate them against the other parent's beliefs. 

Instead, anchor the conversation in things both parents usually care about: community, morals, structure, and friends. 

Bad approach: "I think it's vital for Maya to learn about God and go to church every week so she doesn't lose her way." (This implies the other parent is raising an immoral child.)

Good approach: "Hey, I’m looking at the Sunday school program at St. Luke’s down the street. A few kids from her school go there, and I thought it would be a good way for her to build a routine and make some local friends. Let me know what you think."

## Keep the First Text Low-Pressure and Open-Ended

Do not demand an immediate answer, and do not drop the text on a Friday afternoon when your ex is stressed about the weekend handoff. Give them time to process the idea. A good text should open the door for a conversation rather than backing them into a corner.

Here is a practical template you can adapt:

"Hi [Name], hope your week is going well. I wanted to run an idea by you regarding Leo. Some neighbors mentioned the Sunday morning kids' program at the community church. It includes art, music, and social time, and I think Leo would enjoy the routine. Take a look at their brochure link [insert link] and let me know your thoughts when you have a minute."

Notice what this text does. It provides a direct link so they can look at the facts themselves, it highlights secular-sounding benefits (art, music, routine), and it doesn't demand a yes-or-no response right this second.

## Anticipate the Common Objections

Before you hit send, try to put yourself in your ex's shoes and prepare for how they might react. In the US, religious differences during co-parenting usually fall into a few predictable categories, and having a counter-ready helps keep the text thread calm:

*   **The "Brainwashing" Fear:** If your ex is non-religious or follows a different faith, they may worry you are teaching the child that their other parent is wrong or going to hell. Be ready to reassure them: "My goal is just to give him a foundation in community values. You and I can always talk about our own beliefs with him, too."
*   **The Schedule Conflict:** Sunday school usually happens on weekend mornings. If your ex has parenting time on Sundays, enrollment directly impacts their schedule. Be prepared to offer a trade, such as: "If this interferes with your Sunday mornings, I am happy to adjust our weekend trade schedule so you don't lose any time."
*   **The Cost Issue:** Programs often charge tuition or registration fees. Address this upfront: "I am happy to cover the registration fees and supply costs myself."

## What to Do If They Say No

If your ex flatly refuses or ignores the text, do not escalate over SMS. Text messages are written records that can later be printed out and handed to a judge or a family mediator. Keep your replies entirely civil, even if they send an angry response. 

If you hit a wall, suggest taking it to a neutral third party, such as a parent coordinator or a family counselor, rather than arguing back and forth on your phone. Keeping your cool preserves your standing and shows that you are the reasonable parent trying to make decisions in good faith.

Figuring out how to handle big life choices across two separate households is one of the hardest parts of parenting apart. Taking the time to structure your initial message thoughtfully sets a cooperative tone for whatever faith-based or community activities your child explores next.
