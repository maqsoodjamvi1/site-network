When you and your co-parent live in neighboring states, the distance usually spans anywhere from one to three hours of driving. My ex lives just across the state line, about two hours north of me. At first, that geographic gap feels massive, especially when you are used to seeing your kids every single day. 

Standard custody schedules that rely on a quick mid-week dinner swap simply do not work when you have a county line—let alone a state line—between your homes. You need a rhythm that cuts down on time spent on the interstate while still giving both parents meaningful, unhurried time with the kids.

Here is how I figured out a multi-state routine that actually works, along with practical steps you can take today to build your own cross-border plan.

## Drop the Mid-Week Visits and Shift to Blocks

In-town co-parents often use alternating days or mid-week dinner visits to bridge the gap. When you live in neighboring states, trying to execute a Tuesday night dinner means four hours of driving for a two-hour visit. It drains the kids, burns through your gas budget, and eats up precious homework time.

Instead, build your schedule around longer, consolidated blocks of time. We moved away from the traditional alternating weekends and adopted an alternating long-weekend and extended-block model. For example, my kids spend three weekends a month with me, but those weekends stretch from Thursday afternoon through Monday morning. This way, we only make the drive twice per rotation instead of four times.

## Designate a Reliable Halfway Meeting Spot

Never assume you will split the driving 50-50 for every single exchange unless you live equidistant from each other. When we first started, we tried taking turns driving the entire two-hour stretch. It created endless arguments about whose turn it was and added unnecessary stress to Friday afternoons.

To fix this, we found a neutral midpoint right off the highway—a specific grocery store parking lot with good lighting and coffee shops nearby. 

*   **Pick a well-lit, public landmark** with restrooms and food options so exchanges are comfortable even if someone is running late.
*   **Establish a backup protocol** in case traffic on the interstate is backed up due to construction or accidents.
*   **Keep backpacks and gear in permanent rotation** so you aren't constantly packing clothes across state lines every single weekend. We bought duplicate sets of basic winter coats, toiletries, and school shoes for each house to cut down on trunk clutter.

## Factor State Lines Into School Districts and Holidays

Living in neighboring states introduces a layer of complexity most people forget about until school starts: differing holiday calendars. 

My state’s public school system takes a full week for Thanksgiving, while the neighboring state across the border only gives kids Thursday and Friday off. Spring breaks rarely line up, and teacher workdays almost never match. If you rely strictly on the school calendar to dictate your custody transitions, you will find yourself arguing constantly about whose state rules apply.

Your custody agreement needs a standalone holiday and school-break clause that overrides local school districts. Spell out exact dates and times for Thanksgiving, winter break, and summer vacation rather than relying on phrases like "according to the local school district calendar." This prevents last-minute scrambles when your child has a Monday off for a state holiday that doesn't exist across the border.

## Build Travel Costs and Communication Rules Into the Plan

Driving two hours each way adds up fast in gas, tolls, and vehicle wear-and-tear. Before you finalize a multi-state schedule, map out the financial reality. We agreed to split travel expenses down the middle based on mileage logs, which stopped minor financial resentments from bleeding into our parenting relationship.

Equally important is how you handle the kids when they are two hours away. When you are separated by state lines, you cannot just drop by their soccer game on your off-week. Set a predictable phone or video call schedule, but keep it flexible so you aren't interrupting dinner or homework routines at the other parent's house. 

Moving across state lines forces you to be intentional about every single transition. By cutting out short, exhausting visits, anchoring your exchanges at a reliable halfway point, and ignoring local school board calendars in favor of fixed dates, you can build a predictable routine that gives your kids stability in both states.
