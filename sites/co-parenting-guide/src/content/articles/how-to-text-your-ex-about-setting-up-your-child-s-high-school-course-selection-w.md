When my oldest kid hit eighth grade, the annual high school course selection sheet arrived in the mail, and my stomach immediately dropped. If you are co-parenting with someone you barely tolerate, you already know that a piece of paper about elective credits and math tracks is rarely just about school. It is a battleground for old arguments about futures, control, and who knows the kid better. 

I learned the hard way that sending a quick, frustrated text about academic planning leads straight to a three-hour thumb-war. Over the years, I figured out a formula to keep high school scheduling texts boring, efficient, and impossible to turn into a fight. 

## Separate the timeline from the trigger

The biggest mistake I made early on was texting my ex the moment I looked at the course catalog. If they were already having a stressful workday, dropping a complex decision on them guaranteed a defensive reply. 

Now, I use a two-step text strategy. I never send the actual course options on the first ping. Instead, I send a logistical heads-up at least 48 hours before I want to discuss it. 

Here is an exact text template I use: *"Hey, the high school course catalog for Leo came home. The deadline to submit is next Friday. Can we look over the core classes together this Thursday evening and lock down his electives?"*

This approach works because it establishes a boundary. You aren't demanding an answer right now, and you are framing the task as a joint administrative duty rather than an immediate argument.

## Send a pre-filtered list, not a blank slate

If you ask a high-conflict co-parent, "What do you think Leo should take next year?", you are inviting chaos. You will get back either silence or a wild suggestion that ignores graduation requirements. 

Instead, do the heavy lifting beforehand. Look at the school district’s prerequisite guide, check the graduation requirements, and narrow the choices down to two realistic options per slot. When you text, present those pre-screened options as a fait accompli with room for a minor choice.

For example, I text something like this: *"For Leo’s science slot next year, his counselor recommends either Physics First or Environmental Science. Both meet the graduation requirement for his target college track. I’m leaning toward Environmental Science because of his schedule, but let me know by tomorrow if you strongly prefer Physics."*

Notice what this text does. It removes the open-ended debate. Your co-parent isn't choosing from fifty classes; they are choosing between two pre-approved paths, or simply nodding along with your recommendation. It satisfies their need to be involved without giving them an opening to blow up the process.

## Focus entirely on logistics, never on potential

High school course selection often triggers deeper anxieties about our kids' futures. Your ex might want your child pushed into AP classes to prove something, or they might want them in easy classes to keep life comfortable. If you argue about *why* your kid needs a specific class, you will be fighting until graduation day.

Keep every text strictly anchored to current, verifiable facts from the school. 

* Bad text: *"You always underestimate Maya. She’s smart enough for Honors English and she needs to take it."*
* Good text: *"Maya’s English teacher emailed today noting that her current writing benchmark qualifies her for Honors English next year. Do you have any schedule conflicts with that period?"*

When you ground the text in what the teacher, counselor, or graduation chart says, you remove your personal opinion from the firing line. Your ex isn't arguing with you anymore; they are arguing with the guidance counselor. 

## What to do when they ignore the text or try to start a fight

Even with the best wording, you will occasionally get a text back that tries to bait you into an old argument about parenting styles or past mistakes. When this happens, use the "broken record" technique and ignore the emotional bait entirely.

If they text back, *"You're just trying to control his schedule like you control everything else,"* do not defend yourself. Reply only to the logistical deadline: *"We just need to submit the form by Friday at 3 PM so his counselor can lock his schedule. If I don't hear back by Thursday afternoon, I'll submit the counselor's default recommendation."*

Putting a hard deadline on your communication stops endless back-and-forth. It forces the other parent to realize that their refusal to engage has an administrative consequence, which usually motivates them to reply with a simple "fine" just to get it over with.

Navigating high school planning across two separate homes requires treating the process like a business partnership. By keeping your texts focused purely on deadlines, school counselor recommendations, and pre-screened choices, you can get your teenager's classes locked in without losing your sanity.
