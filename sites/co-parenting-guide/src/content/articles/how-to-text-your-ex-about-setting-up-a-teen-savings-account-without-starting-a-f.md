When my oldest turned thirteen last year, the reality of teenage finances hit me hard. Between her asking for a debit card for after-school outings and the reality that college is somehow right around the corner, I knew it was time to set up a dedicated savings account. 

The problem? Doing it meant texting my ex-husband. 

If your relationship with your co-parent involves high friction, the simple act of asking to split a deposit or sign off on a minor's bank account can feel like walking through a minefield. You worry about triggering an argument, getting left on read, or having a logistical chore turn into a three-day rehashing of old grievances. 

Over the past year of navigating joint financial decisions for our teenager, I have figured out a few tactical rules for texting an ex about money without blowing up your phone. Here is the exact playbook I use to keep things brief, boring, and productive.

## Strip Out the Emotion and Keep It Business-Like

When you are sending a text about money to someone you have history with, your thumb naturally wants to type defensive filler. We add phrases like "I know you're busy" or "Can we please try to agree on this." Stop yourself before you hit send. 

Treat your ex like a business partner in a small corporation where your teenager is the sole client. Business partners do not need emotional validation; they need clear, objective updates and action items. 

Instead of texting, "Hey, can we talk about [Teen's Name]'s savings? I feel like we really need to get on the same page about their future," try shifting to an administrative tone. 

A better text looks like this: "Hi [Ex's Name], I'm looking into opening a high-yield teen savings account for [Teen's Name] so they can start stashing away their babysitting money. I found one through Capital One with no monthly fees. Do you have 10 minutes this week to review the paperwork together so we can both be listed as custodians?"

Notice what that text does. It sets a specific agenda, offers a concrete solution, and names the exact next step. It leaves almost no room for an emotional counter-attack.

## Choose the Right Timing (Hint: Never Sunday Night)

Timing can make or break a co-parenting text, especially when finances are involved. Texting your ex about opening a bank account on a Friday night or a Sunday evening—when stress about the upcoming work week or custody handoffs is peaking—is a recipe for being ignored or snapped at.

Aim for a neutral window, like a Tuesday or Wednesday morning between 10:00 AM and 2:00 PM. During these hours, people are typically locked into work mode, which naturally keeps their replies shorter, more professional, and less reactive. 

If your ex works a chaotic schedule or night shifts, pick a time that aligns with their lunch break rather than their waking-up or winding-down hours. 

## Provide the Blueprint So They Don't Have to Do the Homework

The easiest way to start a fight with an ex is to dump a vague chore in their lap. If you text, "We need to set up a bank account for [Teen's Name], what do you think we should do?", you are forcing them to do mental labor, which usually breeds resentment and delay.

Instead, do 90% of the heavy lifting before you ever open your messaging app. 

* Research two specific banks that offer minor-friendly savings accounts with zero monthly maintenance fees.
* Find out if the bank requires both parents to be physically present at a branch, or if you can sign documents digitally via e-sign software.
* Draft a clear breakdown of how contributions will work (e.g., "I'll match whatever [Teen's Name] saves from mowing lawns up to $50 a month, and we can set up an automatic $25 monthly transfer from our joint account if you're comfortable chipping in").

When you hand them a fully baked plan, all they have to do is say yes or suggest a minor tweak. It removes the friction of brainstorming from scratch.

## Anticipate the Pushback on Control and Autonomy

Money triggers control issues in almost every co-parenting dynamic. Your ex might worry that you are trying to control how the teenager's money is spent, or conversely, they might be annoyed that you are asking them to contribute funds they hadn't budgeted for.

If you suspect they will push back on who manages the account, address it preemptively in your text. 

For instance, you can add: "Since they are 13, I want them to have the app on their phone to track their balance, but we will have the master login so we can monitor transfers. I'm not asking for extra money right now, just your signature/ID upload so we can get it open."

By explicitly stating that you aren't shaking them down for cash, you lower their defenses immediately. You are simply asking for administrative cooperation for the sake of your teenager's financial literacy.

## What to Do If They Ignore the Text

Even with the most polite, business-like text in the world, you might still get left on read. If 48 hours pass with no response, resist the urge to send a follow-up text chewing them out for ignoring you. 

Instead, send a low-pressure nudge that assumes the best. "Hey! Just floating this back to the top of your inbox. Let me know if Thursday works to sign those bank docs, or if you prefer a different bank option." 

If they still refuse to engage or stonewall the process, you may need to check the fine print of your divorce decree or custody agreement to see if major financial decisions require joint consent. Often, a single parent can open a custodial savings account independently, though having both parents on board is always smoother for long-term habits.

Taking charge of your teenager's financial future doesn't have to turn into a battleground. By keeping your communications structured, doing your research ahead of time, and stripping out the old emotional baggage, you can get that savings account up and running while keeping your peace of mind intact.
