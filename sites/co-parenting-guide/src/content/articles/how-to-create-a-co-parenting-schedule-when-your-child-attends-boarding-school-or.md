When our oldest child was admitted to a boarding school three hours away, my ex and I thought our scheduling battles were finally over. No more mid-week handoffs in grocery store parking lots, no more arguing over who has to pick them up from soccer practice on a Tuesday. We traded daily logistics for a massive calendar shift: our kid was essentially gone for ten months of the year.

What we quickly realized, though, is that boarding school doesn't end co-parenting scheduling; it just completely changes the shape of it. Instead of dividing the week, you are now dividing the breaks. 

If you are currently trying to figure out how to structure parenting time when your child attends a boarding school or a residential program, here is the practical approach I wish we had from day one.

## Shift Your Mindset From Daily Splits to Break Management

Traditional co-parenting schedules revolve around the school week and alternating weekends. When your child lives away at school, the school handles Monday through Friday. Your primary job is no longer managing homework routines or packing school lunches; it is managing the calendar of returns. 

Boarding schools typically operate on a specific rhythm: Thanksgiving break, a long winter holiday (usually three weeks), a spring break, and summer vacation, plus a few long weekends or mid-term breaks. 

You and your co-parent need to stop looking at a standard monthly calendar and start treating the school’s academic calendar as your master document. Map out every single departure and return date before the school year even begins.

## Map Out the Major Breaks First

The big holidays are where co-parenting friction usually spikes, especially when travel costs or limited flight windows are involved. 

For Thanksgiving and spring breaks—which are usually only a week long—split them evenly or alternate them year-to-year. If you alternate, Parent A gets Thanksgiving in even years, and Parent B gets it in odd years. Do the exact same for spring break. Trying to split a single week-long break in half rarely works well when your child is traveling several hours to get home, as half the break gets eaten up by driving or flying.

For the winter holiday, which is longer, a straight 50/50 split is usually the most realistic approach. If the break is 21 days, divide it down the middle. Day one through ten with one parent, day eleven through twenty-one with the other, swapping who gets Christmas Day itself each year.

## Divide the Long Weekends and Mid-Term Exeats

Most boarding schools and residential programs have three-day weekends or mandatory "exeat" weekends where the campus completely closes, requiring students to leave. These happen four to six times a year.

Treat these short breaks as a distinct block of time separate from major holidays. Sit down with a spreadsheet and do a strict draft pick for these weekends. If there are four long weekends in the fall term, assign two to each parent. 

Be sure to factor in geography. If one parent lives closer to the school, it might make more logical sense for that parent to host the child for quick three-day weekends to minimize travel fatigue. Build that agreement into your plan, even if you compensate by giving the farther parent extra days during longer summer blocks.

## Handle Travel Logistics and Expenses in Writing

When a child lives away from home, travel is a major variable. Who buys the plane tickets? Who drives the three hours to campus for drop-off and pick-up? 

Your co-parenting schedule needs to include a concrete travel protocol:
* **The Transporter:** Decide whether one parent handles all drop-offs and the other handles all pick-ups, or if you split the driving for every single break.
* **The Financial Split:** If flights or train tickets are required, specify the cost-sharing percentage (usually 50/50, or proportional to income).
* **The Booking Deadline:** Set a firm date—say, six weeks before a break—by which travel must be booked, so neither parent is left guessing or paying last-minute surge prices.

Failing to nail down these travel details turns every break into an argument about who is losing a Saturday to highway traffic.

## Preserve One-on-One Time During the Summer

Residential programs and boarding schools usually mean your child is away for the majority of the year. When summer finally arrives, the temptation is to let them decompress without any rules or structure. 

However, both parents will be starving for quality time with their teenager. Resist the urge to wing the summer. Divide the summer months into structured blocks—such as alternating two-week chunks, or a strict first-half/second-half split. 

Make sure your schedule also accounts for summer camps, jobs, or academic programs your child might want to attend. The co-parenting schedule has to flex around the teenager's growing independence, not just your parenting time demands.

Getting this schedule right takes upfront coordination, but the payoff is immense. When the rules of engagement are clear, your child can focus on their education, and you and your co-parent can avoid unnecessary friction.
