Let’s be honest: asking your ex to sign off on anything related to a teenager feels like walking through a minefield, but a background check for a first job is uniquely stressful. If your state requires a minor's work permit, or if your sixteen-year-old is applying to work at a grocery store, a camp, or a movie theater, you might need your co-parent's signature or authorization. When there is lingering tension from a breakup, a text about legal permissions, dates of birth, and Social Security numbers can easily morph into a three-hour argument about past grievances. 

I learned the hard way that the secret to getting a quick "yes" via text is removing all emotional bait and treating the message like a business memo. Here is the exact playbook I use to text my ex about my teenager's employment paperwork without starting World War III.

## Strip Out the Fluff and Stick to the Facts

When we feel anxious about asking an ex for a favor or a signature, our instinct is to over-explain. We write paragraphs about how much the teen wanted this job, how responsible they are being, and how proud we should be. Don't do this. Long text messages look like anxiety, and anxiety invites pushback.

Instead, lead with the logistics. Your ex doesn’t need a backstory on the hiring process; they just need to know what document requires their attention and by when. 

*   State the name of the employer right away.
*   Mention the specific document that needs signing (such as a minor work permit application or a background check consent form).
*   Provide a hard deadline based on when your teen needs to submit it to the employer.

By keeping the text strictly factual, you deny your co-parent any conversational hooks to drag the chat off-topic.

## Use the "Information-Only" Drafting Method

Before you hit send, read your draft and look for words that invite debate, like "I think," "We need to," or "Can you please do me a favor." Replace them with neutral statements. 

Here is a bad example of what not to send: 
*"Hey, Jackson got a job offer at Target! They need a background check signed by a parent. Can you please sign this when you get a second? I know you were worried about him working during the school year, but it's just weekends."*

That message guarantees a fight about Jackson's grades or your scheduling decisions. Here is a better, practical alternative:
*"Hi [Ex's Name], Jackson has been offered a part-time position at Target. Under state law, their onboarding requires a parent or guardian's authorization on the background check/work permit form. I’ve attached the document here. It requires your signature in Section 2 by Thursday afternoon so he can start orientation next week. Let me know if you have any trouble opening the PDF."*

Notice the shift? You aren't asking for permission to let your kid work; you are notifying them of a legal requirement tied to your teenager's employment and providing the tool for them to execute it.

## Anticipate the Objections Regarding Privacy

One very valid reason an ex might hesitate to authorize a background check is anxiety over sensitive information. First jobs often require entering a minor's Social Security number or home address into an online portal or a paper form. If your co-parent is protective of your teenager’s personal data, they might stall out of caution.

Proactively address this in your text workflow. If the employer uses a reputable, well-known background check platform, mention it casually. If you are handling the paperwork directly, assure your ex that you are filling out the bulk of the form and only need their signature or specific verification. 

If your custody agreement grants joint legal custody and both signatures are strictly mandated by the employer, make sure your ex knows you are following the rules to the letter. Showing that you respect their legal status as a parent lowers their guard immediately.

## Keep the Reply Window Narrow

If your ex responds with questions that veer off into territory you don't want to litigate over text—like complaining about transportation or work hours—do not take the bait. Acknowledge only the paperwork logistics. 

If they reply with, *"Why does he even need a job? Who is driving him there on Tuesdays?"* your reply should look like this: *"We can coordinate the driving schedule this weekend. For now, just need to confirm you received the PDF link for the background check so Jackson doesn't lose the placement."*

By refusing to engage in the sidebar argument, you force the conversation back to the ticking clock of your teenager's new job. Most of the time, common sense wins, and they will sign the form just to get off your phone.

Navigating the bureaucratic hurdles of your teenager's transition into the workforce is a major milestone, and doing it across two separate households adds an extra layer of coordination. Taking the next step means setting up a reliable system for handling these operational tasks so you can minimize friction moving forward.
