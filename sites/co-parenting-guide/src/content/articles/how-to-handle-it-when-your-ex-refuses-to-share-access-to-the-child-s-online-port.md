When my ex first changed the password to our son’s pediatrician and school portals, I felt completely shut out. One day I was checking immunization records and math grades, and the next day I was getting an error message. When I texted to ask about it, I was met with silence, followed later by a text claiming I could just "ask" if I needed to know something. 

If you are dealing with this right now, you know how frustrating and powerless it feels. In many modern co-parenting situations, digital gatekeeping has replaced traditional withholding. Your ex might not be keeping physical custody of your child away from you, but they are keeping you in the dark about their health, education, and daily life. 

Dealing with a locked-out online portal requires a mix of calm documentation, direct action with the third-party institution, and, if necessary, legal leverage. Here is the exact playbook I used to regain access and protect my role as an equal parent.

## Check Your Parenting Plan First

Before you send a fiery text or call a lawyer, pull up your current court order or parenting agreement. Look for specific language regarding educational and medical access. 

Most standard custody agreements contain clauses stating that both parents have equal rights to school records, medical records, and direct communication with providers. If your agreement explicitly grants you joint decision-making or joint legal custody, your ex does not have the legal right to block you from these portals. 

If your plan is vague or was drafted before online portals were standard, it might not mention "online accounts" specifically. However, it almost certainly mentions "medical and educational records." Accessing a portal is just the modern way of viewing those records. Highlight those sections so you have them ready for reference.

## Bypass Your Ex and Go Straight to the Source

You do not actually need your ex’s permission or cooperation to access your child’s information in most cases. You need to bypass the middleman entirely. 

For school portals, call the main office or the IT department. Let them know you are a non-custodial or joint-custodial parent (whichever applies) and that you are locked out of the student information system. Be prepared to email a copy of your court order showing your legal rights. Schools deal with high-conflict co-parents every single day; they have protocols for setting up independent parent accounts that do not rely on the other parent's login.

For medical portals, call the pediatrician's office or healthcare network. Ask to speak to the practice manager or HIPAA compliance officer. State clearly that you are a legal parent and request your own login credentials attached to your email address, or ask them to mail you physical printouts of records if they claim their software only allows one primary account. Under federal and state laws regarding minor medical records, legal parents generally have a right to access this information.

## Create a Paper Trail of Denial

If bypassing the system fails and you must address your ex directly, do it in writing. Do not call them on the phone where conversations turn into he-said-she-said arguments. 

Send a calm, neutral message through a co-parenting app or text message. For example: *"I noticed I can no longer access the school portal. Please send me the updated login by tomorrow, or let me know if you prefer I contact the school directly to set up my own independent account."*

If they respond with excuses, control tactics, or refusal, save screenshots of the conversation. If they ignore you entirely, that silence is also documentation. Judges do not look kindly on parents who use administrative control to freeze the other parent out of basic upbringing decisions. 

## Request Specific Court Remedies

If your ex refuses to cooperate and the school or doctor's office tells you they cannot create a second account without the primary account holder's consent, you may need to involve the court. 

When you file a motion regarding this issue, do not frame it merely as "they won't give me the password." Frame it as a violation of your legal right to participate in your child's medical and educational care. 

Ask your attorney—or draft your pro se motion—to request specific remedies:
* An order requiring the primary parent to grant administrative access within 48 hours.
* An order requiring all communication and scheduling to go through a neutral third-party app where both parents have equal visibility.
* A provision stating that failure to provide access results in make-up time or specific financial penalties for wasting the court's time on an issue that should be basic cooperation.

Navigating high-conflict digital gatekeeping is exhausting, but treating it as an administrative problem rather than an emotional battle helps keep your head clear. When you stop fighting your ex for the password and start establishing independent lines of communication with the institutions themselves, you strip away their power to keep you in the dark.
