When you live in a different household than your child, a simple evening video call can keep you grounded. It bridges the gap between major custodial weekends and lets you say goodnight before school the next day. But what happens when your ex stops answering the phone, turns off the tablet, or flat-out tells you that you aren't allowed to call? 

Dealing with a co-parent who blocks virtual visitation is intensely frustrating. It makes you feel helpless, especially if you are still trying to figure out your long-term parenting arrangement and worried about losing your bond. When I first hit this roadblock with my own co-parenting routine, my immediate reaction was anger. However, reacting out of frustration usually backfires. Here is a practical, step-by-step way to handle it without making a bad situation worse.

## Document Every Single Blocked Call

Before you send a fiery text message or march down to a lawyer's office, you need a paper trail. Courts and mediators do not operate on general impressions like "they never let me call." They operate on specific dates and times. 

Start keeping a dedicated log. Whenever your ex ignores a scheduled FaceTime or Skype session, write it down. Note the exact date, the time you tried to call, and whether it rang out, went straight to voicemail, or if you received a text response refusing the call. If your ex sends a text saying, "We are busy, don't call tonight," take a screenshot and save it to a dedicated folder on your phone. This documentation forms the foundation of every single step that follows.

## Review Your Existing Legal Standing

Your next move depends entirely on where you stand legally. Are you currently going through a divorce or separation without a temporary custody order in place? Or do you already have a signed parenting plan that explicitly outlines virtual visitation rights?

If you do not have a formal order yet, your ex technically has the legal right to manage their household time as they see fit, meaning they can refuse calls. While that feels deeply unfair, rushing to court over a few missed phone calls before a custody agreement is finalized can drain your finances and annoy a judge. Instead, your goal right now is to gather this documentation to present during mediation or your next court date as evidence that your co-parent is actively discouraging your relationship with your child.

If you *do* have a signed court order that guarantees you video calls on specific days and times, your ex is technically in violation of that order. However, calling the police rarely helps in these situations. Law enforcement will usually view it as a civil matter and refuse to get involved. Your remedy here is legal enforcement, which brings us to communication.

## Send One Clear, Neutral Written Request

Keep all communication about blocked calls in writing—either via text or email. Do not call your ex to argue about it, because phone arguments leave no reliable record. 

Send a short, completely emotion-free message. For example, you might write: *"I tried to call Leo for our scheduled Tuesday video chat at 6:30 PM, but the call didn't go through. Please make sure he is available for our next scheduled call on Thursday at the same time."*

This approach does three things. First, it reminds your co-parent of the schedule. Second, it keeps you looking reasonable and child-focused if a judge ever reads the transcript. Third, it boxes your ex into a corner. If they reply with an excuse or an outright refusal, you now have clear evidence of willful denial of contact.

## Troubleshoot the Logistics

Sometimes, what looks like malicious gatekeeping is actually a logistical headache that can be solved with a minor adjustment. Before assuming the worst, ask yourself a few practical questions:

* Is the scheduled time right in the middle of dinner, homework, or bedtime routines, making it stressful for the household?
* Is the device your child uses dying, broken, or shared with a sibling who is playing a game?
* Is your ex using a different app (like Zoom instead of FaceTime) that makes connecting difficult?

Proposing a small time shift—moving the call 30 minutes earlier or later—can sometimes remove the friction. If your ex claims the internet connection is poor, offer to pay for a better tablet or data plan if it is within your budget. Removing every possible logistical excuse leaves your co-parent with nowhere to hide if they continue to block access.

## Pivot to Alternative Methods of Connection

If your ex is stubbornly refusing video calls and your legal or mediation process is moving slowly, do not let your child feel the absence completely. Find alternative ways to stay present in their daily life that require zero cooperation from your ex.

* **Send physical mail:** Kids love getting letters, postcards, or small puzzle books in the mailbox with their name on it. 
* **Record video messages:** If you are allowed to email or text, record short, upbeat videos talking about your day or reading a chapter of a book, and send them to your child to watch whenever they want.
* **Play asynchronous games:** Use mobile games where you and your child take turns making a move over the course of a day or two, even without direct live conversation.

Keeping your presence alive in creative ways protects your mental health and shows your child that you never stopped trying to reach them. 

Navigating the grey area of co-parenting disputes requires patience, thick skin, and meticulous record-keeping. If you are trying to figure out how to address virtual visitation in your upcoming parenting agreement or need to know how to respond to an uncooperative ex, exploring structured co-parenting resources can help you build a stronger, legally sound strategy for your next steps.
