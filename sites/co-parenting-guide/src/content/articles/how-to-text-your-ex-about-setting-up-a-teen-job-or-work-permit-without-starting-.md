When your teenager turns fourteen or fifteen and starts talking about getting their first real job, a lot of emotions kick in. For co-parents who already have a strained relationship, the added paperwork, signature requirements, and schedule coordination can feel like a recipe for a weekend-ruining argument. 

In most US states, minors need a parent or legal guardian's physical signature on a work permit application before they can legally be hired. If you and your ex have joint legal custody, getting that signature often requires cooperation, even if the teen primarily lives with one of you. Sending that text message can induce a lot of anxiety. Here is how I approach texting my co-parent about teen employment without triggering a defensive text war.

## Strip Out the History and Keep It Strictly About the Teen

The biggest mistake I made early on was letting my text bleed into past grievances. When you are asking for a signature or coordination, the message needs to read like a business communication between two board members of a non-profit organization called "Our Kid."

Instead of texting, *"Hey, Leo wants to work at the grocery store this summer, but since you never do paperwork on time, I need you to sign this now,"* try something entirely neutral. 

A better approach: *"Hi Mark, Leo got a conditional offer to bag groceries at Publix starting next week. The state requires a parent signature on the work permit application. I can drop the form off at your office tomorrow at lunch or email you the PDF to print and sign. Let me know which works best."*

Notice what this text does. It sets a clear timeline, states the exact requirement, and offers two concrete options with zero room for emotional debate.

## Anticipate the Logistics Before You Hit Send

Before you text your co-parent, you need to know the actual rules in your state. If you live in California, Texas, New York, or any other state, minor labor laws vary wildly regarding school hours, maximum hours per week, and required documents. 

Do your homework first. If your ex asks, "Does this interfere with his custody days?" or "Who is driving him to the 6:00 AM shifts?", you should already have the answers. 

When I texted my ex about our daughter’s first retail job, I included the logistics right away:
* She will work Friday evenings and Saturday afternoons, which falls entirely on my custody weekend, so it won’t disrupt your parenting time.
* Her manager confirmed she can take the bus from school, so we don't need to split the transportation.
* The school counselor already signed the academic eligibility portion.

By answering the logistical questions before they are asked, you remove the friction points your co-parent might use to stall or object.

## Use the "Information-Only" Rule for Minor Decisions

Unless your custody agreement specifically states that part-time employment requires mutual consent (which is rare, but happens in high-conflict cases), a teenager getting a part-time job is usually an operational decision, not a major medical or religious one. 

Treat it as an update rather than asking for permission. Phrasing your text as a request for approval ("Is it okay if Leo gets a job?") invites a power struggle. Phrasing it as an informational update ("Leo is starting a job at the local library next Tuesday; here is how we need to handle the paperwork") establishes a healthy boundary. 

If your co-parent has joint legal custody, they do technically have a right to know about major employment, but framing it as a done deal—supported by the teenager's desire to work—shifts the focus away from your relationship and onto your teenager's growing independence.

## Protect the Conversation from Scope Creep

Co-parenting arguments love to wander. You start talking about a work permit, and suddenly you are arguing about who forgot to pack the winter coat three years ago. 

If your ex responds to your work permit text with an unrelated complaint or a jab about past child support, do not take the bait. Ignore the emotional filler entirely and respond only to the operational fact.

If they text back: *"Figures you're letting him work when you can't even get him to soccer practice on time,"* your reply should look like this: *"The store manager needs the signed permit back by Thursday afternoon. Let me know if you want me to bring the paper form by tonight."* 

Keep your blinders on. Stay boring, stay polite, and keep bringing the conversation back to the paperwork on the screen.

## Managing the Next Steps

Navigating teen milestones like work permits, driving lessons, and bank accounts requires a lot of behind-the-scenes administrative teamwork, even when you are living in separate houses. Keeping communication brief, direct, and centered on your teenager's goals makes the process much smoother for everyone involved.
