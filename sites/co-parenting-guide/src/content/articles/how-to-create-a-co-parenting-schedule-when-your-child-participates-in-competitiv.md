When my son made his first elite travel baseball team, my ex and I thought we had our custody schedule figured out. We had a comfortable "every other weekend, alternating Wednesdays" routine that kept things predictable. Then tournament schedules dropped, featuring games three hours away starting at 8:00 AM on our designated turnaround days, plus mandatory Thursday night practices two towns over. 

If you are currently trying to decide whether to enter the world of competitive youth travel sports while managing a shared custody arrangement, take a deep breath. It changes the logistics completely, but it is manageable if you approach it with the right framework. Here is how to build a schedule that protects your child's athletic goals while keeping your sanity intact.

## Ditch the Standard Custody Calendar for Sports Seasons

Traditional alternating-weekend schedules rarely survive contact with a competitive sports calendar. If your child has a basketball tournament in another state from Friday afternoon through Sunday night, the parent whose weekend it is loses all ability to trade off, and the other parent misses out entirely. 

Instead, look at the sports season as a distinct block of time—typically fall, winter, or spring/summer—and build a secondary calendar specifically for competition months. 

We had to shift our mindset away from strict equal-time tracking within a single week to a monthly balance. If my ex drove our son four hours south for a volleyball showcase on my weekend, I made sure to take on the Tuesday and Thursday night practices and gear-washing duties the following week to balance the effort. 

## Build the "Parent-Taxi" Matrix Before Tryouts

Before you commit to a travel team, map out the actual geography of practices and tournaments. Travel sports require an immense amount of driving, and bitterness builds fast if one parent shoulders ninety percent of the gas and windshield time.

Sit down with a map and list out:
* The home practice facility location and weekly practice times.
* The expected radius for regional league games.
* How many overnight out-of-town tournaments are on the schedule.
* Which parent's home or workplace is closer to the practice fields.

Create a shared digital calendar—we use a dedicated Google Calendar just for sports—and color-code who is responsible for drop-offs, pick-ups, and tournament hotels. If tournaments require hotel stays, decide in advance whether parents are staying in separate rooms near the venue or if you will coordinate so the child stays in a consistent hotel setup regardless of whose weekend it is.

## Handle Tournament Weekends Fairly

Tournament weekends are intense. They often consume Friday, Saturday, and Sunday completely, wiping out traditional visitation splits. 

To keep things fair, we adopted a "tournament trumps routine" rule. If a tournament falls on my ex's weekend, she takes him to the games, but I get "make-up time" during the following weeks, or we swap the following weekend entirely. This prevents resentment from building up over missed family time. 

When out-of-town travel is involved, establish boundaries on communication and coaching. It is easy for competitive parents to overstep when sitting in bleachers. Agree beforehand that whoever is traveling with the child is the "head coach" of that weekend trip, and the other parent supports from afar via FaceTime without second-guessing playing time or coaching decisions from the sidelines.

## Keep Expenses and Gear Coordination Out of the Dugout

Travel sports are wildly expensive. Between club fees, uniform packages, tournament gate fees, and hotel blocks, the costs add up fast. Never rely on verbal agreements for these expenses.

Your custody agreement or sports addendum should explicitly state how sports costs are split outside of standard child support. Do you split club fees 50/50, or does the parent who wanted the child to play carry a larger percentage? What about equipment upgrades like new bats, cleats, or travel bags? 

Keep a shared expense tracking app specifically for sports costs, and upload receipts immediately. Nothing ruins a successful weekend tournament faster than arguing over who owes $40 for the team gate fee or a post-game team dinner. Furthermore, keep a duplicate set of basic gear—extra socks, practice jerseys, water bottles, and mouthguards—in each parent’s car so you are never frantically driving across town on a Friday afternoon because the gear bag is at the other house.

Deciding to navigate travel sports within a co-parenting dynamic requires flexibility, clear logistics, and a willingness to put your child's athletic experience above scheduling rigidity. As you weigh your options and map out the upcoming season, take time to review your baseline custody agreement to see where adjustments are needed before the first tryout begins.
