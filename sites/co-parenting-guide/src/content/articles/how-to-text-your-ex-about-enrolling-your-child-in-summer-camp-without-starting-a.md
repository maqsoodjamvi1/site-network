Sending a text to your ex about summer camp can feel like walking through a minefield. You are trying to plan ahead for June, but one poorly phrased sentence can trigger a three-day argument about schedules, finances, or control. 

When I first started navigating co-parenting logistics over text, I made every mistake in the book. I sent long paragraphs justifying my choices, brought up past grievances, and asked open-ended questions that invited debate. Over time, I learned a simple formula: treat these texts like business logistics, not emotional conversations. 

If you are trying to get your child signed up for summer camp without starting a fight, here is the exact framework I use to keep things moving smoothly.

## Stop Asking for Permission; Present a Proposal

The biggest mistake I made early on was texting things like, "How do you feel about Leo doing soccer camp this summer?" That phrasing hands your ex veto power and invites an immediate knee-jerk "no" if they haven't had time to process it. 

Instead of asking for permission, present a fully researched proposal. Your goal is to make it as easy as possible for them to say yes by doing all the heavy lifting upfront. 

Before you text, have the logistics locked down:
*   Exact dates and daily drop-off/pick-up times
*   The total cost and your proposed division of expenses
*   A direct link to the camp’s website so they can read the details themselves

When you text, frame it around the child's excitement and the schedule, rather than asking what they think of the idea in the abstract. 

## Use the "Information First, Cost Second" Rule

Money is usually the spark that ignites co-parenting text arguments. If your first text mentions both the camp and the price tag, your ex’s brain will immediately lock onto the dollar amount rather than the benefit to the child. 

Separate the excitement from the invoice. In your initial text, focus entirely on the experience and how it fits into the calendar. Once they acknowledge the camp and agree that it sounds like a good plan, you can gently introduce the financial side.

Here is a real-world example of how I structure this:

> "Hi Mark, I’m looking at summer options for Maya. The local art camp she liked last year has a two-week session from July 10-21. It covers our custody weeks and runs 9-3 daily. Here is the link: [Link]. Let me know if you see any conflicts with your time this summer."

Notice what is missing from that message: any mention of who is paying. By addressing the calendar first, you establish that this is about the child's schedule, not a financial ambush.

## Give a Concrete Response Deadline

Ambiguity breeds anxiety and procrastination. If you text a proposal without a timeframe, your ex might ignore it for a week, leaving you stressed and the camp spots filling up. 

Always include a gentle, realistic deadline in your message. You want to give them enough time to look it over without letting the decision hang in limbo for so long that registration closes. 

Instead of saying "Let me know soon," try adding a specific timeline: "Registration opens to the public next Tuesday, so let me know by Sunday night if you have any schedule conflicts with this week." This shifts the urgency away from you and onto an external factor—the camp's registration deadline.

## Keep Your Emotions Out of the Reply Box

Inevitably, your ex might text back with something defensive or irrelevant. They might complain about the cost, bring up an unrelated scheduling dispute from last month, or accuse you of planning things without consulting them.

This is the danger zone. This is where a simple camp text turns into a screaming match via SMS. 

When this happens, use the "broken record" technique. Ignore the bait, ignore the emotional jab, and pivot straight back to the logistics of the camp. Do not defend yourself against accusations, and do not apologize for planning ahead. 

If they text: *"Must be nice to just sign her up for expensive things without asking me first."*

Your reply should be: *"I'm happy to discuss how we split the cost based on our agreement. Do the dates themselves work with your work schedule for July?"*

By refusing to take the bait, you starve the argument of oxygen. 

Planning for summer should be an exciting milestone for your child, not a source of chronic dread for you. By keeping your texts focused purely on logistics, dates, and facts, you strip away the emotional friction that usually derails these conversations. 

As you start drafting your message today, take a moment to review your options and map out the exact details your co-parent will need to see.
