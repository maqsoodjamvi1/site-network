Planning a family vacation or an international visit to see relatives should be an exciting milestone. But if you are a co-parent trying to renew your child's passport, that excitement can quickly turn to dread when you realize your ex needs to sign the paperwork—and is refusing to do it. 

Under U.S. State Department rules, children under the age of 16 require both parents or legal guardians to execute a passport application (Form DS-11). If your co-parent is withholding their signature out of spite, fear that you might flee the country, or simple apathy, you are stuck in a frustrating legal bottleneck. 

Here is a practical, step-by-step guide on how to break the stalemate, document the situation properly, and navigate the legal channels available to you in the United States.

## Check Your Parenting Plan and Divorce Decree First

Before you do anything else, pull up your final custody order, parenting plan, or separation agreement. Look specifically for clauses regarding international travel, passport acquisition, and decision-making authority.

Many court orders contain explicit language stating that both parents must cooperate in obtaining passports and signing renewal documents, often within a specific timeframe after a request is made. If your ex's refusal violates a specific clause in your court order, they are technically in contempt of court. 

If your order grants you sole legal custody, your path is much easier. You can apply for the passport by submitting Form DS-5525 (Statement of Exigent/Special Family Circumstances) along with court documentation proving you have sole decision-making authority. However, if you share joint legal custody—which is standard in most states—you will need to take additional steps.

## Initiate a Paper Trail via Written Communication

If you have only argued about this over the phone or in person, stop immediately and move your communication to writing. You need a verifiable paper trail showing that you made a reasonable request and that your ex actively refused or ignored it.

Send an email or a message through a court-approved co-parenting app (like OurFamilyWizard or TalkingParents). Keep your message brief, business-like, and focused entirely on the child's best interests. 

*   State clearly what is needed: "I am submitting the paperwork to renew [Child's Name]'s passport so they can attend the family reunion in July."
*   Provide a deadline: "Please sign Form DS-3053 (Statement of Consent) before a notary and return it to me by [Date]."
*   Attach the necessary forms so they cannot claim they didn't know how to do it.

If they reply with an outright refusal, a demand for money, or ignore you completely, save screenshots and PDFs of the conversation. This documentation will be your primary evidence if you have to take the matter to a family law judge.

## File a Motion for Specific Parental Decision-Making

When polite communication fails, you must ask a family court judge to intervene. This requires filing a motion—often called a Motion for Order Compelling Signature or a Motion for Special Relief—asking the court to order your ex to sign the passport documents.

Judges generally look favorably on international travel that enriches a child’s life, such as visiting extended family or school-sponsored trips. To win this motion, come prepared to prove:

*   The passport is necessary for a specific, beneficial trip or general identification purposes.
*   You gave your ex ample notice and opportunity to cooperate.
*   You are not attempting to flee the country or violate custody terms.

Some judges will simply sign an order substituting for the other parent’s signature, which you can then take directly to the passport agency. Others may order the recalcitrant parent to sign within 48 hours or face fines and legal fees.

## Utilize the U.S. State Department's Special Circumstances Process

If you cannot locate your ex, or if communicating with them poses a physical safety risk (such as a history of domestic violence backed by a restraining order), you do not necessarily have to drag them into court. 

The U.S. Department of State allows single parents to apply for a child's passport without the other parent's signature by filing Form DS-5525. To use this route, you must demonstrate "exigent" or "special family circumstances." Examples include:

*   You have no idea where the other parent is and have made diligent, documented efforts to find them.
*   Contacting the other parent would pose a direct threat to the safety or welfare of the child.
*   Severe medical emergencies or extenuating life circumstances make getting the signature impossible.

Be aware that the State Department sets a very high bar for Form DS-5525. If your ex is simply being uncooperative or difficult out of spite—rather than being entirely absent or dangerous—the passport agency will likely reject the form and tell you to resolve the issue in family court.

## Managing the Timeline and Next Steps

Passport processing times fluctuate wildly throughout the year, often taking anywhere from six to twelve weeks, not counting expedited options. If you have a trip booked and your ex is dragging their feet, every day counts. 

Begin documenting your co-parent's lack of cooperation immediately, consult with a family law attorney or your local courthouse self-help center to draft the necessary motion, and keep your travel plans flexible until the legal paperwork is officially signed and stamped.
