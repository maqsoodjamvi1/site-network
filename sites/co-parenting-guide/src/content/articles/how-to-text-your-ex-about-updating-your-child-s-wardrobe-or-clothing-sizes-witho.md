When my son outgrew his winter coat in November, the old version of me would have fired off a tense text to my ex: *“Leo’s coat doesn’t fit anymore, he’s freezing, someone needs to buy a new one.”* And predictably, that text would have started a three-day argument about who bought the last jacket, who has him on the weekends it’s supposed to snow, and whether my ex thinks I’m trying to squeeze him for extra cash. 

Shopping for a growing child is a normal part of parenting, but in co-parenting, buying clothes often acts as an accidental landmine. It touches on money, control, and the deeply sensitive territory of how we each care for our kids. 

Over the years, through plenty of trial and error, I’ve figured out how to text my ex about clothing sizes and wardrobe updates without triggering a defensive response. If you are trying to figure out how to navigate these conversations before you officially split households, here is the exact framework I use to keep things drama-free.

## Strip out the emotion and lead with data

The biggest mistake I used to make was mixing logistics with a critique of my ex’s household. When you text, "Leo needs warmer pants because he’s cold at your house," you are inviting an argument about whether his pants are actually warm enough. 

Instead, treat clothing updates like a business logistics report. Focus purely on physical facts that cannot be argued with: size changes and seasonal shifts. 

*   **Bad text:** "You need to buy Leo some size 8 jeans, the ones he has are way too short and cutting off his circulation."
*   **Good text:** "Hey! Just sorting through Leo's drawers. He has officially graduated to a size 8 in pants—all his current ones are high-waters. I’m ordering a multipack today, just wanted to loop you in so you don't buy duplicates."

By sticking to the physical reality of a growing child, you remove the personal jab. Nobody can argue with a growth spurt.

## Split the list before you go shopping

If you have joint custody, buying a double wardrobe for every single season gets expensive fast and creates clutter. One of the best decisions we made was dividing up the wardrobe responsibilities based on category rather than arguing over every single receipt.

When I text about a wardrobe refresh, I usually attach a proposed plan so my ex doesn't have to do any mental heavy lifting. 

*   "Since school is starting, I’m planning to grab Leo's daily uniform shirts and sneakers. Would you mind picking up his new winter jacket and gym shoes? Let me know if that works or if you'd rather swap items."

This approach gives your co-parent a clear, manageable task instead of leaving them feeling cornered. It moves the conversation from *who* is paying to *what* still needs to get done.

## Set a routine check-in window

Clothes don’t shrink overnight, even though it feels that way. Kids usually jump sizes seasonally—around late August for school and late February for spring. 

Instead of texting reactively every time a button pops off a shirt, I started batching these conversations. I shoot a text at the beginning of each season to align on what's missing. When you make clothing updates a predictable, routine conversation rather than an emergency, your ex is much less likely to react with defensiveness. 

Here is a quick checklist of what to include in a seasonal clothing text:
*   **The specific size change:** (e.g., moving from size 6 to size 7 shoes)
*   **The inventory gap:** (e.g., he only has two pairs of socks that fit)
*   **The proposed solution or purchase plan:** (e.g., I'm hitting the clearance sale this weekend)
*   **An open door for their input:** (e.g., let me know if he needs anything specific for your side)

## Keep receipts out of day-to-day texts

If you split clothing costs 50/50 according to your parenting agreement, do not negotiate the math over text message while you are standing in the middle of Target. That is a recipe for resentment. 

Instead, buy what you need for your home, keep the receipt, and log it in whatever expense-tracking app your custody arrangement uses—or settle it once a month on a specific date. If you must text about reimbursement, keep it deadpan: *"Added the receipt for Leo's winter boots ($45) to our tracker for this month's split."* 

Navigating the logistics of a growing child while living in separate houses takes practice, and it is completely normal to feel anxious about how your ex will react to basic requests. Getting the phrasing right takes time, but shifting your texting style from reactive to cooperative makes all the difference.
