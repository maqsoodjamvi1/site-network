Co-parenting after a separation is hard enough when you are both reading from the same playbook. When your ex suddenly decides to throw out the playbook—specifically by refusing to take your child to the agreed-upon therapist—it can throw you into an instant state of panic. You might worry your child is losing a safe space, or that your ex is trying to undermine your authority. 

When my co-parent and I hit this exact roadblock over our son’s counselor, my first instinct was to fire off an angry text message demanding compliance. That didn't work. What did work was stepping back, looking at our court order, and handling the situation with cold, documented logic rather than raw emotion. Here is the step-by-step approach I wish I had used from day one.

## Check Your Legal Blueprint First

Before you say another word to your ex, open your custody agreement, parenting plan, or separation decree. You need to know what the legal document actually says about decision-making authority and healthcare.

If your agreement grants you "sole legal custody" regarding medical and mental health decisions, your ex legally cannot pull the plug on a therapist of your choosing without a judge's sign-off. If you have "joint legal custody," the language matters immensely. Look for clauses that require mutual agreement for non-emergency medical care or specific mentions of the child's current therapist by name. 

If the specific therapist is named in your order, your ex is technically in violation of a court order by refusing to cooperate. If the order is vague and just says "the child may attend therapy," you have a different challenge on your hands. Knowing where you stand legally dictates your next move.

## Put Everything in Writing and Strip Out the Emotion

Stop having these conversations over phone calls or quick face-to-face drop-offs where things can get heated and details get lost. You need a written paper trail. 

Send an email or use your court-approved co-parenting app (like OurFamilyWizard or AppClose) to address the issue directly. Keep your message remarkably brief and focused entirely on the child's well-being. 

* State the facts clearly: "I noticed [Child's Name] missed their appointment on Tuesday at [Therapist's Name's] office, and you mentioned you won't be bringing them back."
* Ask for the specific reason without being accusatory: "Help me understand your concerns about this provider so we can address them."
* Propose a path forward centered on the child: "Consistency is critical for them right now. Can we discuss what you'd like to see change, or do you have a specific objection to this provider's approach?"

If your ex replies with insults or tries to drag up unrelated arguments, do not take the bait. Simply restate your focus on the child's schedule and the agreed-upon plan.

## Understand the Real Motivation

People rarely pull a child out of therapy for no reason at all. When my co-parent sabotaged our child's therapy, I eventually learned it wasn't actually about the therapist's credentials. It stemmed from deep insecurity and fear. 

Your ex might be acting out because:
* They feel threatened that the therapist is "taking your side" or hearing negative things about their parenting.
* They didn't pick the provider and want a sense of control.
* The co-pay or out-of-pocket cost is inconvenient for their budget.
* They are experiencing cognitive dissonance because the therapist suggested strategies that require the ex to change their own household behavior.

Try to figure out the root cause. If it is financial, offer to cover a larger percentage temporarily to keep the care consistent. If they feel left out, ask if the therapist can schedule a joint parent session so your ex can voice their perspective directly to the professional.

## Consult the Current Therapist for Guidance

Your child's therapist is bound by confidentiality, but you, as a legal parent, can still reach out to share logistical updates. Send an email to the counselor to let them know what is happening. 

Ask the therapist for their professional recommendation on how to handle the disruption. Often, experienced clinicians have seen this exact power struggle play out before. They can provide documentation of your child's progress, write a brief letter for your files emphasizing the necessity of ongoing care, or even offer a modified schedule that accommodates your ex's resistance while still keeping a foot in the door.

## When to Involve Attorneys or the Court

If your ex completely stonewalls you, refuses to communicate, and keeps your child away from necessary mental health care, you may have reached the end of the road for DIY problem-solving. 

Before rushing to court for a motion for contempt, consider asking your attorney to send a formal letter. Sometimes, receiving correspondence on legal letterhead reminds a stubborn co-parent that the custody agreement is a binding court order, not a set of polite suggestions. If that fails, your lawyer can help you file a motion to enforce the parenting plan or request a modification that grants you tie-breaking authority for medical and mental health decisions.

Navigating a co-parent's refusal to follow a shared healthcare plan is incredibly draining, but acting strategically keeps you out of emotional traps and protects your child's stability. As you weigh your next steps and consider how to document these missed appointments, looking at structured communication tools or legal review options can give you a clearer path forward.
