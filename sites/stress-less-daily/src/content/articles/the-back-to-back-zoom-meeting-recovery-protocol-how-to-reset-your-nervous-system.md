If your calendar looks like a stack of tetris blocks where every single meeting touches the next one, you already know the specific kind of exhaustion I am talking about. It is not just physical fatigue; it is a fried nervous system. By 2:00 PM, after four hours of staring at tiny floating heads and listening through compressed laptop speakers, my brain feels like it is running on dial-up internet. 

When you hang up one call and click "Join" on the next within a thirty-second window, your body stays trapped in a low-grade fight-or-flight response. You might look calm on camera, but your heart rate is slightly elevated, your jaw is clenched, and your breathing is shallow. 

Over the past year of working remotely, I had to stop pretending I could just power through five hours of straight video calls without paying a price later that evening. I needed a literal protocol—something fast, repeatable, and completely analog—to reset my system between calls. Here is the exact five-minute routine I use to rescue my afternoon.

## Minute 1: The Hard Screen Break and Physical Separation

The biggest mistake I used to make was trying to use the transition between calls to quickly check Slack, clear an email notification, or scroll my phone. That is not a break; it is just a lateral move to a different type of digital stress. 

Now, the moment a meeting ends, I close my laptop lid completely if I am on a laptop, or I physically push my chair back from the desk. If the next meeting is only two minutes away, those two minutes belong entirely to my physical body, not the screen. 

Standing up changes your posture and forces your spine out of the slouched "desk shrimp" position. Even if you only have ninety seconds, getting your eyes off a glowing rectangle is non-negotiable. I walk over to a window, look at something at least twenty feet away to relax my eye muscles, and let my shoulders drop away from my ears. 

## Minutes 2 and 3: The Physiological Sigh and Temperature Shock

Once I am standing away from my desk, I use two fast physiological hacks to force my nervous system out of overdrive. The first is a breathing technique called the physiological sigh, which naturally lowers carbon dioxide and slows the heart rate. You take two quick inhales through the nose—one deep breath, and then a sharp top-off inhale—followed by one long, slow exhale through the mouth. I repeat this three times. It sounds simple, but it acts like a manual override switch for panic and overwhelm.

Next, I walk to the kitchen and splash cold water directly onto my face, or hold an ice cube in my hand for thirty seconds. The sudden drop in temperature triggers the mammalian dive reflex, which instantly slows down a racing pulse. It sounds dramatic, but it provides an immediate physical boundary between the meeting that just drained you and the meeting you are about to enter. 

## Minute 4: The Brain Dump to Clear the RAM

Have you ever noticed how the thoughts from your last meeting bleed into your next one? You are listening to a project update, but your brain is still quietly panicking about the action items you agreed to on the previous call. 

During minute four, I grab a physical piece of paper—never a digital document—and scribble a messy brain dump. I write down any lingering tasks, worries, or half-formed thoughts from the call that just ended. I don't organize it or make a neat to-do list; I just get the data out of my head and onto the page so my brain stops trying to hold onto it like an open browser tab. Once it is on paper, my mind registers that the information is safe, and I can let it go for the next hour.

## Minute 5: Grounding and Intentional Entry

With sixty seconds left on the clock, I do a quick physical reset before opening the laptop again. Here is my exact checklist for this final minute:

*   Drop my shoulders and release the tension in my jaw by opening my mouth wide for a second.
*   Take a sip of room-temperature water to rehydrate my vocal cords so I don't sound strained when I start speaking again.
*   Ask myself one simple question: *"What is my actual role in this next meeting?"* 

Answering that question prevents you from walking into the next call with defensive energy. If you are just there to listen, you can relax your posture. If you are leading, you know you need to step back into an active state.

Fixing your back-to-back meeting fatigue is not about finding a magic productivity app or drinking more coffee. It is about building an intentional bridge between two high-stress environments so your body stops feeling like it is constantly under attack. When you give your nervous system these few minutes to decompress, you stop carrying the emotional baggage of your 10:00 AM call into your 11:00 AM presentation.
