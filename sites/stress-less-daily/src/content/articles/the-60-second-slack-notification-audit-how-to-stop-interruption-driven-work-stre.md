If you work a desk job, your nervous system is likely being drip-fed low-level panic eight hours a day. It doesn't come from a dramatic emergency or an angry boss standing over your shoulder. It comes from the chime. 

Every time Slack, Teams, or your email client pings, your brain briefly halts what it is doing to assess a threat. *Is that for me? Is it urgent? Do I need to fix something right now?* Even if you ignore the notification, the cognitive cost of deciding to ignore it breaks your focus. Over a week, this constant interruption-driven stress leaves you mentally exhausted by Tuesday afternoon, wondering why you got almost nothing substantive done despite staring at a screen all day.

You do not need to quit your job, throw your laptop in a lake, or turn off all communication to fix this. You just need to change how your digital workspace demands your attention. Here is a practical, step-by-step way to audit your Slack settings in sixty seconds and take back control of your workday.

## The Problem with "Available"

By default, modern communication apps are configured to maximize engagement for the company, not minimize stress for the user. They assume that if you are breathing, you should be reachable. 

When you leave every channel unmuted, desktop banners enabled, and your status set to a green active dot, you are broadcasting a message to your colleagues: *My time has no value, and I am waiting for you to interrupt me.* 

To stop living in a state of reactive stress, you have to shift your communication style from synchronous (where everyone expects an answer in three seconds) to asynchronous (where people expect an answer when you get to it). This starts with changing three specific settings on your desktop app.

## Step 1: Kill the Banners and Sounds (15 Seconds)

Go into your Slack or Teams preferences right now and turn off visual banner previews and audio chimes for standard messages. 

When a blue box pops up in the corner of your screen telling you that Dave in marketing sent a meme, your eye is involuntarily drawn to it. Your prefrontal cortex switches gears to process the visual input, and your heart rate gets a tiny, imperceptible jolt. 

You don't need to know the second Dave posts. You will see the message when you intentionally open the app. Keep the red badge counter on your dock icon if you must, but eliminate anything that moves, flashes, or makes noise while you are trying to write a report, analyze a spreadsheet, or code. 

## Step 2: Mute the Noise and Tame the Channels (30 Seconds)

Most workplace stress doesn't come from direct messages; it comes from chaotic public channels. If you are in twenty channels and all of them are notifying you of every single comment, you are drowning in digital shrapnel.

Look at your sidebar and apply these rules immediately:
*   **Mute general and social channels:** Right-click channels like `#random`, `#watercooler`, or company-wide announcements and mute them. You can still check them when you take a coffee break, but they shouldn't buzz your phone or desktop.
*   **Leave unnecessary channels:** If you joined a project channel six months ago and the project is over, leave it. You are paying a mental tax every time it lists unread messages.
*   **Use mentions only for the rest:** For active working channels, change your notification preference from "All new messages" to "Direct messages and mentions only." If someone needs the whole team, they can tag everyone. Otherwise, you don't need a play-by-play of a conversation you aren't currently participating in.

## Step 3: Set an Expectation Boundary (15 Seconds)

The final step of the sixty-second audit is changing your status message to reflect reality. If you are deep in focused work for a two-hour block, do not leave your status blank. 

Type something functional into your status, such as "Head down writing until 2:00 PM — ping for emergencies" or "In deep work, checking messages at the top of the hour." 

At first, you might feel anxious about setting this boundary, worried people will think you are slacking off. In practice, the opposite happens. Colleagues respect clear boundaries, and paradoxically, they learn that when you do reply, you are fully present rather than half-distracted. 

Implementing these small changes won't solve every source of workplace pressure, but it removes the artificial urgency that keeps your body stuck in fight-or-flight mode.
