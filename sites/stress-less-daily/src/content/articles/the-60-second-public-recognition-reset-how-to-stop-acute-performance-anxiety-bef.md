My heart was hammering so hard against my ribs I was convinced the people in the front row could hear it. Our VP had just called my name for the quarterly excellence award, and the distance between my chair and the podium suddenly looked like a mile-long tightrope. Instead of feeling proud, my body went straight into emergency mode: palms sweating, chest tightening, and my mind racing with thoughts of tripping on the stage or making a fool of myself during a speech I hadn't prepared. 

If you are reading this, you probably know that exact brand of dread. We spend so much time worrying about how to survive high-stakes presentations or difficult meetings, but nobody talks about the acute performance anxiety that hits *after* you succeed. When the spotlight shifts squarely onto you—whether it is a shoutout in a crowded boardroom, a birthday announcement, or walking up to collect a plaque—your nervous system can treat praise like a literal threat.

When your body floods with adrenaline, high-level thinking goes offline. You cannot simply "think your way" out of a physical panic response in five seconds. That is why I stopped trying to force positive affirmations on myself and instead developed a physical, 60-second routine. It is a sequence you can run entirely in your head and body while walking to the front of the room, completely invisible to the audience.

## Step 1: Drop the Shoulders and Shift the Center of Gravity (Seconds 1–15)

The moment your name is called, your body instinctively contracts. Your shoulders crawl up toward your ears, your chin juts forward, and you hold your breath. This defensive posture tells your brain that you are bracing for impact, which only accelerates the panic. 

Use the first fifteen seconds of your walk to radically alter your physical posture. You do not need to stand like a superhero; just focus on gravity. 

*   **Drop your shoulders:** Actively pull them down and away from your neck, imagining heavy lead weights hanging from your elbow joints.
*   **Unclench your jaw:** Separate your teeth and let your tongue fall away from the roof of your mouth.
*   **Ground your feet:** As you take your first few steps, feel the full surface of your soles hitting the carpet or floor, rather than walking on your tiptoes out of haste.

This physical reset breaks the fight-or-flight loop by signaling to your autonomic nervous system that you are not actually running from a predator; you are just standing up from a chair.

## Step 2: The Physiological Sigh for Instant Carbon Dioxide Offloading (Seconds 16–35)

When anxiety spikes, we tend to take shallow, rapid breaths into our chest. This traps stale air and spikes carbon dioxide levels in the blood, which worsens that dizzy, lightheaded feeling right before you have to speak. 

To override this chemically, use a tool known as the physiological sigh. It is a built-in biological mechanism that humans (and animals) automatically deploy when crying or sleeping to quickly offload stress.

*   Take a deep inhale through your nose until your lungs feel full.
*   At the very top of that breath, take one sharp, quick "top-off" sip of air.
*   Exhale slowly and completely through your mouth, making the exhale twice as long as the inhale.

Doing this just twice while walking up an aisle or standing up at your desk forces your air sacs to reinflate and immediately slows down a racing pulse. It acts like an emergency brake for your nervous system.

## Step 3: Shift the Lens from Internal Threat to External Connection (Seconds 36–60)

Performance anxiety thrives on self-absorption. When you are panicking, your entire world shrinks to the feeling of your heartbeat, the sweat on your hands, and the terrifying belief that everyone is judging your flaws. The final step of the reset is a cognitive pivot: moving your attention outward.

As you reach the front of the room or turn to face the team, pick one friendly face in the crowd—someone who is already smiling or nodding. Remind yourself of a simple, grounding fact: *this moment is not about my performance; it is about celebrating shared work.* 

Shift your mental script from "Don't mess this up" to "Thank you." When you focus on genuine gratitude for the people who helped you get there, there is no room left in your brain for panic. You can simply accept the handshake, smile, and say thank you without your voice shaking.
