That Slack notification pops up. "Hey, can you jump on a quick camera-on huddle?" 

Your stomach drops. Your heart rate spikes. You have roughly sixty seconds before you have to click that link, and right now, your brain is doing that frantic thing where it convinces you that everyone on the screen is going to notice your shaky voice, your flushed neck, and the fact that you feel completely unprepared.

If you are dealing with acute panic right before an unexpected video call, standard relaxation advice like "just go meditate for twenty minutes" is entirely useless. You don't have twenty minutes. You have about a minute before your face is projected in high definition onto five different monitors.

Over the years of working remotely while managing a nervous system that occasionally treats routine meetings like tiger attacks, I have developed a rapid-fire routine specifically designed for this exact window. It is not about achieving inner peace. It is about slamming the emergency brakes on your physical panic response just long enough to get through the call without fleeing your desk.

## The 60-Second Physical Override

When a surprise video call triggers panic, your sympathetic nervous system takes over. Blood rushes away from your digestive tract and extremities, your breathing becomes shallow and high in your chest, and your posture collapses into a defensive crouch. 

You cannot logic your way out of this state. Your brain thinks you are in physical danger. You have to communicate safety directly to your nervous system through physical action. 

Here is how to spend the sixty seconds between the notification and the click:

*   **Seconds 0 to 15: Drop the shoulders and unhinge the jaw.** Panic makes us hunch. Force your shoulders down away from your ears, and consciously separate your teeth. Let your tongue drop away from the roof of your mouth. This immediately signals to your brain that you are not actively biting or fighting.
*   **Seconds 16 to 35: The heavy seat check.** Plant both feet flat on the floor—shoes off if you can, to feel the carpet or wood directly. Press your weight down into your chair. Notice the physical boundary where your body meets the furniture. This grounds your spatial awareness and pulls you out of your looping thoughts.
*   **Seconds 36 to 50: Physiological sigh breathing.** Take two quick inhales through your nose (one deep breath, followed immediately by a sharp top-off sniff), then a long, slow exhale through your mouth like you are blowing through a coffee stirrer. Do this three times. This specific breathing pattern physically reinflates collapsed air sacs in the lungs and rapidly offloads excess carbon dioxide, slowing a racing heart.
*   **Seconds 51 to 60: The tactical distraction.** Look around your room and name three blue objects out loud or in a harsh whisper. This forces your visual cortex to engage with your actual immediate environment rather than the catastrophic movie playing in your head.

## Managing the First Three Minutes on Screen

Once you click the link and your camera flickers on, the acute panic usually morphs into a low-grade hum. You are visible now, which brings its own layer of stress. 

The biggest trap during the opening moments of an unexpected call is staring at your own video feed. Seeing yourself in real-time while anxious is like looking in a mirror while someone criticizes your appearance; it only amplifies self-consciousness. Move the video window as close to your webcam as possible, or right-click your own image and select "Hide Self-View" if the platform allows it. If you must see yourself, look at your forehead or the bridge of your nose rather than your eyes, which prevents you from locking into a feedback loop of judging your own expression.

Next, give yourself permission to buy time. You do not need to deliver a brilliant monologue the second you enter the virtual room. 

When you join, use a low-friction opener that puts the conversational ball in someone else's court. Say something simple like, "Hi everyone, glad to jump on. Just a heads up, I was right in the middle of wrapping up another project, so I'm catching up—who wants to kick us off with the context?" This takes the spotlight off you for the first ninety seconds, giving your adrenaline levels time to naturally recede once you realize the sky isn't falling.

## Building Your Personal Runway

Dealing with surprise video calls successfully isn't just about what you do in that exact minute; it's about setting up guardrails so your environment doesn't work against you. 

Keep a glass of ice water at your desk. If an emergency call comes in, taking a single sip of freezing water acts as a mild mammalian dive reflex, gently dragging down an elevated heart rate. Keep a physical object—a smooth smooth stone, a stress ball, or even a paperclip—right under your mousepad. You can squeeze it below camera level while you listen, giving your nervous system a physical outlet for trapped tension.

Nobody loves being ambushed by an impromptu meeting, but with a reliable routine, you can stop panic from hijacking your professional presence. As you look at ways to build more permanent resilience against work-related anxiety, exploring structured stress-management strategies can help prevent these sudden spikes from throwing off your entire workday.
