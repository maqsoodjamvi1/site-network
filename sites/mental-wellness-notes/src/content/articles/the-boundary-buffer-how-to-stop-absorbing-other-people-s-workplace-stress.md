Have you ever walked out of a meeting with a high-strung coworker and realized your own shoulders are pinned to your ears, your heart is racing, and you are suddenly dreading a project that was fine ten minutes ago? 

I used to call this "office empathy," but I eventually realized it was something else entirely: emotional sponge-hood. I was walking around my workplace with my energetic defenses completely lowered, soaking up everyone else's panic, frustration, and urgency as if it were my own. 

If you are someone who naturally picks up on the emotional temperature of a room, the modern workplace can feel like an obstacle course of other people's nervous systems. Protecting your peace doesn't mean you stop caring about your team. It means building a practical filter so you can support them without letting their chaos hijack your nervous system. Here is how I learned to build that buffer in real time.

## Name the Source Before You React

The moment you feel a sudden spike of anxiety, irritation, or dread at your desk, stop for three seconds and ask yourself a very specific question: *Did this feeling originate with me, or did it walk into the room with someone else?*

Most of us skip this step. We feel a sudden wave of overwhelm and immediately assume our own workload is too heavy. But often, if you retrace your steps, you realize that feeling hit you right after reading an aggressively worded Slack message from a stressed manager, or sitting next to a colleague who was pacing and venting before a client call. 

Just labeling the emotion as "not mine" creates a tiny wedge of cognitive space. You can recognize that your coworker is panicking about a deadline without adopting their panic as your mandatory emotional uniform for the afternoon.

## Create a Physical Decompression Ritual

When you share an office—or even a digital workspace—with chronically stressed people, your body often mirrors their physical tension. You might not notice you are holding your breath or clenching your jaw until you are dealing with a tension headache by 3:00 PM.

To stop carrying other people's stress into your personal life, you need a physical transition between "work mode" and "recovery mode." Crossing the threshold of your front door is usually too late. 

*   **The digital close-out:** Before you shut your laptop, write down three unfinished tasks for tomorrow. This tells your brain it is safe to stop holding them in working memory.
*   **The commute reset:** If you drive or take public transit, use the last ten minutes to listen to music or a podcast that has nothing to do with current events, productivity, or business. 
*   **The bathroom break shakeout:** If you just endured a high-stress conversation, go to the restroom, wash your hands in cold water, and consciously drop your shoulders away from your ears. Shake your hands out to physically discharge the adrenaline surge.

## Reframe How You Handle "Empathy Traps"

Empathetic people often fall into the trap of trying to fix a coworker's emotional state just to make the room feel safer for themselves. When a colleague drops a heavy, stressful complaint on your desk, your instinct might be to jump into problem-solving mode or absorb their grievance to validate them.

Instead, try practicing what I call "compassionate witness" without absorption. You can validate someone's frustration ("That sounds like a really rough situation") without taking on the responsibility of solving it or matching their frantic energy. 

Keep your voice calm, your tone steady, and your posture relaxed. When you refuse to panic alongside a panicked person, you actually act as a stabilizing anchor for them, rather than joining them in the whirlpool.

## Build a Mental Firewall for Digital Comms

Working remotely or in a heavily digital office comes with a unique hazard: toxic text. An all-caps email or a flurry of urgent Slack pings can trigger a fight-or-flight response even if the issue is minor. 

When a high-stress message lands in your inbox, force a pause before you reply. Give yourself a strict two-minute rule: close the tab, look out a window, or take three slow belly breaths before typing a single word. This prevents you from writing a reactive response fueled by their adrenaline rather than your own professional judgment.

Setting these boundaries takes practice, and you will occasionally slip up and absorb someone else's bad day. That is normal. What matters is noticing it happening earlier and earlier, and gently handing the emotional luggage back to the person who brought it to work in the first place.
