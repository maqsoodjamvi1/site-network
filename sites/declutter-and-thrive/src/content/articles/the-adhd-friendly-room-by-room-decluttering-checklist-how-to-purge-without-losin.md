If you have ADHD, standard decluttering advice usually fails. Telling someone to "just sort items into keep, donate, and trash piles" sounds simple until you are standing in a living room knee-deep in tangled charging cords, half-read books, and mail from four months ago, completely paralyzed by the sheer volume of choices. When every single object requires a micro-decision, decision fatigue hits within ten minutes. You end up sitting on the floor, overwhelmed, wondering why you ever started.

The secret to purging when your brain works differently isn't trying harder; it's changing the system. You need rigid boundaries, physical containers that limit your scope, and a room-by-room approach that keeps you from wandering off task. Here is the exact checklist I use to reset my own home without losing my mind or my focus.

## The Kitchen: Start with the Junk Drawer Trap

Kitchens are high-density zones that swallow tiny items. Do not try to clean the whole kitchen at once. Start with the single worst hot spot: the dreaded utensil or junk drawer.

*   Pull everything out of that one single drawer onto the counter. Do not look at the rest of the kitchen.
*   Toss the broken rubber bands, dried-out pens, and takeout condiment packets immediately into a trash bag held directly in your hand. 
*   Group the remaining items into hyper-specific categories: batteries, tape, keys, tools. 
*   Put a strict limit on what goes back. If you have ten screwdrivers, keep the two you actually use and put the rest in a donation box or garage toolbox right now. Do not leave them in a "figure it out later" pile on the counter.
*   Move to the pantry only after that drawer is done. Check expiration dates aggressively; if something expired in 2022, it goes straight to the trash without a second thought.

## The Bedroom: Out of Sight, Out of Mind

Bedrooms become storage units for clothes we tried on once and abandoned. The biggest ADHD trap here is the chair—the place where clean clothes and dirty clothes merge into a single fabric mountain.

*   Strip the chair completely. Dump every single garment onto the bed. This forces a deadline: you have to clear the bed before you can sleep tonight.
*   Use the sniff test or a strict "did I wear this this week?" rule. If it's dirty, put it in the hamper. If it's clean, hang it up immediately. 
*   Tackle the closet floor by pulling everything out into the hallway. Hallways are boring, which means you won't get distracted looking at your old yearbooks; you'll just want to finish and get out of the way. 
*   If an item of clothing is itchy, too tight, or requires dry cleaning that you never actually do, put it in a donation bag immediately. Do not try it on again.

## The Bathroom: Expire and Exhale

Bathrooms are relatively small, which makes them great for quick wins, but they are packed with tiny bottles of hotel shampoo and makeup samples that trigger hoarding tendencies.

*   Bring a trash can right to the sink counter. 
*   Pick up products one by one. If it smells weird, is half-empty, or you haven't touched it since last year, drop it in the trash. Do not try to salvage the last drop of expensive face cream.
*   Consolidate duplicates. You only need one open bottle of body wash in rotation. Put backups in a storage bin out of sight, or better yet, stop buying backups until the current one is empty.

## The Home Office: Taming the Paper Monster

Paper is the ultimate ADHD kryptonite because it looks important, takes up no space individually, and piles up into unmanageable drifts.

*   Never sort mail where you open it. Move it straight to a designated tray, and once a week, take that tray to the recycling bin.
*   If a paper requires action (like paying a bill or signing a permission slip), put it on a clipboard hanging on the wall at eye level. Horizontal surfaces are black holes for paper; vertical surfaces force you to look at them.
*   Shred outdated tax documents or utility bills older than a year in batches no larger than ten minutes. Set a timer on your phone so you stop when the alarm goes off, preventing burnout.

Decluttering with ADHD is less about having a spotless minimalist home and more about lowering the friction of daily life. When you finish a zone, celebrate the win and walk away before the urge to reorganize the entire house takes over.
