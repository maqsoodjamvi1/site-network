I love my morning coffee, but I hate looking at my coffee maker, my blender, and my toaster all day long. For years, my kitchen counters looked like a showroom floor for small electronics. Every time I wanted to wipe down the laminate surface, I had to pick up three different plugs and shift heavy plastic bricks just to get a clean sweep. 

If you are tired of playing Tetris around your toaster just to chop vegetables, clearing your counters feels like a massive upgrade. But hiding everything inside lower cabinets usually backfires. If I have to crawl on the floor to pull out a heavy stand mixer, I simply stop using it. 

That is why building a proper appliance garage changed how my kitchen functions. You get the clean, minimalist look of empty countertops without sacrificing the gadgets you actually use every day.

## What Actually Belongs in an Appliance Garage?

Not every small appliance deserves prime real estate in a garage. If you use something twice a year—like the ice cream maker or the holiday turkey roaster—it belongs in a pantry or deep base cabinet. 

An appliance garage is specifically for your daily or near-daily drivers. In my setup, that means the espresso machine, the everyday four-slice toaster, and a high-speed blender. These are the items that create visual clutter because they live out in the open, yet they are too heavy or awkward to haul up from a basement or low cabinet twice a day.

Before you buy hardware or clear shelf space, do a one-week audit. Leave a sticky note on every small appliance. Every time you use one, pull the note off. By Sunday, anything still wearing a sticky note goes into long-term storage. Everything else is a candidate for your new garage.

## Choosing Your Style: Countertop vs. Built-In

You have two main paths when setting up an appliance garage, depending on your DIY comfort level and budget.

The first option is a countertop cabinet box. These stand directly on your counter, sitting underneath your upper cabinets. They often feature tambour doors (the kind with wooden slats that roll up on a track) or simple cabinet swing doors. This is the easiest route for renters or anyone who doesn't want to cut into drywall or cabinetry. You simply assemble the box, place it against the backsplash, and slide your gear inside.

The second option is a built-in conversion. This involves modifying an existing upper cabinet so that it extends all the way down to the countertop, or removing a section of drywall to recess shelving into the wall cavity between studs. This takes more carpentry skill, but it delivers the ultimate minimalist result because the storage area sits flush with the rest of your kitchen architecture.

## Tackling the Power Problem

The biggest mistake people make with appliance garages is ignoring how power works. If you have to pull your blender completely out onto the main counter just to plug it into a wall outlet, you have only traded one annoyance for another.

To make this truly functional, you need an interior power source. If you are handy or working with a contractor, installing an electrical outlet inside the cabinet box is a game-changer. Look into recessed power strips or specialized cabinet outlets with built-in safety switches that cut power when the door closes. 

If running new electrical wiring isn't an option, use a flat-plug heavy-duty extension cord routed neatly through a small, drilled hole in the back corner of the cabinet. Plug that cord into your backsplash outlet, and plug a multi-outlet tap inside the garage. This allows you to leave your coffee maker and toaster switched "on" permanently, meaning you only have to tap a power strip switch to wake them up.

## Designing for Ergonomics and Weight

When you eliminate the visual clutter, you have to be careful not to create physical friction. Small appliances are heavy, and lifting a thirty-pound espresso machine from counter-height into a recessed box can strain your wrists.

If you are building or buying a countertop garage, look for or install a pull-out sliding shelf. These function just like kitchen drawer glides. You slide the shelf out toward you, use the appliance right there on the pull-out platform, and slide it back in when you are done. No lifting required.

Pay attention to vertical clearance, too. Measure the tallest item—usually the blender with the pitcher locked onto the base—before you set your shelf heights. There is nothing more frustrating than having to detach and reattach a blender pitcher every single time you want a smoothie.

Transitioning to a minimalist kitchen setup takes an afternoon of planning, but it fundamentally changes how you experience your cooking space. When your surfaces are clear, prepping dinner feels less like a chore and more like a blank canvas.
