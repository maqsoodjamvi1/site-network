Owning a historic home means falling in love with quirks like wavy glass windows, pocket doors, and plaster moldings. It also usually means accepting a glaring architectural truth: houses built a century ago simply did not have much built-in storage. When I bought my 1920s bungalow, I quickly realized that modern organizing advice—like buying giant plastic bins to shove into deep walk-in closets—would not work. My closets are shallow, my basement is damp, and my attic is uninsulated. 

To declutter a historic home without losing the character or the precious storage space you actually have, you need a room-by-room strategy. Here is the exact checklist I used to purge my belongings without sacrificing the historic footprint of the house.

## The Kitchen: Preserving Pantry Space and Original Cabinets

Older kitchens often feature freestanding Hoosier cabinets, pantry closets with narrow shelves, or lower cabinets with fixed, unmovable wooden dividers. You cannot rely on sprawling pantry shelving units here. 

Start by emptying one cabinet at a time, wiping down the wood, and ruthlessly evaluating duplicates. In a historic kitchen, every inch of cabinet space is valuable. 
*   **Let go of single-use gadgets:** Unless you make ice cream every week, donate the novelty maker. 
*   **Consolidate baking supplies:** Transfer flour and sugar into matching square containers that maximize the depth of shallow shelves better than round bags do.
*   **Check the hardware:** If you are swapping out modern wire racks inside your cabinets, stick to tension-mounted shelving rather than screwing into original wood interiors.

## The Bedroom: Maximizing Shallow Closets

My bedroom closets are barely fourteen inches deep. Hangers have to hang facing forward rather than sideways on a traditional rod. If I tried to pack these closets with bulky winter coats and oversized storage bins, the doors wouldn't even shut.

When purging the bedroom, focus heavily on clothing rotation. If you live in a four-season climate, your heavy wool sweaters do not need to share prime real estate with your summer linen shirts in July. 
*   Move off-season clothing to under-bed canvas storage bags rather than buying bulky plastic totes that won't fit up narrow basement stairs.
*   Evaluate your wardrobe by the "one in, two out" rule for vintage hooks. If you have installed period-appropriate brass hooks on the back of your bedroom door, keep your daily-use items there, but keep overflow out of sight.
*   Clear the floor of the closet entirely. Historic floors often lack subflooring thickness, meaning heavy floor stacks can block drafty floor vents or warp old wood.

## The Bathroom: Working Around Pedestal Sinks

Historic bathrooms are notoriously tight on square footage. Many feature pedestal sinks and clawfoot tubs with zero under-sink vanity storage or built-in medicine cabinets. 

When clearing out the bathroom, the goal is to keep counters entirely bare to let the vintage tile or beadboard shine. 
*   Toss expired medications and half-used hotel toiletries immediately. 
*   Look upward rather than outward. Install a glass shelf just above the doorframe or use the back of the bathroom door for a vintage-style towel rack to free up wall space.
*   Store overflow toilet paper and cleaning supplies in a hallway linen press rather than crowding the bathroom floor.

## The Living Areas: Taming Bookshelves and Built-Ins

Built-in bookcases flanking a fireplace are a historic home’s crown jewel. Unfortunately, they also attract clutter faster than almost any other surface. When books, mail, and random knick-knacks pile up here, the room instantly looks cramped.

Take everything off the shelves and sort items into three piles: keep, donate, and relocate. 
*   Be honest about your reading habits. If you bought a classic novel at an estate sale just because the leather binding matched your rug, but you never intend to read it, let it find a new home.
*   Leave some negative space. In older homes, letting the architectural details of the built-ins breathe is just as important as displaying items. 
*   Avoid plastic storage boxes in living areas. If you need to corral remote controls or coasters, use vintage wooden boxes or woven baskets that complement the era of the house.

Taking stock of an older home requires balancing practicality with respect for the architecture. Once you finish purging room by room, you will likely find that your remaining belongings fit much better into the historic footprint, leaving you with less mess and more appreciation for the home's original craftsmanship. As you plan your next steps for tackling the remaining corners of your house, consider how keeping your storage systems simple and period-appropriate will help you maintain your hard work for years to come.
