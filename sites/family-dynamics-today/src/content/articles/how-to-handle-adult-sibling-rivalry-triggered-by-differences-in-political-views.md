It started over a bowl of mashed potatoes at Thanksgiving, and by Christmas, my brother and I weren’t speaking. Our adult sibling rivalry, which had been dormant since our teenage years, roared back to life fueled entirely by starkly different political views. He was convinced I was ruining the country’s future with my ballot; I was convinced he had fallen down a rabbit hole of misinformation. 

For months, family gatherings felt like a minefield. If you are reading this, chances are you are standing in that same minefield right now, trying to figure out how to preserve a lifelong relationship with a brother or sister who looks at the evening news and sees a completely different reality. You do not have to cut them out of your life, but you do need a new playbook. Here is how I learned to navigate adult sibling rivalry when politics drive a wedge between you.

## Shift From Winning Arguments to Protecting the Bond

When we care about someone, we naturally want them to see the world the way we do. With siblings, that instinct is supercharged by our shared history. We think, "We grew up in the same house with the same parents; how can you possibly think this way?" 

The turning point for me came when I realized that every time I tried to correct my brother's political stance, I was treating our relationship like a debate stage. No one has ever changed their worldview because their sibling yelled a talking point across the dining room table. I had to ask myself a hard question: Would I rather be right, or would I rather have a relationship with my brother? 

When you feel the urge to fact-check your sibling in real time, pause. Remind yourself that winning the argument means losing the connection. Your goal is no longer to persuade them; your goal is to figure out how to coexist peacefully despite the disagreement.

## Establish Strict Boundaries and Script Your Exits

Good fences make good neighbors, and good boundaries make good siblings. You cannot control what your brother or sister posts on social media or brings up at family dinner, but you can control your participation. 

Before heading into the next family function, decide on your limits and practice how you will enforce them. When my sister-in-law starts steering conversation toward controversial legislation, I no longer take the bait. Instead, I use pre-scripted exit lines that are polite but firm:

* "You know, we see this completely differently, and I’d rather keep today about catching up."
* "I’m taking a break from talking about politics these days. Can we pass the salad?"
* "If we keep going down this road, we’re going to ruin dinner. Let’s talk about your trip instead."

If they push past the boundary—which they might, out of old sibling habit—you have to physically remove yourself. Get up to refill your glass, go check on the food, or step outside. You are training them to realize that political hot-takes are an instant conversation-killer.

## Untangle Childhood Patterns From Current Disagreements

Adult sibling rivalry rarely exists in a vacuum. Often, political disagreements are just a new container for old, unresolved childhood dynamics. 

In my case, my brother was the oldest, used to being the authority figure, while I was the youngest, prone to pushing his buttons just to get a reaction. When we argued about politics, we weren't just debating tax policy; we were falling back into our default sibling roles from 1995. He was lecturing me like a parent, and I was rebelling against him like an annoyed teenager.

Take a step back and examine if your current fights are really about the ballot box, or if they are about respect, feeling heard, or perceived favoritism from your parents. Recognizing that my brother’s condescending tone triggered my childhood defensiveness allowed me to respond as a calm, grounded adult rather than snapping back like a kid.

## Find Common Ground Outside the News Cycle

When political tension takes over a sibling relationship, you tend to forget what you actually like about each other. You stop calling because every conversation feels exhausting. 

To break this cycle, you have to intentionally create neutral zones. Reach out to your sibling about topics that have nothing to do with current events. Send them a text about a childhood band you both loved, ask for advice on a home repair project they have experience with, or invite them to do an activity where talking isn't the main focus, like hiking, fishing, or cooking a difficult recipe together.

Rebuilding trust takes time, and it starts with small, positive interactions that prove you can still enjoy each other's company. You do not need to agree on the state of the nation to share a laugh over old memories or support each other through a personal crisis.
