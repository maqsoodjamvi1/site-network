When my mother chose my brother to hold her medical power of attorney, the group text didn't explode right away. It took about three weeks—right until the first time my brother scheduled a cardiologist appointment without copying me on the calendar invite. 

Suddenly, decades-old competition over who was the "favorite" child flared right back up. We were both in our forties, with our own mortgages and teenagers, yet we were instantly thrust back into the backseat of a station wagon fighting over who got the window seat. 

If you are dealing with adult sibling rivalry sparked by aging parent medical decisions, you are likely exhausted. You aren't just managing appointments and hospital portals; you're managing bruised egos, historical grievances, and the terrifying reality of your parents declining. Here is how I learned to navigate this messy dynamic without completely destroying my relationship with my sibling.

## Separate the Paperwork from the Parenting

The core of sibling rivalry over a medical power of attorney (POA) usually isn't about the actual healthcare decisions. It is about validation. When a parent appoints one child over another, the unappointed sibling often feels demoted, untrusted, or erased. 

My brother was acting like an authoritarian CEO because he felt the weight of the sole responsibility crashing down on him. I was acting like an offended auditor because I felt sidelined and treated like an unreliable teenager. 

To break this cycle, you have to separate the legal role from emotional worth. Being named the POA is an administrative burden, not a gold star. It means endless phone calls with Medicare, sitting in uncomfortable hospital chairs, and carrying the moral weight of end-of-life choices. Recognizing this shift changed my perspective: my brother wasn't holding a trophy; he was holding a liability. 

## Establish a Transparent Information Pipeline

Silence breeds paranoia. When the non-POA sibling is left in the dark about a doctor's visit, imagination fills in the blanks. They assume the worst—that decisions are being made behind their back or that their parent's wishes are being ignored.

To fix this, stop waiting for your sibling to guess what is happening and set up a low-friction communication habit. Do not rely on group text messages, which often turn into emotional ping-pong matches. Instead, implement a system like a shared notes app on your phone or a dedicated email thread with a strict subject-line convention. 

*   Designate one person to take notes during doctor appointments and paste them into the shared document within two hours of leaving the clinic.
*   Keep updates strictly factual: symptoms, doctor's recommendations, and next steps. Leave emotional commentary out of the initial report.
*   Agree on a weekly check-in time (even if it's just ten minutes on Sunday night) so you aren't reacting to emergencies in real time.

By creating a single source of truth, you starve the rivalry of the oxygen it needs to grow. 

## Assign Out-of-Office Portfolios

A common trap in sibling caregiving is the "all-or-nothing" fallacy: because one person holds the legal power of attorney, they assume they must do 100 percent of the work. Meanwhile, the other sibling feels useless and sidelined, which triggers resentment and backseat driving.

You need to divide the labor based on practical strengths, regardless of who holds the legal paperwork. The legal POA has the final say on medical consent, but that does not mean they have to do every task.

For example, my brother legally holds the POA and signs the consent forms. However, I handle insurance appeals, coordinate physical therapy schedules, and manage the medication refill logs. Because I own specific portfolios, I am an active participant in my mother's care rather than a passive critic. Sit down with your sibling and map out the administrative tasks—researching facility reviews, managing the calendar, packing hospital bags—and consciously delegate ownership of those items.

## Address the Elephant in the Room Separately

If your sibling is weaponizing the medical POA to shut you out, or if you find yourself nitpicking every choice they make out of spite, you need to clear the air. Do this away from your parents, away from the hospital, and ideally not over a screen.

When you talk, use "I" statements that anchor back to your shared goal: keeping your parent safe. You might say something like, "I know Mom put you in charge of the medical decisions, and I respect that. But when I find out about specialist appointments after they happen, I feel sidelined. I want to support you, but I need to know I'm part of the team."

Most of the time, defensive behavior crumbles when the other person realizes you aren't trying to overthrow their authority—you just want to share the weight. 

Navigating aging parent care alongside sibling friction requires conscious effort and firm boundaries. As you look at the road ahead and figure out how to balance family dynamics with the practical demands of medical advocacy, taking small, structured steps today can prevent bitter regrets tomorrow.
