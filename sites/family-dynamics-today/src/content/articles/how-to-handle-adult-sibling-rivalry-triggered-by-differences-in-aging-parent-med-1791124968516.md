When my brother and I sat in the parking lot of our mother’s cardiologist office three years ago, we nearly came to blows over a prescription change. Mom had named him her medical power of attorney, but because I lived five minutes away and drove her to every appointment, I felt I should have final say on treatment adjustments. That afternoon argument dredged up every childhood competition we had supposedly outgrown. 

If you are dealing with adult sibling rivalry sparked by aging parent medical decisions, you are likely exhausted. It is a grueling mix of anticipatory grief, parental protectiveness, and lingering sibling dynamics that make every phone call feel like a minefield. The good news is that you can stop the escalation before it compromises your parent’s care.

## Acknowledge the Root Cause Behind the Fight

When siblings bicker over medical directives, the argument is rarely about the actual medical choice. It is usually about fear, guilt, and roles. 

In my case, my brother lived three hours away and was terrified he was missing out on our mother’s final chapters, which made him fiercely protective of his legal authority. Meanwhile, I was drowning in daily caregiving tasks and resented that he could swoop in for thirty minutes, offer an opinion, and drive back home without dealing with the fallout. 

To break the cycle, you need to look past the immediate disagreement—whether it is about moving Mom to assisted living or agreeing to a risky surgery—and recognize what your sibling is actually feeling. Are they projecting guilt because they cannot visit often? Are they terrified of losing the parent who held the family together? Naming this dynamic out loud takes the wind out of its sails.

## Define Clear Roles Based on Logistics, Not Titles

Being named the medical power of attorney is a legal designation, not a full-time job description. If your sibling holds the paperwork but you are the one on the ground, friction is guaranteed unless you deliberately divide the labor. 

Sit down—or get on a video call—and draft a functional breakdown of who handles what. For example:
* **The Logistics Lead:** The sibling who lives closest handles appointment scheduling, physical transportation, and immediate communication with home health aides.
* **The Research Lead:** The long-distance sibling takes charge of digging into specialized care facilities, reading up on new diagnoses, and coordinating second opinions online.
* **The Family Communicator:** One person is designated to send a single, comprehensive weekly email update to the rest of the extended family, stopping the endless cycle of fragmented phone calls and misquoted prognoses.

When everyone has a defined lane, there is less room for turf wars. The power of attorney still makes the final legal call, but they make it armed with data gathered by the team.

## Institute a "No Ambush" Communication Rule

Sibling rivalry thrives on group chats gone wrong and late-night panic calls. Our family finally found peace when we established strict ground rules for how we discuss Mom's health. 

First, we banned medical updates via text message. Texting strips away tone, inviting misinterpretation and immediate defensive replies. Instead, we use a shared digital document to log doctor's notes. 

Second, if a major decision looms—such as whether to pursue palliative care—we schedule a dedicated phone or video meeting with at least twenty-four hours of notice. This gives the non-POA siblings time to process their emotions and prepare rational questions rather than reacting out of sudden panic. If emotions start running hot during the call, either person can invoke a "cool-down timeout," meaning the conversation pauses for a mandatory two hours before resuming.

## Accept That Fair Does Not Mean Equal

One of the biggest triggers for adult sibling rivalry is the scorecard mentality. We keep mental tallies of who spent more money on Mom’s prescriptions, who called her more often last week, or who got to spend Mother’s Day with her. 

In the realm of aging parent healthcare, fairness is a myth. One sibling might have the financial flexibility to pay for private nursing care, while another has the schedule flexibility to sit in a hospital room for twelve hours straight. Both contributions are vital, even if they look entirely different on paper. Letting go of the need for absolute equality in workload prevents resentment from poisoning your relationship with your brother or sister.

Navigating your parent’s decline while repairing or maintaining a relationship with a sibling requires immense patience and boundaries. By shifting your focus from who holds the power of attorney badge to how you can practically support each other through a shared loss, you can honor your parent without tearing your family apart. As you consider your next steps and look for additional frameworks to manage these delicate family transitions, the right guidance can make all the difference.
