When my fiancé and I called off our wedding, packed up our apartment, and spent four months living apart, everyone told us we were done. But we weren't. We both realized the wedding planning stress and poor communication had broken our engagement, not our actual love for one another. When we decided to start over from scratch and rebuild our relationship, we quickly realized our old habits wouldn't cut it. 

One of the first practical projects we tackled was figuring out each other's love languages again. After trauma like a broken engagement, people change. The things that used to make you feel loved might actually trigger anxiety now. If you are in the trenches of rebuilding trust with your partner, here is how I figured out what my partner actually needed—and how you can do it too.

## Look at Where the Resentment Lived

When trust breaks down, resentment usually moves in. If you want to find your partner’s true love language during this fragile recovery phase, look backward at what you argued about most before the split. Often, complaints are just love languages wearing a mask of frustration.

For example, my partner used to constantly complain that I "never helped around the house" or noticed when he was overwhelmed. I thought he was just being nitpicky. But looking back through the lens of rebuilding trust, I realized his complaints were screaming *Acts of Service*. He wasn't mad about the dishes; he felt completely unsupported by me during high-stress times. 

Take out a notebook today and write down the top three arguments you had before your engagement fell apart. Strip away the anger and look at the underlying request. Were they asking you to spend more focused time together? Were they wishing you would notice when they bought a new shirt or changed their hair? That history is a goldmine for understanding what they are currently starved for.

## Pay Attention to How They Try to Reconnect Now

Rebuilding trust means watching your partner closely as they extend olive branches. People in distress naturally revert to giving the kind of love they most desperately want to receive. If your partner is trying to mend fences, look at their specific actions this week.

*   Are they constantly trying to buy you your favorite coffee, pick up groceries without being asked, or fix things around your place? (Acts of Service)
*   Are they leaving sticky notes on your bathroom mirror, sending long text messages, or telling you specific things they admire about your character? (Words of Affirmation)
*   Are they putting their phone face-down on the table when you talk, asking to go for evening walks, or suggesting weekend getaways? (Quality Time)
*   Are they buying small "just because" gifts, picking up souvenirs when they travel, or bringing you your favorite snack? (Receiving Gifts)
*   Are they initiating hugs, holding your hand in the car, or pulling you close on the couch? (Physical Touch)

When my partner and I started dating again, he started showing up at my apartment with my favorite takeout every Friday night. I didn't need to take a quiz to figure out his love language had shifted; his actions were spelling it out clearly. He wanted comfort, consistency, and tangible proof of care through acts of service and physical presence.

## Test Small Gestures and Watch for Relief, Not Just Gratitude

Guessing love languages during the trust-rebuilding phase can feel like defusing a bomb. You want to try things out, but you are both easily triggered. Instead of making grand romantic gestures, run micro-experiments. 

For instance, try a week of heavy verbal validation. Tell your partner specifically what you appreciate about how they are handling this reconciliation. Then, watch their body language. Do they visibly relax, or do they look uncomfortable and brush it off? 

If you try giving a gift and they look stressed about spending money, that might not be their language right now. If you try an act of service—like taking over a chore they usually dread—and they let out a massive sigh of relief, you have your answer. During trust-building, relief is the best indicator you have hit the right nerve. Pay attention to what makes their shoulders drop.

## Have the Direct "Safety Conversation"

When you are trying to figure out a love language after a broken engagement, you cannot rely on intuition alone. The old relationship is dead; you are building a new one. Have a conversation specifically centered on emotional safety, not romance.

Sit down and ask your partner: "When you feel scared or insecure about us right now, what action makes you feel safe again?" 

Notice the word *safe* instead of *loved*. When you are recovering from a broken engagement, love and safety are deeply intertwined. Your partner’s answer might surprise you. They might tell you they don't need romantic dinners or expensive gifts; they just need you to sit on the porch with them for twenty minutes without phones. 

Figuring this out isn't about checking boxes for a personality test. It is a daily, intentional practice of learning a stranger who happens to have your partner's face. As you navigate this delicate phase of figuring out what makes them feel secure, it helps to have a structured roadmap to keep both of you from falling back into old, destructive patterns.
