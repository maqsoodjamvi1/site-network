When my husband and I first got together, I thought I was doing everything right. I left thoughtful notes in his lunchbox, planned surprise weekend getaways, and said "I love you" multiple times a day. He smiled, but his responses always felt a little polite rather than deeply moved. Meanwhile, when he bought me groceries or spent hours fixing my squeaky car door, I barely registered it as romance. 

It took us two years of mismatched expectations to realize the issue wasn't a lack of care. We were simply trying to read love stories written in completely different languages. More accurately, we were trying to decode affection across an ocean of cultural differences. 

If you are trying to understand a partner whose background looks different from your own, standard relationship advice often falls short. Gary Chapman’s classic love languages—words of affirmation, acts of service, receiving gifts, quality time, and physical touch—are a great starting point, but culture profoundly shapes how those categories are expressed and valued. Here is how to figure out what your partner is actually trying to tell you.

## Look Beyond the Western Blueprint of "Words of Affirmation"

In many Western relationship spaces, verbal validation is treated as the gold standard. We expect daily verbal praise, explicit reassurance, and comfortable declarations of feeling. However, in many East Asian, Latin American, and Mediterranean cultures, verbalizing intense personal emotions every single day can feel awkward, performative, or even unnecessary. 

If your partner rarely says "I love you" out loud, don't automatically assume they are emotionally detached. Look at what they do instead. In my husband’s family, saying "have you eaten yet?" or waking up early to scrape the ice off your windshield is the emotional equivalent of a sonnet. Direct verbal praise is often reserved for monumental life milestones, while quiet, daily reliability carries the weight of devotion.

## Shift Your Focus from Grand Gestures to Constant Provision

Acts of service can also look radically different depending on cultural upbringing and socioeconomic history. If your partner comes from a culture that emphasizes communal living or has experienced financial instability, their primary expression of love might be providing security.

For example, a partner might show love by working long, grueling shifts at a job they hate, strictly to ensure the rent is paid and the pantry is full. If you grew up valuing "quality time," you might feel neglected because they are always working. To bridge this gap, you have to reframe how you view their exhaustion. They aren't choosing work over you; they are often viewing the labor itself as an act of profound self-sacrifice on your behalf. Notice if they manage administrative burdens for you, handle logistics, or protect you from daily stressors. That is often where their heart actually lives.

## Observe How Affection Is Shown to Family and Friends

Culture dictates the boundaries of public and private intimacy. In some cultures, physical touch like holding hands or kissing in public is strictly taboo, even within committed relationships. In others, physical closeness extends naturally to extended family, meaning your partner might be deeply affectionate with their siblings or parents, but reserve a more reserved, respectful distance with you in public spaces.

To figure out what brings them comfort, watch how they interact within their family unit. Do they show love by feeding people? Do they show respect by listening quietly while an elder speaks? Often, the way a person’s family exchanged care becomes the blueprint they unconsciously try to recreate in an adult partnership—even if they claim they want to do things differently.

## Have Direct, Curiosity-Driven Conversations About Family Traditions

You cannot guess your way through a cultural divide. At some point, you need to ask questions that go beyond "What is your love language?" because your partner might not even know the framework. 

Instead, ask specific questions about their childhood and family dynamics:
* "When you were growing up, how did you know your parents cared about you?"
* "What did a comforting Sunday look like in your house?"
* "When you are stressed, what is the one thing someone can do that immediately makes you feel safe?"

These prompts bypass relationship jargon and tap into lived memory. When my husband told me about how his grandfather used to quietly sit on the porch shelling peas next to his grandmother for hours without speaking, I finally understood why our quiet evenings on the couch—even while staring at separate phones—felt deeply intimate to him.

## Translate Your Own Love Back to Them

Once you start spotting the patterns, the real work begins: learning to translate your affection into a dialect they can actually feel. If you need verbal reassurance, tell them gently that hearing words of affection helps your specific nervous system relax, rather than accusing them of being cold. Conversely, try showing up for them in the ways their culture values—whether that means packing a lunch, learning to cook a dish from their home country, or respecting their need for quiet downtime after work.

Figuring this out takes time, patience, and a willingness to step outside your own cultural defaults. As you navigate these differences and look for practical ways to build a stronger daily rhythm with your partner, it helps to have reliable frameworks and expert guidance to keep both of you on the same page.
