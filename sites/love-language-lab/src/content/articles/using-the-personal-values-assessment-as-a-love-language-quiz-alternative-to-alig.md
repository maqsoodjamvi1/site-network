If you have spent any time looking for relationship advice online, you have likely taken a love language quiz. While knowing whether your partner prefers words of affirmation or acts of service can help you navigate daily friction, it rarely solves the big questions. It does not tell you whether you both want to buy a house in the suburbs, how you plan to handle aging parents, or whether having children is a hard line for either of you. 

When my partner and I found ourselves circling the same fundamental questions about our future without reaching any real clarity, the standard relationship quizzes stopped helping. We needed something that went deeper than daily affection styles. That is when I started using a personal values assessment instead. Here is how shifting our focus from affection to core values helped us align our long-term goals and make a confident decision about our future.

## Why Love Language Quizzes Fall Short for Long-Term Decisions

Love languages describe how we *give and receive* care within a relationship. They are behavioral tools designed to improve communication and emotional intimacy. However, they are entirely neutral when it comes to life direction. 

Two people can have matching love languages—say, both valuing quality time—and still break up because one person wants to travel the world indefinitely while the other wants to plant roots and buy a fixer-upper. A love language quiz measures your current relational habits, but a personal values assessment measures your foundational drivers. 

Your values are the internal compass points that dictate what you prioritize when resources—like time, money, and energy—are scarce. When you are deciding whether to commit to a long-term future, marriage, or major life relocations, you need to know if your compasses point in the same general direction.

## How to Take and Adapt a Personal Values Assessment

You do not need a clinical psychologist to run a values assessment. You can find free inventories online—such as the Barrett Values Centre framework or basic workplace values sorts—or you can build your own list using a standard dictionary of values (words like autonomy, security, creativity, community, stability, and adventure).

Here is the exact step-by-step exercise my partner and I used:

*   **Individual Sort:** Each of us independently reviewed a list of fifty common values and narrowed them down to our top ten. From those ten, we aggressively cut down to our absolute top five non-negotiables. 
*   **Definition Writing:** We didn't just write down the words; we wrote a two-sentence description of what that value meant in practice. For example, "financial security" meant having six months of living expenses saved at all times, not just earning a high salary.
*   **The Comparison:** We sat down with our lists side-by-side to look for overlaps, gaps, and potential flashpoints.

This exercise removed the emotion from abstract arguments. Instead of arguing about why one person was being "too rigid" or "too reckless," we could point to our core values—in my case, stability, and in his, autonomy—and figure out how to honor both.

## Translating Abstract Values Into Concrete Life Goals

The real magic of using a values assessment happens when you translate those abstract words into daily reality and long-term timelines. Values are useless if they remain buzzwords. 

Take the value of "family." That sounds great on paper until you realize one person defines family as living within twenty minutes of aging parents, while the other defines it as putting all your energy into the nuclear family you are building together across the country. 

To bridge this gap, take your top three values and map them against three major life categories: career, finances, and living situation. Ask yourselves specific, practical questions based on your results:
*   If *creativity* is in your top three, does our current budget allow for artistic hobbies, or are we entirely consumed by survival and stability?
*   If *community* is a core value, why are we looking at isolated rural properties instead of neighborhoods with active social calendars?

When we did this, we realized our values actually aligned well—we both valued growth and security—but our timeline was mismatched. One of us wanted to rush into major milestones to feel secure, while the other wanted to take measured risks. Seeing the values behind our urgency helped us compromise on a three-year roadmap instead of forcing a premature choice.

## Moving Forward With Clarity

A personal values assessment will not give you a neat, romantic score at the end like a pop-culture quiz, and it might occasionally spark an uncomfortable conversation. But that discomfort is precisely the point. By digging beneath daily habits and communication styles, you can uncover whether your foundational motivations point toward the same destination. 

As you weigh your next steps together, consider sitting down this weekend to map out your individual non-negotiables before making any major commitments.
