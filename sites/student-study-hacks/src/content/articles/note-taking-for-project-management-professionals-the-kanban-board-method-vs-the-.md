Balancing multiple client deliverables, cross-functional team updates, and shifting stakeholder priorities means my daily work life runs at a breakneck speed. Early in my career as a project manager, I tried to capture every detail of every meeting using a standard running list in a notebook. By Thursday afternoon, my notes were a chaotic mess of action items buried beneath random project ideas and budget figures. 

When I started pursuing formal project management certifications and advanced credentials, I realized my haphazard note-taking was actively hurting my productivity. To survive rigorous professional exams and fast-paced client sprints, I tested two completely different note-taking strategies: the Kanban Board Method and the Cornell Method. If you are trying to figure out which system fits your study habits or your day-to-day project work, here is how they stack up against each other based on my trial and error.

## The Cornell Method: Best for Deep Theory and Exams

Walter Pauk of Cornell University originally designed this system for college students, but it is remarkably effective for mastering dense project management frameworks like PMBOK guidelines, Agile methodologies, or risk management formulas. 

The physical layout requires dividing your paper into three distinct sections: a narrow left-hand column (about two and a half inches) for cues and keywords, a wide right-hand column (about six inches) for your main notes, and a two-inch block at the very bottom for a summary.

When I used the Cornell Method to study Earned Value Management (EVM) formulas, I put the raw definitions and step-by-step math problems in the wide right column. In the left cue column, I wrote trigger questions like "What does a CPI under 1.0 mean?" At the end of my study session, I summarized the entire concept in the bottom box in two sentences.

*   **Pros:** Forces active recall because you can cover the right side of the page and use the cue column to test yourself. It turns passive reading into a self-assessment tool.
*   **Cons:** Terrible for capturing non-linear brainstorming sessions or rapidly shifting meeting agendas where topics jump backward and forward.

## The Kanban Board Method: Best for Action-Oriented Workflows

If your brain works visually and you find yourself constantly tracking deliverables, the Kanban Board Method transforms a standard notebook into an operational task manager. Instead of writing top-to-bottom lines of text, you divide your page into columns—typically labeled "To Do," "In Progress," and "Done." 

During a chaotic kickoff meeting with a new client, I use this method by drawing three vertical columns across a landscape-oriented notebook page. As team members throw out requirements, constraints, and open questions, I write each distinct item on a small sticky note or directly into the corresponding column. 

If a scope requirement starts as an open question in "To Do," I physically draw an arrow or move the sticky note to "In Progress" once the project sponsor clarifies it. This visual limitation mirrors physical Kanban boards in software like Jira or Trello, but keeps it entirely analog.

*   **Pros:** Immediately highlights bottlenecks and stagnant tasks. You can see at a glance what is moving forward and what is stuck, making it ideal for tracking study modules you still need to review.
*   **Cons:** Fails completely when you need to retain heavy theoretical definitions, historical context, or detailed step-by-step calculation processes.

## Head-to-Head Comparison: Which One Should You Choose?

Choosing between these two methods comes down to the specific nature of what you are trying to accomplish. If your immediate goal is passing a certification exam that requires memorizing standard definitions, governance structures, and process groups, the Cornell Method wins hands down. Its structured layout builds a reliable study guide that you can review in minutes before an exam.

Conversely, if you are managing a live project, mapping out a complex work breakdown structure (WBS), or trying to organize a messy list of action items from a stakeholder alignment call, the Kanban method is far superior. It bridges the gap between raw notes and actionable project execution without requiring you to open a software application.

In my current routine, I actually use a hybrid approach. I rely on the Cornell Method when I am sitting down with a textbook to learn new methodologies, and I switch to the Kanban approach when I am facilitating live meetings or mapping out a sprint backlog. 

As you plan your next steps for tackling your current professional development goals, think about whether your main hurdle is memorizing complex theory or organizing shifting tasks, and choose the layout that targets your specific bottleneck.
